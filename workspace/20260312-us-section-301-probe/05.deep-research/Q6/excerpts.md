# Congressional Dynamics and the Future of U.S. Tariff Authority — Research Excerpts

## Question: Are any members of Congress introducing legislation to reclaim tariff authority in the wake of the Supreme Court ruling? What specific bills have been proposed, and what are their prospects?

### [Q6-001] The Trade Review Act of 2025 — Bipartisan Congressional Oversight Bill
**Source**: SRC-101 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly answers the question with the most significant bipartisan legislation to reclaim tariff authority.

> The Trade Review Act of 2025 (S. 1272 / H.R. 2665), modeled after the War Powers Resolution of 1973, would reestablish limits on the president's ability to impose unilateral tariffs. Cosponsors include Sens. Cantwell (D-WA), Grassley (R-IA), Moran (R-KS), Klobuchar (D-MN), Murkowski (R-AK), Warner (D-VA), McConnell (R-KY), Bennet (D-CO), Tillis (R-NC), Welch (D-VT), Young (R-IN), Coons (D-DE), Collins (R-ME), and Blumenthal (D-CT). Key provisions: President must notify Congress within 48 hours; within 60 days Congress must pass a joint resolution of approval or tariffs expire; Congress can end tariffs at any time via resolution of disapproval. House companion introduced by Reps. Bacon (R-NE), Hurd (R-CO), Gottheimer (D-NJ), Meeks (D-NY).

### [Q6-002] Bacon's Congressional Authority Rationale
**Source**: SRC-095 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows bipartisan cosponsor count and practical prospects.

> Bacon pointed to the Trade Review Act (H.R. 2665) with eight bipartisan cosponsors in the House, and Sen. Cantwell's companion bill (S. 1272) with 13 bipartisan cosponsors in the Senate.

### [Q6-003] Grassley's Long-Standing Push to Reassert Congressional Role
**Source**: SRC-094 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows the legislative effort predates the current crisis.

> Iowa Senator Chuck Grassley: "I'm one of the only sitting members of Congress who was in office during IEEPA's passage. Since then, I've made clear Congress needs to reassert its constitutional role over commerce, which is why I introduced prospective legislation that would give Congress a say when tariffs are levied in the future."

### [Q6-004] Pro-Tariff Republicans Push to Codify Trump Tariffs via Reconciliation
**Source**: SRC-094 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows the competing legislative impulse — to expand rather than reclaim tariff authority.

> Ohio Senator Bernie Moreno called the ruling "outrageous": "This betrayal must be reversed and Republicans must get to work immediately on a reconciliation bill to codify the tariffs." Tennessee Representative John Rose: "It is the high court's opinion that it is now up to Congress to protect U.S. economic interests. I'm ready to lead the effort to empower the president to continue his very effective work."

### [Q6-005] Sanctioning Russia Act as Backdoor Tariff Authority
**Source**: SRC-057 [existing]
**Relevance**: Identifies a separate bill that could inadvertently expand presidential tariff power.

> Post-SCOTUS ruling, most analysts are rightly shifting their attention to established trade measures. But a bipartisan bill awaiting a Senate vote could be even more consequential. The Sanctioning Russia Act, which enjoys near-unanimous support in the Senate, would quietly grant the White House sweeping authority to levy 500% tariffs — effectively an embargo — on any country importing Russian crude or refined product. Last year, roughly 40 countries imported oil from Russia, representing around 40% of U.S. imports.

### [Q6-006] Democrat Tariff Refund Legislation
**Source**: SRC-095 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents additional legislative proposals on the tariff front.

> A group of Senate Democrats, led by Ron Wyden of Oregon, released legislation that would force Customs and Border Patrol to issue tariff refunds to importers and small businesses. Rep. Steven Horsford, D-Nev., introduced similar legislation in the House (HR 7865, the American Consumer Tariff Rebate Act of 2026), though neither has a clear path to passage as long as Democrats are in the minority.

### [Q6-007] Johnson Acknowledges Dim Legislative Prospects
**Source**: SRC-095 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows the practical difficulties for any tariff legislation.

> House Speaker Mike Johnson, R-La., acknowledged the dim prospects: "It's going to be, I think, a challenge to find consensus on any path forward on the tariffs on the legislative side. That is why I think you see so much of the attention on the executive side."

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. The Trade Review Act of 2025 is the most significant bill, with strong bipartisan support (13 Senate cosponsors including McConnell). Competing proposals to codify Trump's tariffs via reconciliation face dim prospects given GOP divisions. The Sanctioning Russia Act is an underappreciated vector for expanding presidential tariff power. Prospects for passage of any tariff legislation remain uncertain due to the narrow GOP majority and internal divisions.

## Question: What is the political dynamic between Congressional Republicans and the Trump administration on trade? Is there any significant Congressional opposition to the Section 301 probe, or is the tariff consensus bipartisan?

### [Q6-008] Hidden GOP Opposition to Tariffs
**Source**: SRC-095 [new]
**Relevance**: Reveals the gap between private sentiment and public votes.

> "I was in the cloakroom and I heard people saying 'I don't like tariffs but I voted no,'" Bacon said. "I expect that if people were left to their own devices, without pressure, I think the number would have been five or six times higher. But obviously it's a big priority for the president, so people struggle with it."

### [Q6-009] Six House Republicans Break Ranks
**Source**: SRC-097 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents concrete defections and their consequences.

> Six House Republicans voted alongside Democrats to condemn Trump's tariffs on Canada. Senior House Democrats have vowed to bring up at least three more similar resolutions that will force GOP members to choose between their adherence to free trade principles and their MAGA base.

### [Q6-010] Trump Punishes Defectors
**Source**: SRC-095 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows the political cost of opposing Trump on trade.

> Trump withdrew his endorsement of Rep. Jeff Hurd, a Colorado Republican who voted to overturn Trump's Canada tariffs. Hurd faces right-wing primary opponent Hope Scheppelman, who Trump is now endorsing.

### [Q6-011] McConnell's Warning on Congressional Role
**Source**: SRC-099 [new]
**Relevance**: A senior Republican leader's view on trade authority.

> Sen. Mitch McConnell (R-KY) said "Congress's role in trade policy, as I have warned repeatedly, is not an inconvenience to avoid."

### [Q6-012] Private Loathing of Sweeping Tariffs
**Source**: SRC-099 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents scale of private opposition.

> A significant portion of conservatives in Congress privately loathe such sweeping tariff agendas and have advocated the administration to take a more targeted approach.

### [Q6-013] Slim Bipartisan Majorities Against Tariffs
**Source**: SRC-057 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents pre-ruling Congressional dynamics.

> House Speaker Mike Johnson tried to squelch tariff-related votes for much of 2025, but slim bipartisan majorities in both chambers have supported curtailing some of the tariffs. Democrats are likely to force more such votes, putting Republicans on the spot: Stay loyal to Trump and share the blame for the economy or align with public discontent?

### [Q6-014] Section 301 Has Bipartisan Pedigree
**Source**: SRC-059 [existing]
**Relevance**: Explains why Section 301 draws less opposition than IEEPA tariffs.

> Section 301 is a tried-and-true tool that has been around even before the WTO was established, and it has proven durable over the decades since. It has the advantage of providing impressive USTR authority to impose tariffs and other trade restrictions to address a full range of foreign trade barriers. A more predictable, defensible, and legally credible approach could have been to initiate multiple Section 301 investigations on a country-by-country and sector-by-sector basis.

### [Q6-015] Kiley and Hurd Frame Opposition as Constitutional Principle
**Source**: SRC-100 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how Republican dissenters frame their opposition.

> Rep. Kiley: "Congress ought to have a role in trade policy — in a broad sense." Rep. Hurd: "What do we say to the future president who declares a climate change emergency, and says that there is unlimited unilateral authority to implement tariffs? What is our principled response to that? We don't have one if we don't stand up here on something like this."

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. There is significant but mostly private Republican opposition to sweeping tariffs, with the Section 301 probe drawing less specific opposition because it follows established procedural channels and has bipartisan pedigree (Biden maintained Trump's first-term Section 301 tariffs). The political dynamic is characterized by a gap between private opposition and public votes, with Trump's endorsement withdrawal against Hurd showing the cost of defection. The consensus is not bipartisan in favor of tariffs — it is bipartisan opposition to the *mechanism* (IEEPA) while there is broad acceptance of the *tool* (Section 301).

## Question: What is the legal vulnerability of the Section 122 bridge tariff (10% global tariff requiring a "balance-of-payments emergency")? Are legal challenges already underway, and what are experts predicting?

### [Q6-016] 24-State Attorney General Lawsuit Filed March 5, 2026
**Source**: SRC-098 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly documents the primary legal challenge.

> A coalition of 24 states' Attorneys General and Governors filed a lawsuit on March 5, 2026, in the Court of International Trade challenging Section 122 tariffs on two grounds: (1) the required balance-of-payments deficit does not exist — the actual BOP deficit is $53 billion or 0.2% of GDP, "essentially a rounding error" when capital inflows are counted; (2) the statute requires nondiscriminatory application, yet the tariffs exempt certain countries and include 80+ pages of product exceptions.

### [Q6-017] Liberty Justice Center Lawsuit Filed March 9, 2026
**Source**: SRC-096 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the second major legal challenge, by the same firm that won at the Supreme Court.

> The Liberty Justice Center filed a new lawsuit in the U.S. Court of International Trade arguing that "economists across the political spectrum agree that the United States faces no such emergency today. Moreover, no previous president has ever invoked Section 122 to impose tariffs." The administration conflates trade deficit with balance-of-payments deficit. Jeffrey Schwab, the attorney who won V.O.S. Selections, Inc. v. Trump at the Supreme Court: "Section 122 authorizes temporary tariffs for certain economic conditions that do not currently exist."

### [Q6-018] Section 122 Designed for Obsolete Fixed-Rate Currency System
**Source**: SRC-098 [new]
**Relevance**: The core legal argument — Section 122 addresses a problem that ended 50 years ago.

> Section 122 was intended to address "fundamental international payments problems" related to the fixed rate exchange system. As Milton Friedman put it: "A system of floating exchange rates completely eliminates the balance of payments problem." In other words, Section 122 was intended to address an economic problem that not only does not exist today, but that has not existed for 50 years.

### [Q6-019] PIIE Expert Warning on Overreach
**Source**: SRC-058 [existing]
**Relevance**: Authoritative expert prediction on Section 122 and broader strategy vulnerability.

> Alan Wm. Wolff (PIIE): "The courts are unlikely to substitute their judgment as to whether this test is met during this relatively short period as a practical matter. It is highly unlikely that an injunction will be issued to stay the president's hand during the 150-day interim." However: "The risk for the Trump administration is that it again engages in overreach. Will the use of alternative statutory authorities, in a transparent sham to largely restore the tariff wall, be rejected by the Supreme Court?... combining these other authorities to recreate the 'reciprocal' tariffs will inescapably raise the larger question of whether the president and not the Congress has the tariff power... Any broad action may even be enjoined by the courts."

### [Q6-020] Baker McKenzie Analysis: Weak Statutory Basis
**Source**: SRC-048 [existing]
**Relevance**: Professional legal analysis from a global law firm.

> Questions are being raised around Section 122's statutory applicability, as it requires a "balance-of-payments emergency" not a trade deficit. Given the weak statutory basis, strict time limits and potential litigation risks, the move is likely to be a stopgap rather than a durable solution.

### [Q6-021] Tax Foundation: Section 122 Never Used Before
**Source**: SRC-102 [new]
**Relevance**: Comprehensive framework of tariff authorities and their limitations.

> Section 122 has never been used to impose tariffs. It requires a balance-of-payments deficit, is capped at 15%, and expires after 150 days. Section 122 at 10-15% could replace 56-73% of IEEPA tariff revenue over 150 days ($27-36 billion net).

### [Q6-022] Solicitor General's Own Admission Undermines Section 122
**Source**: SRC-096 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows the administration's legal position contradicts itself.

> Liberty Justice Center attorneys noted that Trump's solicitor general had already conceded this point when defending the initial reciprocal tariffs: "When the Supreme Court held that the President lacked authority under IEEPA to impose tariffs, he responded by doing the exact thing he told the federal courts he could not do: use Section 122 as a replacement to IEEPA to impose worldwide tariffs due to the United States trade deficit."

**Assessment**: This question is fully answered. Section 122 faces substantial legal vulnerability on multiple grounds: (1) the statute was designed for a fixed-rate currency system that ended in 1976; (2) the actual balance-of-payments deficit is negligible (0.2% of GDP); (3) the tariffs violate the nondiscriminatory treatment requirement; (4) the solicitor general's own arguments in the IEEPA case undermine the Section 122 claim. Two major lawsuits are already filed (24-state AG coalition on March 5; Liberty Justice Center on March 9). However, experts predict the litigation may not be resolved before the 150-day period expires in July 2026, making the practical impact uncertain.

## Question: How does the upcoming November 2026 midterm election affect the trade policy calculus? Could a shift in Congressional control alter the tariff trajectory?

### [Q6-023] Tariffs as Midterm Liability for Republicans
**Source**: SRC-097 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly addresses the midterm-tariff nexus.

> "It's very possible that the Supreme Court just threw Trump's economy a life preserver, and the president is refusing it and demanding an anchor," said Matthew Bartlett, Republican strategist. A 45% plurality said tariffs damage the economy. Roughly a quarter of Trump's own 2024 voters agree. Among non-MAGA Republicans, 32% said tariffs are damaging.

### [Q6-024] Section 122 Extension Vote Before Midterms
**Source**: SRC-057 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows how Section 122 expiration creates a politically dangerous vote.

> The Trump administration is eyeing Section 122 tariffs, which last only 150 days and require a vote from Congress to extend them (which Senate Democrats could filibuster). Some Republicans may balk if Democrats portray these tariffs as a regressive tax on constituents. As political scientist David Mayhew argued decades ago, lawmakers are rewarded (or blamed) for the positions they take, not the policy outcomes that follow.

### [Q6-025] Over 60% Disapproval Rate for Tariffs
**Source**: SRC-057 [existing]
**Relevance**: Polling data on tariff unpopularity heading into midterms.

> In one recent survey, over 60% of Americans disapproved of tariffs, including nearly a quarter of Republicans and three-quarters of pivotal, swing vote independents. With midterms approaching, that could put GOP lawmakers in a tough spot.

### [Q6-026] Farm State Vulnerability
**Source**: SRC-097 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows specific geographic impact on midterm races.

> In agriculture-heavy states, the tariffs have been felt acutely. The Trump administration approved a $12 billion bailout for farmers in December. American Farm Bureau President Zippy Duvall urged Trump to "avoid using any other available authorities to impose tariffs on agricultural inputs." Wisconsin-based GOP strategist Craig Peterson: "In this last year with all the tariffs and increased costs, it's going to take a little while for folks to forget about that. That's not real long, unfortunately, for the Republicans."

### [Q6-027] Democrats' "Scarlett T" Strategy
**Source**: SRC-097 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how Democrats plan to weaponize tariffs in midterm campaigns.

> Democratic strategist Jesse Ferguson: "Not only do voters hate these tariffs because they've raised costs, but now the Supreme Court has said they're illegal. Every Republican in 2026 has a Scarlett 'T' on their chest." DCCC spokesperson: the decision "makes crystal clear the need for Congress to exercise its constitutional authority over trade policy."

### [Q6-028] Schumer Vows to Block Extension
**Source**: SRC-099 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the Democratic strategy to make tariffs a July 2026 issue.

> With slim GOP majorities and some Republicans celebrating the ruling, Democrats almost certainly have the numbers to block any extension. Schumer vowed: "Democrats will not go along with furthering Trump's economic carnage."

### [Q6-029] "Breathing Room" for Republicans
**Source**: SRC-097 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows the political calculus of incumbents.

> "There are a lot of members of Congress excited to see that the tariffs are temporarily not in effect. It has been a pinch at home and it's been hard to message around," said one Republican strategist involved with House races. "It gives them some breathing room."

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. The November 2026 midterms are a major constraint on trade policy. The Section 122 tariffs expire in July 2026, forcing a Congressional vote just months before the election. Tariff unpopularity (60%+ disapproval) makes this vote politically toxic. Democrats plan to block extension and use tariffs as a campaign issue. A shift in Congressional control toward Democrats would likely result in reduced executive tariff authority rather than expanded tariffs, given Democratic opposition to the sweeping tariff regime.

## Question: What institutional reforms to U.S. trade authority are being discussed in legal and policy circles — e.g., requiring Congressional approval for tariffs above a threshold, sunset clauses, or mandatory economic impact assessments?

### [Q6-030] Trade Review Act: 60-Day Sunset and Mandatory Impact Assessment
**Source**: SRC-101 [new]
**Relevance**: The most concrete reform proposal with detailed provisions.

> The Trade Review Act of 2025 (modeled after the War Powers Resolution): requires presidential notification to Congress within 48 hours with explanation and impact analysis; 60-day sunset unless Congress passes joint resolution of approval; Congress can end tariffs at any time via resolution of disapproval; expedited procedures for Congressional consideration.

### [Q6-031] Tax Foundation: Clearer Statutory Definitions Needed
**Source**: SRC-102 [new]
**Relevance**: Identifies specific statutory ambiguities that need reform.

> "The president still has too much power to unilaterally impose tariffs because of Congressional legislation granting this authority, and the statutes themselves could benefit from clearer definitions on exactly what constitutes a 'national security threat' or a 'serious injury' to industry. Altogether, Congress should wrest back its trade authority from the president and resume its role in levying taxes such as tariffs."

### [Q6-032] Brookings: Tariffs as Fiscal Policy Require Congressional Authorization
**Source**: SRC-057 [existing]
**Relevance**: Frames the institutional design argument.

> "When tariffs are modest, questions about delegation may seem technical. When they rival major tax legislation in revenue impact, they are fiscal policy by another name. The Court's decision does not end the tariff debate. But it does reinforce a foundational principle: if the United States is going to raise trillions of dollars through import taxes, that choice must be made clearly and deliberately by Congress."

### [Q6-033] Brookings: Institutional Design Has Lagged Policy Ambition
**Source**: SRC-043 [existing]
**Relevance**: Describes the systemic reform challenge.

> "Trade authorities enacted in the 1960s and 1970s for episodic use now anchor not only trade policy, but elements of fiscal and industrial policy. That evolution occurred through the cumulative use of existing authorities to pursue objectives that might otherwise have required new legislation. It did not follow from a deliberate reassessment of how trade, fiscal, and industrial policy should interact in an era of technological change and geopolitical competition."

### [Q6-034] Cato Institute: Bipartisan Reform Proposals
**Source**: SRC-039 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents the pre-existing reform agenda.

> "Trump's actions have prompted lawmakers in both the House and Senate to consider bipartisan legislation to curtail presidential authority to impose import tariffs without significant congressional input or oversight."

### [Q6-035] Hurd's Precedent Argument for Reform
**Source**: SRC-100 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how reform advocates frame the cross-partisan case.

> Rep. Jeff Hurd: "What do we say to the future president who declares a climate change emergency, and says that there is unlimited unilateral authority to implement tariffs on any and all energy-related products? What is our principled response to that? We don't have one if we don't stand up here."

### [Q6-036] Rand Paul's Constitutional Principle
**Source**: SRC-094 [new]
**Relevance**: Libertarian Republican case for structural reform.

> "Tariffs are taxes and the power to declare them belongs to the Congress. This goes beyond the case. No future administration, including a socialist one, can use 'emergency' powers to get around Congress and tax by decree."

### [Q6-037] Brookings: Coordinated Action with Allies as Alternative Framework
**Source**: SRC-043 [existing]
**Relevance**: Proposes an alternative institutional framework beyond just Congressional oversight.

> "A more stable approach would require an articulation of how economic performance, strategic competition, domestic politics, and foreign policy fit together in modern U.S. trade strategy — and legal authorities aligned with key principles. Until such a framework emerges, U.S. trade policy will continue to turn on executive discretion exercised through statutes never intended for systemic redesign."

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. The Trade Review Act of 2025 is the most concrete institutional reform proposal, incorporating sunset clauses (60 days), mandatory impact assessments, and Congressional approval requirements. The broader discussion in policy circles centers on three themes: (1) clearer statutory definitions for when executive tariff authority can be invoked; (2) Congressional oversight mechanisms modeled on the War Powers Resolution; (3) systemic rethinking of how trade, fiscal, and industrial policy authorities should be structured for the modern economy. The cross-partisan appeal (future Democratic presidents could use the same tools) strengthens reform prospects, but passage remains uncertain.
