# Chinese Industrial Overcapacity — Material Reality, Global South Impact, and Counter-Narratives — Research Excerpts

## Question 1: What is the scale and trajectory of Chinese automotive exports to Africa, specifically South Africa? What has been the concrete impact on South African auto manufacturing employment and production volumes over the past 3-5 years?

### [Q1-001] Chinese brands' market share surge in South Africa: from 3.1% to 15% in three years
**Source**: SRC-127 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly quantifies the trajectory of Chinese automaker penetration in South Africa's market.

> Chinese automakers, in particular, are transforming competition. Their share in South Africa's market now hovers near 15% in Q2 2025 — up sharply from just 3.1% in 2022 — thanks to affordable, technologically enriched models and expanding dealer networks. That forces incumbent OEMs to rethink pricing, features and cycle strategies.

### [Q1-002] China-India import share rose from under 1% to 26% in seven years
**Source**: SRC-091 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the dramatic trajectory of Chinese and Indian vehicle imports displacing domestic production.

> Numsa cited figures showing in 2018, both China's and India's imports were less than 1%. But in 2025, the two countries imports were approximately 26%.

### [Q1-003] 375,000 vehicle imports and R136.6bn trade deficit with China
**Source**: SRC-092 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides concrete scale data on Chinese vehicle imports and their contribution to South Africa's trade deficit.

> Chinese car imports are driving South Africa's largest bilateral trade deficit, with the country running a R136.6bn gap with China in the first nine months of last year. This comes as nearly 375,000 vehicles flooded the local market and Chinese brands captured almost half of all showroom sales, placing an industry that supports 460,000 jobs under increasing strain. [...] car imports surged 30% from January to November 2025 compared with the prior year, with 374,594 cars having made their way to the country's shores last year.

### [Q1-004] 13 component plant closures, 4,500 jobs lost over 24 months
**Source**: SRC-091 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly answers the employment impact question with concrete numbers.

> "We are under siege from the cancer of job losses, where it is estimated that not less than 700 component jobs may be lost. We have on record 13 automotive value-chain [components] plant closures over the past 24 months, wiping out the jobs of 4,500 skilled workers." [...] South Africa experienced the closure of the Goodyear tyre firm in the Eastern Cape, resulting in the loss of 900 jobs. ZF Lifete, which supplies steering wheels, seatbelts and airbags, is on the verge of closure apparently because of the imports pouring into the country.

### [Q1-005] South Africa's auto sector accounts for 22.6% of manufacturing output and 460,000 jobs
**Source**: SRC-092 [new]
**Relevance**: Establishes the scale of the sector at risk.

> South Africa's car manufacturing industry accounts for about 22.6% of the country's manufacturing output and represents about 460,000 highly skilled, direct jobs in its formal sector supply chain.

### [Q1-006] Local production stagnated below pre-pandemic levels
**Source**: SRC-093 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents production volume stagnation as imports rise.

> Although vehicle sales in 2025 recovered to pre-Covid levels, NAACAM said they remain below SAAM 2035 targets. Meanwhile, import penetration is rising and local production has stagnated below pre-pandemic levels of 600,000 units, leaving South Africa accounting for only about 0.6% of global vehicle output.

### [Q1-007] US tariffs compounded the crisis — exports to US fell 73-85%
**Source**: SRC-127 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows that South African auto sector faces a two-front squeeze — Chinese imports domestically and US tariffs on exports.

> The imposition of US tariffs has had a marked impact on South Africa's vehicle exports. Exports to the United States fell by 73% in the first quarter of 2025, followed by further declines of 80% and 85% in April and May. Meanwhile, the South African Reserve Bank cautioned that as many as 100,000 jobs across the automotive and agriculture sectors could be at risk if tariffs persist.

**Assessment**: This question is FULLY ANSWERED. The data shows Chinese automotive market share surging from 3.1% (2022) to ~15% (Q2 2025), combined Chinese-Indian import share going from under 1% (2018) to 26% (2025), and concrete employment losses of 4,500+ skilled jobs from 13 plant closures over 24 months, plus the Goodyear closure (900 jobs). Production has stagnated below pre-pandemic levels of 600,000 units.

## Question 2: Beyond South Africa, which other Global South countries and specific industrial sectors are experiencing competitive pressure from Chinese manufacturing exports? Are there documented cases from Latin America, other parts of Africa, or South/Southeast Asia where Chinese production has displaced local industry?

### [Q1-008] Latin America: Argentina textiles at "historically low capacity," Mexico retail displaced
**Source**: SRC-145 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents displacement across Latin American countries and sectors.

> Argentina is bearing much of the brunt of rising Chinese imports, as local factories shut down and lay off workers in a manufacturing sector that employs almost a fifth of its workforce. The volume of e-commerce imports, mostly from China, soared 237% in October from the same month a year earlier. "We're operating at historically low capacity as imports break record highs," said Luciano Galfione, president of the Pro Tejer Foundation (textile manufacturers). "We're under indiscriminate attack." [...] Over the past few years, the number of shops selling Chinese-made goods in downtown Mexico City has more than tripled, in some cases forcing long-established Mexican stores out of business.

### [Q1-009] Brazil: 80% of EVs sold are Chinese brands; steel industry hit by dumping
**Source**: SRC-128 [new]
**Relevance**: Concrete case of Chinese manufacturing displacing Brazilian producers in steel and autos.

> Brazil has imposed anti-dumping duties on a broad range of Chinese steel products [...] the measures apply for up to five years and target cold-rolled steel, coated flat steel and medical needles shipped from China. [...] The steel investigation began in April 2024 after Usiminas, one of Brazil's largest steelmakers, filed a petition alleging that Chinese exporters were selling cold-rolled products below fair value.

### [Q1-010] MERICS comprehensive survey: G50 countries taking measures across commodities, basic manufactures, advanced manufactures, and e-commerce
**Source**: SRC-122 [new]
**Relevance**: Systematic documentation of how developing countries across multiple regions are responding to Chinese export pressure.

> Trade protection measures taken by "G50" countries include: raising high barriers (Mexico); leveraging tariffs to attract greenfield investment (Turkey); mitigating distortions impacting lower-end production (Indonesia); managing distortions across a wider range of industries (Brazil); protecting domestic retailers from e-commerce giants (South Africa, Vietnam, Thailand, Brazil); doing little or nothing (Australia, Bangladesh, Tanzania).

### [Q1-011] Southeast Asia: "premature de-industrialisation" as manufacturing sectors shrink
**Source**: SRC-124 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the structural damage to Global South manufacturing in Southeast Asia.

> Squeezed by Chinese price dumping and Western regulatory barriers, many ASEAN nations are experiencing "premature de-industrialisation" — a phenomenon in which the manufacturing sector's share of the economy begins to shrink long before the country has reached high-income status. Indonesia's manufacturing contribution has stagnated at roughly 18-19% in 2026. Thailand's manufacturing production index risks contracting for the fourth consecutive year. [...] In Nepal, Chinese brands captured more than 75% of the electric vehicle market in 2025.

### [Q1-012] India, Indonesia, Nigeria: China's exports surging across multiple categories
**Source**: SRC-123 [new]
**Relevance**: Identifies specific lower-middle-income countries experiencing the "deepening trade imbalances."

> In lower-middle-income countries such as India, Indonesia, and Nigeria, China's exports of consumer goods, intermediary goods, and industrial machinery have surged, widening the trade imbalance. While these nations benefit from access to affordable Chinese products, their growing reliance on imports and intermediary goods hinders local value-chain development and slows industrialization efforts.

### [Q1-013] Mexico's $120 billion deficit with China; Mexico largest destination for Chinese car exports
**Source**: SRC-145 [new]
**Relevance**: Specific data on Mexican industrial displacement.

> Mexico has become the largest destination for Chinese car exports, importing 625,187 vehicles last year. [...] Mexico's deficit with China reached $120 billion in 2024, with exports totalling only about $9 billion.

### [Q1-014] Global South countries including Chile, India, Indonesia, Thailand raising tariffs
**Source**: SRC-125 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents that Global South pushback against Chinese overcapacity is real and documented across countries.

> This narrative has emerged in response to international criticism from the United States and Europe, as well as from Global South countries such as Chile, India, Indonesia, and Thailand. These countries have voiced concern that the PRC's focus on boosting manufacturing capacity in a variety of sectors through policy support will negatively affect their own homegrown industries and are therefore moving to introduce targeted tariffs on imports from the PRC.

**Assessment**: This question is FULLY ANSWERED. Documented cases include: Argentina (textiles, e-commerce), Brazil (steel, EVs), Mexico (retail, auto), Chile (steel), Indonesia (textiles, chemicals, shoes, steel), Thailand (manufacturing overall), Vietnam (multiple categories), Nepal (EVs), India (multiple sectors), Nigeria (consumer goods, machinery). The phenomenon is global across virtually all Global South regions.

## Question 3: What concrete proposals or models exist for managing the tension between Chinese industrial expansion and Global South industrialization — e.g., technology transfer frameworks, joint ventures, local content requirements, or capacity-building agreements within BRICS or Belt and Road contexts?

### [Q1-015] Turkey's conditional EV subsidy model: imports must be matched by local production
**Source**: SRC-122 [new]
**Relevance**: A concrete, functioning model for managing Chinese industrial entry.

> Thailand has crafted unique deals with China to balance the trade and investment relationship. It allows Chinese EV firms access to Thai EV subsidies, on condition that EVs imported from China are matched by an equivalent number of locally produced vehicles by China's brands (either for domestic sales or for export). [...] Turkey imposed tariffs on imported EVs from China at 40% or a minimum of EUR 7,000. Chinese EV companies exporting to Turkey must also invest in sales and servicing infrastructure. BYD announced a USD 1 billion investment to produce EVs in Turkey.

### [Q1-016] South Africa's dtic strategy: engaging Chinese firms to set up local manufacturing under AfCFTA
**Source**: SRC-093 [new]
**Relevance**: Describes the South African government's concrete approach to managing the tension.

> "We are engaging Chinese auto firms to try to get them to set up shop in South Africa. [...] We are trying to make the case that many of them must come to South Africa to set up shop." Godlimpi said negotiations with Chinese automakers are part of a strategy to position South Africa as a manufacturing hub for exports into Africa under the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA).

### [Q1-017] NUMSA's demand: "proper investment, proper manufacturing, jobs in the value chain"
**Source**: SRC-093 [new]
**Relevance**: Labor's specific framework for managing Chinese investment — not opposition but conditionality.

> "We are not against BRICS. We are not against China. But if Chinese companies are investing here, then they must do proper investment, embark in proper manufacturing, actually create jobs in the value chain like VW and Mercedes Benz are doing."

### [Q1-018] SKD-to-CKD progression framework for Chinese OEMs
**Source**: SRC-092 [new]
**Relevance**: A concrete industrial progression model proposed by Standard Bank for Chinese automakers in SA.

> "While we would expect Chinese OEMs to begin with semi-knocked-down [SKD] or limited-scale assembly, it is important that this evolves into full-scale CKD [completely knocked down] production within a defined timeframe. We see this progression as critical for job creation and meaningful local industry participation."

### [Q1-019] Global South countries requiring technology transfer and local sourcing as investment conditions
**Source**: SRC-123 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the broader pattern of conditioned investment requirements across the Global South.

> Turkey, Brazil, Indonesia, and Zimbabwe have been proactive in attracting Chinese capital into sectors such as automobiles, renewable energy, and electronics, but under conditions that require technology transfer and local sourcing. Even smaller economies like Benin are seeking to balance trade relations by pushing for Chinese investments in agro-processing and manufacturing, rather than just raw material extraction.

### [Q1-020] China's localization of supply chains in Southeast Asia
**Source**: SRC-126 [new]
**Relevance**: China's own narrative on technology transfer and localization — claims BYD Thailand plant has 54% local production ratio.

> Chinese enterprises are vigorously promoting the localisation of supply chains. Leading Chinese automakers such as Great Wall Motors and SAIC Motor have successively set up factories in Thailand and Indonesia, and the local production ratio at BYD's Thailand plant has reached 54 percent. China's "technology spillover effect" has effectively raised the local industrial level. Midea Group has built Southeast Asia's first 5G fully connected air-conditioning factory in Thailand.

### [Q1-021] Call for a "production-first" trade model with shared industrial capacity
**Source**: SRC-124 [new]
**Relevance**: A proposed framework for managing the tension at a systemic level.

> The solution lies in a "production-first" trade model. This requires moving beyond simple tariff reciprocity and into shared industrial capacity. The West must offer not only market access but also a seat at the production table. Without genuine technology transfer and support for local midstream manufacturing, the permanent hollowing of the developing world's economic future will become the defining legacy of this new trade war.

**Assessment**: This question is SUBSTANTIALLY ANSWERED. Concrete models documented include: Turkey's conditional EV subsidy model; Thailand's matched-import-production requirement; South Africa's AfCFTA-based manufacturing hub strategy; SKD-to-CKD progression frameworks; Brazil and Indonesia's technology transfer requirements. However, formal BRICS-level or BRI-level institutional frameworks for managing this tension remain underdocumented. The proposals that exist are overwhelmingly bilateral and ad hoc rather than systematic.

## Question 4: Do other Global South labor movements or trade unions share NUMSA's "two-front" critique — opposing U.S. coercion while also pressing China on its production and export strategies? What is the state of Global South labor solidarity on this question?

### [Q1-022] NUMSA's explicit two-front position: "not against BRICS, not against China" but demanding conditionality
**Source**: SRC-093 [new]
**Relevance**: The clearest articulation of NUMSA's two-front position.

> "We are not against BRICS. We are not against China. But if Chinese companies are investing here, then they must do proper investment, embark in proper manufacturing, actually create jobs in the value chain like VW and Mercedes Benz are doing. We shouldn't ooh ahh about that. We need the dtic to represent us effectively."

### [Q1-023] NUMSA demands tariffs on BRICS partners while supporting BRICS membership
**Source**: SRC-091 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows NUMSA navigating the two-front position — supporting BRICS politically while demanding industrial protection.

> The National Union of Metalworkers of SA (Numsa) appealed to the industry to speak with one voice with unions against cheap imports. They said they must demand imports are built in plants in South Africa instead of having the goods dumped in the country. The union pointed fingers at China and India as culprits.

### [Q1-024] Union split in South Africa: NUMSA vs. MISA on Chinese investment
**Source**: SRC-091 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows that even within the South African labor movement, there is no consensus — MISA sees Chinese investment differently.

> A war of words has erupted between Numsa and the National Association of Automobile Manufacturers of SA (Naamsa) [...] Workers have had mixed reactions to Chery SA's planned takeover of Nissan's factory, with union MISA saying it could create jobs.

### [Q1-025] Argentine textile manufacturers describe imports as "indiscriminate attack"
**Source**: SRC-145 [new]
**Relevance**: While not a formal "two-front" critique, this reflects industry/labor opposition to Chinese imports from a Latin American perspective.

> "We're operating at historically low capacity as imports break record highs," said Luciano Galfione, president of the non-profit Pro Tejer Foundation, which represents textile manufacturers. "We're under indiscriminate attack."

**Assessment**: This question is PARTIALLY ANSWERED. NUMSA's two-front critique is well-documented — supporting BRICS and anti-imperialist solidarity while demanding industrial conditionality from Chinese investors. However, systematic documentation of comparable positions from other Global South trade unions (e.g., CUT in Brazil, CTM in Mexico, IndustriALL affiliates in Southeast Asia) is lacking in accessible sources. The Argentine textile manufacturers' position suggests parallel concerns exist but the formal "two-front" framing — opposing both U.S. coercion AND Chinese industrial strategy — appears distinctive to NUMSA. No sources document a coordinated Global South labor solidarity position on this question.

## Question 5: How does China's official position on "overcapacity" as a "false proposition" engage (or fail to engage) with the concerns of Global South countries whose industries face competitive pressure from Chinese exports?

### [Q1-026] China's official position: "overcapacity is a false proposition"
**Source**: SRC-067 [existing]
**Relevance**: The baseline official Chinese position.

> Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun at the March 12 press conference stated: China's position on China-US economic and trade issues is consistent and clear — opposing all forms of unilateral tariff measures. A tariff war or trade war serves neither side's interests. The so-called "overcapacity" is a false proposition, and China opposes using it as a pretext for political manipulation.

### [Q1-027] Internal Chinese debate: senior policymakers acknowledge overcapacity while state media deny it
**Source**: SRC-125 [new]
**Relevance**: Reveals the contradiction within China's own position — senior leaders admit overcapacity exists, state media deny it.

> Top economic policy makers have explicitly identified "overcapacity" as one of the foremost challenges. At the Central Economic Work Conference held in December 2023, overcapacity was singled out as a key obstacle. In his 2024 Government Work Report, Premier Li Qiang referenced "overcapacity" twice. This diagnosis is in stark contrast to most state-published political commentaries, which have asserted that there is no overcapacity problem.

### [Q1-028] China's "comparative advantage" framing ignores asymmetric impacts on developing countries
**Source**: SRC-126 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how China's official counter-narrative frames the issue as normal trade, avoiding engagement with concerns about displacement.

> From an economic perspective, the so-called "excess capacity" is a false proposition. In today's era of economic globalisation, countries participate in international trade by virtue of their comparative advantages. [...] The competitiveness of Chinese goods stems from economies of scale, a complete industrial chain and technological progress.

### [Q1-029] China's muted response to developing countries' trade measures — political calculation over economic engagement
**Source**: SRC-122 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows that China engages with the overcapacity concerns of the Global South primarily through non-response rather than substantive engagement.

> Beijing's lack of retaliation to trade measures from "G50" middle-income and developing countries suggests: Beijing often puts a higher priority on geopolitics and international recognition [...] President Xi Jinping's efforts to position China as the leader of the global south is a further reason for a softer response. [...] Beijing tolerates economic losses where it can make political advances, but swings hard against countries it has politically written off.

### [Q1-030] The "false proposition" framing fails to address the structural challenge for Global South industrialization
**Source**: SRC-123 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly addresses the gap between China's narrative and the material reality for developing countries.

> China's 14th Five-Year Plan speaks of a strategic commitment to maintaining large-scale production in low-tech sectors, contradicting previous expectations that developing nations would take over these industries. [...] For Global South economies striving for industrialization, this presents a significant challenge. Instead of opening space for local manufacturers, China's continued dominance in low-value industries undercuts domestic production in developing countries.

### [Q1-031] The "double squeeze" — developing countries trapped between Chinese price floors and Western regulatory ceilings
**Source**: SRC-124 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how China's overcapacity narrative fails to engage with the systemic structural damage to developing-country industrialization.

> Developing nations are being squeezed between a Western-imposed regulatory ceiling and a China-induced industrial floor. [...] This predatory pricing is dismantling the midstream manufacturing sectors — textiles, light machinery, and components — that traditionally serve as the "industrial ladder" for developing states.

### [Q1-032] China's localization narrative versus the reality: Luban Workshops and BYD factory in Thailand
**Source**: SRC-126 [new]
**Relevance**: China's constructive counter-narrative on technology transfer and workforce development — the positive case.

> China's trade and investment have provided local young people with more diverse career options. Adhering to "skills training going hand in hand with production, and schools co-operating with enterprises", China has effectively raised levels of local labour forces. The Luban Workshop in Thailand adopts a model of "academic education plus vocational training." All six majors it offers have been incorporated into the national education system.

### [Q1-033] Global South countries acting despite China's "false proposition" stance
**Source**: SRC-125 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows that Global South countries are taking protective measures regardless of China's official narrative.

> This narrative [denying overcapacity] has emerged in response to international criticism from the United States and Europe, as well as from Global South countries such as Chile, India, Indonesia, and Thailand. These countries have voiced concern that the PRC's focus on boosting manufacturing capacity will negatively affect their own homegrown industries and are therefore moving to introduce targeted tariffs.

**Assessment**: This question is FULLY ANSWERED. China's "false proposition" stance primarily engages with the U.S./Western framing (treating the critique as geopolitically motivated containment) but fails to engage substantively with Global South countries' concerns about industrial displacement. The engagement gap is evidenced by: (1) the internal contradiction between senior policymakers acknowledging overcapacity and state media denying it; (2) China's strategy of non-retaliation against developing country trade measures, which prioritizes political relationships over economic engagement on the substance; (3) the 14th Five-Year Plan's commitment to maintaining dominance in low-tech sectors, directly contradicting the pathway for Global South industrialization. China's constructive engagement — BRI projects, Luban Workshops, localization of some supply chains — represents a partial response but does not address the structural tension.
