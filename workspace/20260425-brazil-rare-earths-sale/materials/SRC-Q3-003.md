Brazil invests in research to master the production cycle of rare-earth minerals and supermagnets
Frances Jones and Yuri Vasconcelos
Issue #356, October 2025, Pesquisa FAPESP

A three-story, glass-fronted building in Lagoa Santa, a town 35 kilometers from Minas Gerais state capital Belo Horizonte, houses a fabrication and laboratory facility that may help Brazil to master the full cycle of permanent rare earth magnet production. Known as the Innovation & Technology Center of the National Service for Industrial Training's Institute of Rare Earth Magnets (CIT SENAI ITR), the unit commenced operations in 2024 at two sites -- one with laboratories and a small-scale pilot plant for the research and development of technologies associated to rare earth metal alloys and super magnets, and the other with a semi-industrial production line for the manufacture of sizeable batches of super magnets. Formerly known as LabFabITR, the laboratory-factory was set up eight years ago by the Minas Gerais Development Agency (CODEMGE), a unit of the MG state government. The Minas Gerais State Federation of Industries acquired the facility in 2023, and CIT SENAI then assumed responsibility for managing the Rare Earth Institute (ITR).

"On a pilot scale, we have already mastered the technological production cycle for permanent magnets made from neodymium [Nd], iron [Fe], and boron [B], the most common metal alloy used in the manufacture of these materials. On an industrial level we have mastered between 60% and 70% of the process," says chemist and materials engineering master Andre Pimenta de Faria, who runs the CIT SENAI ITR. "We need to conclude the commissioning [real-time operational testing] of some equipment items, all imported from China. We are looking to achieve this by January." The primary raw material used to make the magnets -- small metallic neodymium bars -- is imported from China. Although Brazil has vast rare earth mineral reserves, the country's production of oxides or pure metals from these chemical elements is still in the very early stages, and only one mining company, with overseas capital and technology, commercializes rare earth concentrate. Brazil has no commercial operation of the other stages in the chain, notably the separation of rare earths.

The aim of the laboratory-factory is to provide a link between research and industrial application; its output will be used to validate prototypes and processes in a semi-industrial manner. The maximum output capacity of the unit is 100 tonnes per year of permanent magnets, but as it is also a research center, this volume will not likely be achieved. "It is not our intention to commercially fabricate these magnets -- as a research institute we cannot do that," explains Faria. "We are seeking to master the productive chain of these components and then transfer technology to companies interested in producing the magnets in Brazil."

The CIT SENAI ITR is a part of the project MagBras: From Mine to Magnet, approved after a call under the Brazilian federal government's Green Mobility and Innovation program. Launched in July 2025, the initiative brings together 38 companies, start-ups, innovation centers, universities, research institutions, and support foundations. With a budget of R$73 million, of which R$60 million is provided by the federal government and the remainder cofunded by the participating entities, the program is aimed at consolidating the Brazilian productive chain for permanent magnets from rare earth mining to final product fabrication.

"Here in Brazil we have the world's second biggest reserve of rare earths, second only to the Chinese, and all the right conditions to achieve an industrial-scale production cycle of these minerals and permanent magnets," says mechanical engineer Luis Gonzaga Trabasso, coordinator of MagBras and chief researcher at the SENAI Institute for Innovation in Manufacturing and Laser Processing Systems, in Joinville, Santa Catarina State. "MagBras is a technical alliance forged with this aim, and we are looking to achieve this objective by 2030."

The separation bottleneck
Despite the name rare earths, these elements are relatively abundant in nature. The issue is that they are found in very low concentrations, normally mixed, with considerable chemical similarities between them, meaning that they require advanced extraction and refinement technologies, primarily during the chemical separation stage.

At the CETEM, a public research institution linked to the Brazilian Ministry of Science, Technology, and Innovation, the most significant progress has been made in the chemical separation stage. Chemical engineer Ysrael Marrero Vera says that the center has successfully separated praseodymium (Pr) and neodymium. "We separate these materials using what we call synthetic solutions, and not with real chemical rare earth mineral concentrate, because for the time being we don't produce that," he explains. The process was registered with the National Institute of Industrial Property (INPI), and the patent granted in 2022.

The MagBras project will be an opportunity to work with the real material, since there are twelve mining companies involved in the program, focused on producing rare earth concentrate from deposits found in Brazil. Vera explains that to chemically separate rare earths, a minimum of 20 kilograms of chemical rare earth concentrate is required, and very few companies in Brazil are currently capable of providing this quantity.

In the magnet fabrication stage, mechanical engineer Paulo Wendhausen, coordinator of the Magnetic Materials Group (MAGMA) at UFSC, says the biggest challenge comes in obtaining high magnetic property values. On a laboratory scale, the UFSC group was able to produce magnets with a grade of 42 megagauss-oersteds (MGOe) -- to achieve commercial scale, this value must be 55 MGOe. "We conduct our tests on smaller devices of lower power, such as small motors. The material we develop is not suitable for application in bigger machines such as wind turbines," emphasizes Wendhausen.
