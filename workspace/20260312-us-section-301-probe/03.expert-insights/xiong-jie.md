## Raw Input
全球南方学术论坛熊节指出：中国应当有能力用稀土作为杠杆，与美国谈判；但其他全球南方国家面临美国的压力将很难对抗。美国在衰落阶段不断试图将自身危机外化，这种情况很可能成为未来一个时期的新常态，对于全球南方来说是一个艰难的挑战。

Translation for context: Global South Academic Forum scholar Xiong Jie pointed out: China should have the ability to use rare earths as leverage to negotiate with the US; but other Global South countries will find it very difficult to resist American pressure. The US is constantly trying to externalize its own crises during its decline phase, and this situation is likely to become the new normal for the foreseeable future, posing a difficult challenge for the Global South.

## Core Points
1. China possesses meaningful leverage in its negotiations with the US through its control of rare earth supply chains, and should be able to use this leverage effectively in trade negotiations.
2. Other Global South countries lack comparable leverage and will find it very difficult to resist American coercive pressure — there is a sharp asymmetry between China's negotiating position and that of the rest of the Global South.
3. The US is in a phase of decline and is responding by attempting to externalize its own internal crises onto other countries — this is the structural driver behind actions like the Section 301 probe.
4. This pattern of crisis externalization is not a temporary aberration but is likely to become the new normal for a sustained period, making it a persistent and recurring challenge rather than a one-off event.
5. The Global South as a whole faces a difficult and prolonged challenge in navigating this environment of US-driven coercive pressure.

## Relevance to Identified Contradictions
- **Principal contradiction**: Xiong Jie's analysis directly reinforces the principal contradiction identified in the initial summary — the declining economic hegemony of the United States and its attempt to use coercive trade instruments to arrest the structural shift of productive capacity. His framing of the US as being "in a phase of decline" and "externalizing its own crises" provides a concise theoretical characterization of the same dynamic. His distinction between China (which has leverage) and other Global South countries (which do not) adds a critical differentiation within the broader category of countries facing US pressure.

- **Other connections**:
  - **Alliance management versus coercive leverage** (Secondary Contradiction 2): Xiong Jie's point about the asymmetry between China and other Global South countries maps onto this contradiction. The Section 301 probe targets allies and non-allies alike, but Xiong Jie highlights that the impact is sharply uneven — countries without strategic resources like rare earths are far more exposed.
  - **The squeeze on middle-power economies** (Commentary Direction 3): His observation that other Global South countries "will find it very difficult to resist" directly supports the analysis of countries caught between the US and China, unable to effectively push back against coercive pressure.
  - **"New normal" framing**: Xiong Jie's characterization of US crisis externalization as a likely "new normal" extends the initial summary's analysis by suggesting that the Section 301 probe is not an isolated event but part of a structural pattern that will recur and intensify. This has implications for how the commentary frames the probe — not as a specific policy dispute to be resolved, but as a symptom of a long-term systemic condition.

## Research Direction Suggestions
- **Asymmetry within the Global South**: Xiong Jie draws a sharp distinction between China's position (possessing rare earth leverage) and that of other Global South countries (lacking comparable leverage). This suggests a research direction examining what specific forms of leverage, if any, different Global South countries possess — resource control, market access, supply chain positioning — and how these shape their ability to resist US coercive pressure. The initial research touches on Southeast Asian vulnerability but does not systematically analyze the leverage asymmetry across the full range of targeted economies.

- **Rare earth supply chains as negotiating leverage**: The expert specifically identifies rare earths as China's key bargaining chip. This points toward deeper investigation of: the current state of rare earth supply chain dependencies between the US and China; the effectiveness of China's recent rare earth export controls; whether other critical mineral dependencies (antimony, gallium, germanium) compound this leverage; and how rare earth leverage interacts with the Paris negotiations and the upcoming Trump-Xi summit. The initial summary mentions China's critical minerals control in Question 4 but does not develop this as a central analytical thread.

- **US crisis externalization as a structural pattern**: Xiong Jie frames US behavior not as a policy choice but as a structural feature of hegemonic decline — the US "constantly trying to externalize its own crises." This suggests a research direction examining the historical pattern of crisis externalization by declining hegemonic powers, and what forms this externalization takes beyond tariffs (e.g., dollar weaponization, sanctions, security pressure). This would deepen the initial summary's historical comparisons (Smoot-Hawley, 1980s Japan) by placing them within a broader theoretical framework of hegemonic decline dynamics.

- **Global South collective response mechanisms**: The expert's characterization of the situation as a "difficult challenge" for the Global South implies a question about whether collective responses are possible. This points toward research on: existing Global South coordination mechanisms (BRICS, regional trade agreements, South-South trade frameworks); whether the simultaneous targeting of 16 economies creates conditions for collective bargaining or coalition-building; and what precedents exist for multilateral resistance to unilateral US trade pressure.

## Novel Insights
- **The "new normal" framing**: The initial research materials treat the Section 301 probe largely as a specific policy event triggered by the Supreme Court ruling. Xiong Jie reframes it as an instance of a recurring structural pattern — crisis externalization by a declining hegemon — that is likely to become the sustained baseline condition. This shifts the analytical frame from "how will this particular probe play out" to "how should the Global South prepare for a prolonged period of intensifying US coercive pressure."

- **Leverage asymmetry as a central axis of analysis**: While the initial summary documents that different countries face different exposures, Xiong Jie introduces the concept of leverage asymmetry as a more fundamental organizing principle. The distinction is not just between large and small economies, or between allies and non-allies, but between countries that possess strategic resources the US needs (and can therefore negotiate) and those that do not (and therefore cannot effectively resist). This frames rare earth control not merely as one factor among many but as a defining variable in determining which countries can negotiate and which will be coerced.

- **Explicit structural decline framing from a Global South scholar**: The initial research includes Western expert commentary on the probe's legal and economic dimensions, but Xiong Jie provides a perspective grounded in Global South political economy that names US behavior as a symptom of decline — not a temporary policy aberration but a structural condition. This perspective is largely absent from the initial research materials, which tend to analyze the probe through legal, economic, or geopolitical frameworks without foregrounding the decline thesis as clearly.
