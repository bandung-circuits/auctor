# Expert Insight: Fred M'Membe

## Expert Profile
- **Name**: Fred M'Membe
- **Role/Affiliation**: President of the Socialist Party of Zambia

## Raw Input
We gather in Accra today, following in the footsteps of Kenneth Kaunda, Julius Nyerere, Jomo Kenyatta, Patrice Lumumba and other pioneers of national liberation struggles, to renew a conviction born in the early twentieth century that refuses to die — Pan-Africanism. Six hundred years of slavery, colonialism and neocolonialism, six centuries of ideologies and institutions designed solely to perpetuate plunder, exploitation and the humiliation of our peoples, have taught us clearly who our friends are and who our adversaries. Consider Zambia: after independence, we had direction. I was among the first cohort of children to enrol in school after independence — free uniforms, books, pencils, and meals provided. The children of leaders shared dormitories with those of workers and farmers. Then neoliberalism arrived — privatisation, deregulation, dismantling the state — and poverty rates soared to 82 per cent in some regions, briefly making Zambia the world's fifth most hungry nation. This was no natural disaster, but the continuation of colonial legacies through structural adjustment policies. After the colonisers departed, economic control never truly passed to the people. New colonies led by puppets could not deliver the unity sought by Pan-Africanism. Without genuine sovereignty, there can be no genuine development. Without the resolve to break imperialist economic shackles, African unity remains an empty slogan.

## Core Points
1. Pan-Africanism is a living conviction rooted in the legacy of national liberation pioneers (Kaunda, Nyerere, Kenyatta, Lumumba) and remains the necessary framework for African self-determination.
2. Six centuries of slavery, colonialism, and neocolonialism form a single continuous system of plunder, exploitation, and humiliation — not distinct historical phases.
3. Post-independence Zambia initially demonstrated what sovereignty could deliver: universal access to education with material equality (leaders' children sharing dormitories with workers' and farmers' children).
4. Neoliberal structural adjustment policies — privatisation, deregulation, dismantling the state — constituted a continuation of colonial extraction by other means, driving Zambia's poverty rate to 82% in some regions.
5. Formal political independence without economic sovereignty is hollow: after the colonisers departed, economic control never truly passed to the people.
6. Neo-colonial states led by compliant leaders ("puppets") cannot deliver Pan-African unity or genuine development.
7. African unity requires the material act of breaking imperialist economic shackles — without this, it remains an empty slogan.

## Relevance to Identified Contradictions
- **Principal contradiction**: M'Membe's testimony directly reinforces the principal contradiction identified in the initial summary — that the core capitalist states' accumulated wealth rests on extraction from the periphery, and that this extraction did not end with formal decolonisation. His Zambia case study provides a concrete, firsthand illustration of how structural adjustment policies continued the extractive logic of colonialism after political independence. His framing of "six hundred years" as a single continuum challenges any attempt to sever contemporary inequality from its historical roots in slavery and colonialism.

- **Secondary contradiction — rhetoric vs. material accountability**: M'Membe's account of structural adjustment devastating Zambia directly illustrates the contradiction between Western rhetoric of "partnership" and the material reality of policies that deepened poverty and dependency. The neoliberal reforms he describes were not imposed by colonial administrators but through institutions (IMF, World Bank) that claimed to be acting in Africa's developmental interest.

- **Secondary contradiction — reparations as systemic transformation vs. redistribution**: M'Membe's position implicitly addresses the tension between reformist and transformative approaches to reparations. His insistence that "economic control never truly passed to the people" and that "imperialist economic shackles" must be broken suggests he falls firmly on the side of structural transformation rather than redistribution within existing structures. His framework demands not merely compensation but the dismantling of the mechanisms of continued extraction.

- **Secondary contradiction — intra-African divisions**: M'Membe's reference to "new colonies led by puppets" is a pointed critique of African leaders who serve as intermediaries for external interests. This acknowledges from within the Pan-African movement that continental unity is undermined not only by external pressure but by the complicity of certain African governing elites — directly relevant to the concern about fragmentation during UNGA negotiations.

## Research Direction Suggestions
1. **Structural adjustment as neo-colonial mechanism**: M'Membe's Zambia case study points toward deeper investigation of how IMF/World Bank structural adjustment programmes in the 1980s-1990s functioned as a continuation of colonial extraction. The specific trajectory — from post-independence gains to 82% poverty — demands documentation of which policies were imposed, by whom, and with what measurable consequences. This connects to the initial summary's data on Africa losing $500 billion annually through structural economic extraction.

2. **The gap between political and economic sovereignty**: The assertion that "economic control never truly passed to the people" points toward research on the specific mechanisms through which economic sovereignty was denied after formal independence — debt structures, trade agreements, commodity pricing, ownership of extractive industries, and conditions attached to development financing. This is an under-explored dimension in the initial research.

3. **Neo-colonial leadership and internal complicity**: M'Membe's "puppets" reference opens a research direction on the role of compliant African leadership in perpetuating external extraction. This connects to the initial summary's gap regarding intra-African politics around the resolution and the concern about states preferring bilateral diplomacy over collective action.

4. **Post-independence social provision as evidence of sovereignty's potential**: The personal testimony about Zambia's post-independence education system — free uniforms, books, meals, egalitarian dormitories — suggests a research angle on what African states actually achieved during their brief periods of genuine sovereignty before structural adjustment, as counter-evidence to narratives of inherent African state incapacity.

5. **Pan-Africanism as the political framework for reparations**: M'Membe explicitly roots the reparations demand in the Pan-African tradition (Kaunda, Nyerere, Kenyatta, Lumumba). This suggests investigation of how earlier Pan-African articulations framed the question of reparations and economic justice, and how the current Ghana resolution connects to or departs from that tradition.

## Novel Insights
1. **Firsthand testimony of post-independence and structural adjustment in Zambia**: M'Membe offers personal witness to both the achievements of the immediate post-independence period and the devastation wrought by neoliberalism — a before-and-after account that is not present in the initial research materials. The specific detail of children of leaders sharing dormitories with children of workers provides a vivid, concrete image of what egalitarian sovereignty looked like in practice.

2. **Structural adjustment as the bridge between colonialism and the present**: While the initial summary documents ongoing economic extraction ($500 billion in annual losses, Ghana retaining only 14% of gold value), it does not explicitly identify structural adjustment as the mechanism through which colonial economic relations were reimposed after formal independence. M'Membe's framing — "this was no natural disaster, but the continuation of colonial legacies through structural adjustment policies" — provides this analytical bridge.

3. **The "puppet" critique from within the Pan-African movement**: The initial summary notes low turnout at the AU summit and some states preferring bilateral diplomacy, but treats this as an organisational challenge. M'Membe's characterisation of compliant African leaders as running "new colonies" reframes this as a structural problem: some African states are themselves instruments of neo-colonial control, making continental unity not merely a logistical challenge but a political struggle within Africa.

4. **Sovereignty as precondition, not outcome**: M'Membe's formulation — "without genuine sovereignty, there can be no genuine development" — inverts the common framing (development leads to sovereignty) and positions economic sovereignty as the precondition for any meaningful reparatory outcome. This has implications for how the reparations demand is framed: if sovereignty must come first, then reparations cannot be merely financial transfers within existing structures but must involve the transfer of economic control.
