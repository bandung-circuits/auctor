# Domain Knowledge — Relevant Excerpts

## [M-D-001] International Legal Precedent for Reparations: Factory at Chorzow
**Source**: SRC-D-001
**Relevance**: Establishes the foundational international legal precedent for state reparations obligations, directly relevant to the legal basis Ghana invokes for its UN resolution.

> Reparations have long been seen as the best way to atone for international wrongs. *Factory at Chorzów* made this international precedent. In that case the Permanent Court of International Justice ("PCIJ") stated, "reparation must, as far as possible wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act[.]" Additionally, the ILC's Articles on State Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts state in Article 31, "The responsible State is under an obligation to make full reparation for the injury caused by the internationally wrongful act. Injury includes any damage, whether material or moral, caused by the internationally wrongful act of a State." With regards to slavery and colonization, this is complemented by Article 14 which notes, "The breach of an international obligation by an act of a State having a continuing character extends over the entire period during which the act continues and remains not in conformity with the international obligation."

## [M-D-002] Durban Declaration and EU Refusal to Apologize
**Source**: SRC-D-001
**Relevance**: Documents the key legal framework (Durban Declaration 2001) and reveals European states' deliberate strategy to avoid legal liability by refusing formal apology for slavery.

> In 2001, leaders from around the world held the World Conference Against Racism ("WCAR") in Durban, South Africa under authority of the United Nations General Assembly Resolution #52/111. There were two noteworthy and seemingly disparate outcomes from this Conference. First, the Durban Declaration and Programme of Action ("DDPA") acknowledged the historic and modern-day practices of slavery and the slave trade as morally disgraceful, and activities that would be listed as a crime against humanity today. On the other hand, there was an outright refusal by certain countries from the European Union to apologize for slavery. This push was led by Britain and joined by Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands who believed that formally apologizing for slavery and colonization (as requested by African, Caribbean, Latin American and Asian countries) would create legal implications that would force their countries to pay reparations. In order to appease these countries, the European Union released a draft statement noting its "regret" about the Trans-Atlantic slave trade.

## [M-D-003] CARICOM Reparations Commission Legal Framework
**Source**: SRC-D-002
**Relevance**: Provides the most developed existing institutional and legal framework for reparatory justice claims, which the Ghana-AU initiative builds upon.

> In 2013 Caribbean Heads of Governments established the Caricom Reparations Commission (CRC) with a mandate to prepare the case for reparatory justice for the region's indigenous and African descendant communities who are the victims of Crimes against Humanity (CAH) in the forms of genocide, slavery, slave trading, and racial apartheid. [...] The CRC asserts that victims and descendants of these CAH have a legal right to reparatory justice, and that those who committed these crimes, and who have been enriched by the proceeds of these crimes, have a reparatory case to answer. The CRJP recognizes the special role and status of European governments in this regard, being the legal bodies that instituted the framework for developing and sustaining these crimes.

## [M-D-013] Reparations as Structural Economic Transformation, Not Just Compensation
**Source**: SRC-D-007
**Relevance**: Presents the UN-level framing of reparations as systemic economic restructuring rather than mere financial transfer, articulating the economic mechanisms at stake.

> The true meaning of reparations in the 21st century cannot be confined to rectifying past wrongs through merely monetary terms. If reparations are framed solely as a historical debt to be paid, without addressing the structural injustices that have persisted and evolved, the conversation risks becoming hollow and ineffective.
>
> True reparations demand: A re-configuration of global trade systems to end Africa's dependence on raw commodity exports and build competitive, value-added economies; Transforming the international financial architecture that systematically disadvantages African nations, including ending the "African Premium" that artificially inflates borrowing costs for African nations; The restitution of African sovereignty over its natural resources, human capital, and policy choices; The end of illicit financial flows and the repatriation of stolen assets; The political will to ensure that Africans and people of African descent have an equal say in shaping the global order by introducing critical reforms in multilateral institutions.

## [M-D-014] Africa as Net Creditor: Economic Data on Continuing Extraction
**Source**: SRC-D-007
**Relevance**: Provides concrete economic data illustrating how colonial-era extraction patterns continue, underpinning the economic case for reparations.

> Ghana exported $9.58 billion in gold in 2024, yet it only retained 14% of the value due to the nature of multinational agreements. The DRC produces over 70% of the world's cobalt, yet only 1% is refined in the country before being exported. Zimbabwe was ranked as the third-largest producer of chromium in 2023, yet most was exported in raw form. Collectively, West Africa produces 70% of the world's cacao beans but contributes less than 1% of the global chocolate market. In Somalia, illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing by foreign fishing fleets costs the economy $300 million a year.
>
> In this system, Africa is a net creditor to the world, losing more than $500 billion every year through illicit financial flows, unfair trade practices, exploitative investment frameworks, and debt servicing, while it is home to some of the world's poorest populations.

## [M-D-015] Five Forms of Reparations Under International Law
**Source**: SRC-D-008
**Relevance**: Provides the standard international law taxonomy of reparation forms -- the legal framework within which any reparations mechanism must operate.

> Under international law, victims of crimes under international law and human rights violations have a right to reparations. The word reparations refers to measures to redress violations of human rights by providing a range of material and symbolic remedies to victims or their families as well as affected communities.
>
> Reparations can take several forms: Compensation, where a state would give money to those affected as a way to compensate for damage, harm or losses suffered. Rehabilitation, which can include medical and psychological care as well as legal and social services. Restitution, which involves restoring victims to the position they would have been in had the harm not occurred. Satisfaction, which include effective measures aimed at the cessation of continuing violations; verification of the facts and full and public disclosure of the truth; or a public apology. Guarantee of non-repetition, which include measures aimed at preventing harm and human rights violations in the future by addressing the root causes.

## [M-D-016] European States' Legal Argument Against Retroactive Obligation
**Source**: SRC-D-008
**Relevance**: Documents the primary legal counter-argument from European states -- that slavery was not illegal at the time and thus creates no present obligation -- which the Ghana resolution must overcome.

> One of the biggest challenges in the fight for reparations is that many States argue that slavery and colonialism weren't considered illegal acts at the time they happened. Because of that, they claim they don't have any obligation to repair today.
>
> This argument has been raised by a number of European states, notably those who benefitted and continue to benefit from the legacy of slavery and colonialism, to refute the existence of a duty under international law to provide reparation for these historic wrongs.
>
> For example, in 2022 the UK voted against a UN resolution calling on States to provide reparation for past injustices, explaining that the UK does not agree "with claims made in this resolution that states are required to make reparations for the slave trade and colonialism, which caused great suffering to many but were not, at that time, violations of international law."

## [M-D-017] Scale of the Transatlantic Slave Trade
**Source**: SRC-D-008
**Relevance**: Provides authoritative data on the scale of the transatlantic slave trade that contextualizes the magnitude of the crime the resolution addresses.

> It is estimated that between 25 million and 30 million people were violently uprooted from Africa for enslavement throughout history. From the beginning of the sixteenth century to the mid-nineteenth century, approximately 12.5 million Africans were enslaved by Europeans and shipped to colonies in the Americas in what has come to be known as the "transatlantic slave trade." Of those 12.5 million enslaved Africans, it is estimated that close to 2 million lost their lives during the Middle Passage across the Atlantic Ocean.
>
> The colonial empires built by European states between the 15th and 20th centuries extended across nearly 80% of the entire globe. This means that two-thirds of the membership of the United Nations, approximately 127 out of 193 Member States, were subject to European colonial rule at some point in history.
