# Tricontinental Dossier Style Guide
## A Prescriptive Writing Manual Derived from Corpus Analysis

*Based on systematic stylistic analysis of 5 Tricontinental: Institute for Social Research dossiers spanning political economy of drugs, Indian economic crisis, anti-imperialist history, Palestinian cultural resistance, and the environmental crisis*

---

## Part I: Analytical Summary

### Corpus Overview

The analyzed corpus comprises dossiers from Tricontinental: Institute for Social Research, a politically engaged research institute operating from a Marxist, anti-imperialist perspective. Document types include:

- Political-economic analyses of global systems (drug trade, Indian deindustrialisation)
- Historical-political narratives tracing anti-colonial and anti-imperialist struggle
- Cultural analyses of resistance movements (Palestinian art and cultural production)
- Policy-oriented critiques with programmatic agendas (environmental crisis)
- Hybrid forms combining scholarly research, first-person testimony, and political argumentation

### Key Stylistic Observations

The Tricontinental dossier voice is characterised by **partisan scholarly authority**, **structural-systemic argumentation**, and **solidarity with the oppressed**. The writing fuses academic rigour with political commitment, employing a dialectical stance that positions the reader as an informed ally who must understand the deep structures of imperialism, capitalism, and resistance. The prose moves fluidly between historical narrative, economic analysis, direct testimony from workers and activists, and explicit political argumentation -- always grounding abstract claims in concrete material conditions.

## Part II: Prescriptive Style Guide

## Section A: Voice and Stance

### A.1 Persona

**Write as a politically committed research collective speaking from a position of solidarity with the oppressed.** The implied author is a Marxist analytical intelligence -- rooted in collaboration with popular movements, grounded in historical materialism, and unafraid to name the systemic causes of injustice. The voice is institutional but warm, scholarly but accessible, analytical but partisan.

- Present the institute as an organisation embedded in popular struggles, drawing authority from both research and direct engagement with workers, peasants, and movements
- Claim authority through historical evidence, structural analysis, and testimony from the communities studied
- Use institutional first-person plural ("we," "our") to signal collective authorship and solidarity
- Position findings as products of both rigorous research and lived experience of collaborating organisations
- Never pretend to neutrality; the commitment to the working class and oppressed peoples is explicit

**Example:**
> "Over the past several years, our work with peasant organisations across the world -- particularly COCCAM in Colombia -- has allowed us to examine the drug economy not as a matter of crime and security but as a window into the deeper structures of contemporary capitalism."

> "This dossier seeks to popularise the debate on the environmental crisis from a Brazilian and Global South perspective in dialogue with working-class organisations."

**Avoid:**
- Simulated objectivity or false neutrality ("some might argue...")
- Individual authorial voice ("I believe...")
- Detached academic distance that severs the connection to struggle

### A.2 Relationship to Reader

**Address the reader as a politically engaged intellectual and potential ally.** Assume the reader is literate, interested in structural analysis, and sympathetic to (or at least curious about) anti-imperialist and socialist perspectives. Treat the reader as someone who wants to understand the world in order to change it.

**Pronoun patterns:**

| Context | Pronoun | Usage |
|---------|---------|-------|
| Institutional analysis and argumentation | "we" / "our" | Primary voice for the institute's positions and research |
| Inclusive political claims | "we" / "our" | To include reader in shared humanity or struggle: "we must not forget" |
| Naming the oppressed | Third-person collective nouns | "the peasantry," "the working class," "campesinos," "the Palestinian people" |
| Naming the oppressor | Third-person specific | "the United States," "the ruling classes," "capital," "imperialism" |
| Direct address | Rare; only in quoted speech | Avoid "you" in the analytical voice |

**Reader positioning constructions:**
- "This dossier seeks to undermine the dominant narrative that..."
- "It is evident that a decisive break from the neoliberal tenets... is essential"
- "One thing is clear: there are no capitalist solutions to a crisis created by capitalism"
- "If we are to save the Earth and humanity, the answers must come from beyond capitalism"

**Avoid:**
- Condescension toward the reader's political knowledge
- Assuming the reader shares all premises without establishing them through argument and evidence
- Marketing language or consultancy tone

### A.3 Epistemic Stance

**Express analytical claims with confident conviction grounded in evidence.** Use hedging sparingly and strategically -- for genuinely uncertain empirical claims, not for political positions. The Tricontinental voice is assertive about structural analysis while acknowledging empirical limitations when they exist.

**Hedging vocabulary (use for empirical uncertainties, estimates, contested data):**

| Hedge Type | Approved Terms |
|------------|----------------|
| Estimation | "estimated," "roughly," "approximately," "about" |
| Uncertainty | "the actual share may be even lower," "these figures should be read as indicative" |
| Attribution | "according to one estimate," "as one study notes" |
| Qualification | "in some cases," "in practice," "often" |

**Boosting vocabulary (use for structural claims, political positions, systemic analysis):**

| Booster Type | Approved Terms |
|--------------|----------------|
| Structural certainty | "it is clear that," "it is evident that," "what is apparent" |
| Political conviction | "must," "will not," "cannot," "is essential" |
| Demonstrative force | "reveals," "exposes," "makes visible," "demonstrates" |
| Emphatic assertion | "in fact," "indeed," "precisely," "precisely because" |
| Moral force | "cannot be allowed to," "is nothing less than," "there is no doubt" |

**Calibration guidance:**
- Structural-systemic claims: State directly with full conviction ("The environmental crisis is a product of the crisis of capital")
- Empirical data with acknowledged uncertainty: Hedge appropriately ("According to one estimate, in 2015 the unequal exchange... totalled $10.8--$14.1 trillion")
- Political conclusions: Express with imperative force ("The starting point must be neither militarisation nor eradication but the reconstruction of rural life")
- Controversial factual claims: Provide evidence then state firmly ("Despite offering zero evidence for the connection... the US has engaged in a violent campaign")

**Example of calibrated stance:**
> "These figures should be read as indicative, since most countries do not collect reliable national statistics on IFFs." [Hedged empirical claim]
>
> "The persistent underdevelopment of industry, particularly manufacturing, lies at the core of India's tryst with globalisation." [Confident structural claim]
>
> "Any genuine solution must therefore begin with the campesinos and with the recognition that the frontier between legal and illegal capitalism is far thinner, and far more politically useful, than the architects of the War on Drugs will admit." [Assertive political conclusion]

### A.4 Evaluative Stance

**Evaluate explicitly and unapologetically from the standpoint of the oppressed.** Unlike consultancy writing that evaluates through implication, the Tricontinental voice makes its evaluative commitments transparent. Use evaluative language that names systemic violence, structural exploitation, and the agency of resistance.

**Approved evaluative vocabulary (negative -- for systems of oppression):**

| Term | Usage Context |
|------|---------------|
| exploitation, super-exploitation | Labour relations under capitalism |
| plunder, drain, extraction | Wealth transfer from South to North |
| criminalisation | State treatment of peasants, the poor |
| dispossession | Land and livelihood theft |
| violence, brutality, barbarism | Imperial and colonial acts |
| devastation, destruction | Environmental and social consequences |
| hollowing out, erosion, attrition | Deindustrialisation processes |
| rotten, parasitic | Financial and banking systems |
| failed, meagre, dismal | Neoliberal policy outcomes |

**Approved evaluative vocabulary (positive -- for people's struggle and alternatives):**

| Term | Usage Context |
|------|---------------|
| resistance, defiance, steadfastness | Popular and national liberation movements |
| dignity, emancipation, liberation | Goals of struggle |
| solidarity, internationalism | Movement relations |
| sovereignty, self-determination | Political aspirations |
| organised, mobilised | Working-class and peasant action |
| popular, democratic | Alternatives from below |
| genuine, real, decisive | Contrasted with false or inadequate solutions |

**Evaluative constructions:**
- "This is not a neutral classification: it was a political decision taken in an international system dominated by the North" [Unmasking false neutrality]
- "The fallacy -- and failure -- of such projects is clear" [Direct judgement]
- "These are conditions of state abandonment that both sustain and are reinforced by the coca economy" [Structural critique]
- "The entire process of accumulation was bathed in blood" [Visceral moral evaluation]

**Avoid:**
- Euphemism for imperial violence ("intervention" instead of "invasion" when the latter is accurate)
- Both-sides framing that implies moral equivalence between oppressor and oppressed
- Evaluative language disconnected from material evidence

## Section B: Sentence-Level Patterns

### B.1 Sentence Types

**Distribution target:**
- Declarative: 92--97%
- Interrogative: 2--5% (primarily rhetorical questions that structure argument)
- Imperative: 1--3% (in programmatic/conclusion sections and demands)
- Exclamative: Rare, only in quoted speech or historical declarations

**Rhetorical questions:** Use to frame sections, challenge dominant narratives, or prompt the reader's critical thinking. Place at the start of sections or paragraphs.

**Approved question patterns:**
- "So, how does Zionism, as a settler-colonial project, fabricate the legitimacy to carry out a programme of such violence and barbarity?"
- "But is the Bandung Spirit the same as the Tricontinental Spirit?"
- "What can your camera do when the whole world does nothing to help me?"
- "Can cultural production humanise a people, rescue their history, and advance their struggle?"

**Imperatives:** Reserve for programmatic demands, calls to action, and policy prescriptions. Cluster in concluding or agenda sections.
- "Halt all illegal deforestation by 2026."
- "Implement comprehensive agrarian reforms that decentralise and democratise access to land."
- "Classify ecocide as a crime in national legislation."

### B.2 Sentence Complexity

**Target sentence length:** 25--50 words for body text; sentences may extend to 60+ words for complex analytical constructions that require qualification and layered argumentation. Short sentences (10--20 words) are used for emphasis and to deliver key claims.

**Complexity distribution:**
- Simple sentences: 15--20% (for delivering key claims, emphatic points, section-opening declarations)
- Compound sentences: 20--25% (for balanced comparisons, coordinated arguments)
- Complex sentences: 55--65% (for nuanced analysis with subordination, qualification, and embedded evidence)

**Maximum embedding depth:** 3 levels of subordination (the Tricontinental style tolerates -- and requires -- more complex sentences than consultancy writing)

**Examples of appropriate complexity:**

Short emphatic sentence:
> "Imperialism was intolerable to these young people."

Standard analytical sentence (30--40 words):
> "The persistent underdevelopment of industry, particularly manufacturing, lies at the core of India's tryst with globalisation."

Long analytical sentence with layered subordination (50--70 words):
> "In practice, Plan Colombia's counternarcotics architecture also operated as a counterinsurgency and territorial-recapture project aimed at reasserting state control in coca-growing regions (the FARC-EP itself emerged from campesino self-defence communities and drew much of its social base from rural Colombia)."

### B.3 Sentence Openings

**Approved opening patterns:**

| Pattern | Example | Frequency |
|---------|---------|-----------|
| Subject-first (standard) | "The economy of illicit commodities... has a peculiar logic" | 35% |
| Temporal/circumstantial | "From the 1830s onwards..." / "In 1966..." / "By the late nineteenth century..." | 20% |
| Concessive/contrastive | "Although...," "Despite...," "While...," "Yet..." | 15% |
| Demonstrative reference | "This dossier seeks to..." / "These conditions are central to..." | 10% |
| Prepositional phrase (thematic) | "In the modern era..." / "In practice..." / "At its root..." | 10% |
| Participial | "Taken together..." / "Produced under..." / "Drawing on..." | 5% |
| Conditional | "If we are to save..." / "If the objective is to end..." | 3% |
| Emphatic/evaluative | "What is apparent..." / "One thing is clear..." / "It is evident that..." | 2% |

**Avoid:**
- Beginning with "It is interesting to note that..." or similar filler
- Starting with numerals (spell out or restructure)
- Excessive use of "There is/are" constructions

### B.4 Coordination and Subordination

**Preferred coordinating conjunctions:**

| Conjunction | Function | Example |
|-------------|----------|---------|
| and | Addition, accumulation | "to take land, exploit labour, and extract bullion" |
| but | Sharp contrast, correction | "But what they came to do was plunder" |
| yet | Unexpected contrast, resistance | "Yet these same conditions have fuelled generations of organised struggle" |
| while | Simultaneous or contrastive processes | "While the ruling classes insist... we must not forget..." |
| or | Alternative, disjunction | "Whether the narcotic drug is plant-based (cocaine) or synthetic (methamphetamine)" |

**Preferred subordinating conjunctions:**

| Conjunction | Function | Example |
|-------------|----------|---------|
| because | Causation | "...because capitalism requires the constant replenishment of new reservoirs of wealth" |
| since | Causal/temporal | "Since only a small share of these crimes is ever detected..." |
| although / though | Concession | "Although coca is native to the Andes..." |
| as | Reason/simultaneity | "As Fidel Castro warned..." |
| when | Temporal | "When India gained independence in 1947..." |
| where | Locative | "...in regions where the state's presence has long been militarised" |
| if | Conditional | "If we are to save humanity from this self-destruction..." |

**Logical connectors for discourse cohesion:**

| Function | Approved Connectors |
|----------|---------------------|
| Addition | moreover, furthermore, in addition, also, beyond this |
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, nonetheless, by contrast, on the other hand, yet |
| Consequence | therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, accordingly |
| Concession | despite, notwithstanding, although, while |
| Exemplification | for example, for instance, such as, including, among them |
| Specification | in particular, specifically, notably, especially, precisely |
| Reformulation | in other words, that is, more correctly, put differently |
| Summarisation | in sum, taken together, in the long run |
| Temporal sequence | initially, subsequently, eventually, over the past [period], from [date] onwards |

### B.5 Passive vs. Active Voice

**Target ratio:** 20--30% passive constructions (significantly lower than consultancy writing; the Tricontinental voice privileges clear attribution of agency)

**Use passive voice for:**
- Describing what is done to oppressed peoples when agency is diffuse or systemic: "Peasants are directly attacked through the process of violent dispossession"
- Methodological descriptions: "The online survey was in the field from..."
- Foregrounding the affected: "More than 67,000 Palestinians have been killed"
- When the agent is obvious from context: "The trade was entrenched and its reach widened"

**Use active voice for:**
- Attributing imperial and capitalist agency: "The United States unilaterally began to break down the global arms control regime"
- Describing resistance and popular action: "COCCAM was established, bringing together peasant, indigenous, and Afro-descendant communities"
- Making structural arguments: "Capital accumulated in the China trade also flowed into US enterprises"
- Political conclusions and demands: "The environmental struggle must therefore be waged as a direct confrontation with... the capitalist mode of production"

**Critical principle:** When naming the agents of violence, exploitation, and oppression, always prefer active voice to make agency visible. Use passive voice to describe the oppressed only when emphasising their position as affected parties.

**Example of strategic voice alternation:**
> "These criminal organisations establish new rural and urban dynamics of territorial control [active -- agent named] while at the same time providing precarious economic opportunities for rural workers [active -- structural irony]. In this way, lives can be expended without disrupting the process of capital accumulation [passive -- systemic violence, agent deliberately obscured to show systemic nature]."

## Section C: Vocabulary and Word Choice

### C.1 Register and Formality

**Maintain a scholarly-political register that bridges academic rigour and popular accessibility.** The writing should be comprehensible to educated non-specialists while deploying Marxist and political-economic terminology with precision. Unlike academic writing, the register allows for occasional vivid, even visceral, imagery. Unlike journalistic writing, it sustains dense analytical argumentation.

**Register characteristics:**
- Latinate and Greek-derived vocabulary for formal analytical concepts (accumulation, exploitation, appropriation)
- Marxist and political-economic terminology used with confidence, sometimes glossed
- Occasional Germanic vocabulary for directness and moral force ("plunder," "drain," "blood")
- Foreign-language terms (especially Spanish and Arabic) preserved in italics with glosses
- No colloquialisms or slang in the analytical voice (though these may appear in quoted testimony)
- Contractions avoided in analytical prose

**Formal alternatives and preferred terms:**

| Avoid (too neutral/euphemistic) | Use (politically precise) |
|--------------------------------|--------------------------|
| developing countries | Global South, dependent countries, the periphery |
| developed countries | Global North, imperial centres, the capitalist core |
| growth | accumulation, expansion (specify for whom) |
| reform | transformation, restructuring (if systemic change) |
| conflict | class struggle, armed struggle, resistance |
| stakeholders | the working class, peasants, popular movements |
| intervention | invasion, occupation, interference |
| aid | solidarity, reparation, climate compensation |
| resources | commons (when referring to shared natural wealth) |
| market failures | contradictions of capitalism |
| human capital | the workforce, workers, the toiling classes |

### C.2 Technical Terminology

**Use Marxist, political-economic, and movement-specific terms with confidence. Define specialised or less familiar terms on first use, especially those from non-English contexts.**

**Core political-economic vocabulary (use freely):**

| Term | Usage |
|------|-------|
| capitalism / capitalist mode of production | The systemic framework being analysed and critiqued |
| imperialism / imperial | The global system of domination by the North |
| neoliberalism / neoliberal | The post-1970s policy regime of privatisation, liberalisation, austerity |

| proletariat / working class / toiling classes | Those who sell their labour |

| sovereignty | Control over territory, resources, and political life |
| dignity | The inherent worth and rights of every person |

### C.3 Semantic Fields

**Primary semantic fields and their key vocabulary:**

**Field: Capitalist Exploitation and Accumulation**
- Preferred: accumulation, exploitation, super-exploitation, extraction, plunder, drain, dispossession, expropriation, appropriation, concentration
- Associated verbs: exploit, extract, plunder, drain, dispossess, accumulate, concentrate, siphon
- Avoid: "wealth creation" (obscures exploitation), "trickle-down" (ideological), "development" without qualification

**Field: Imperialism and Domination**
- Preferred: imperialism, colonialism, neo-colonialism, hegemony, domination, subjugation, occupation, blockade, asphyxiation
- Associated verbs: impose, dominate, subjugate, occupy, blockade, bomb, invade, discipline, crush
- Avoid: "influence" (too weak for structural domination), "involvement" (euphemistic for invasion)

**Field: Resistance and Liberation**
- Preferred: resistance, struggle, liberation, emancipation, revolution, solidarity, mobilisation, defiance, steadfastness, sovereignty, dignity
- Associated verbs: resist, struggle, fight, liberate, emancipate, organise, mobilise, demand, defend, reclaim
- Avoid: "advocacy" (too institutional), "engagement" (too neutral)

**Field: Political Economy and Structure**
- Preferred: structure, system, mode of production, commodity chain, financial system, trade regime, regulatory apparatus, class character
- Associated verbs: structure, reproduce, sustain, reinforce, entrench, deepen, undermine, erode
- Avoid: "ecosystem" (corporate jargon), "landscape" (vague)

**Field: The Environment and Ecological Crisis**
- Preferred: environmental crisis, destruction, devastation, deforestation, pollution, commons, biodiversity, agroecology, food sovereignty
- Associated verbs: destroy, devastate, deforest, pollute, poison, commodify, financialise, protect, restore, reforest
- Avoid: "sustainability" without qualification (co-opted by green capitalism), "natural resources" (prefer "commons")

### C.4 Nominalization Patterns

**Use nominalization moderately to heavily. Nominalizations are essential for naming systemic processes, structural conditions, and political concepts. However, balance them with verbal forms to maintain narrative momentum and attribute agency.**

**Approved nominalizations (high frequency):**

| Verb/Adjective | Nominalization | Example Usage |
|----------------|----------------|---------------|
| accumulate | accumulation | "originary accumulation resumes once more" |
| exploit | exploitation | "vulnerable to super exploitation at unimaginable levels" |
| liberate | liberation | "national liberation through armed struggle" |
| industrialise | industrialisation / deindustrialisation | "the trajectory of deindustrialisation" |
| criminalise | criminalisation | "the criminalisation of growers" |
| dispossess | dispossession | "violent dispossession" |
| eradicate | eradication | "eradication and militarisation will not end..." |
| privatise | privatisation | "aggressive privatisation and chronic neglect" |
| liberalise | liberalisation | "trade liberalisation accelerated" |
| deforest | deforestation | "halt all illegal deforestation" |

**Balance guidance:**
- Use nominalization for naming systemic processes and structural conditions ("deindustrialisation," "financialisation")
- Use verbal forms when attributing agency and action ("the United States invaded," not "the US invasion occurred")
- Chains of 3+ consecutive nominalizations are acceptable when naming systemic processes but should be broken up for readability in narrative passages

## Section D: Paragraph and Discourse Structure

### D.1 Paragraph Structure

**Target paragraph length:** 4--8 sentences; 80--200 words. Paragraphs in Tricontinental dossiers are substantially longer than in consultancy writing, reflecting the need for sustained analytical argumentation.

**Structural template:**
1. **Topic/claim sentence:** State the main analytical point, historical fact, or argument
2. **Historical or structural context:** Place the claim within broader historical or systemic context
3. **Evidence/elaboration:** Provide data, historical examples, testimony, or structural analysis
4. **Specification/complication:** Add nuance, qualifications, or additional dimensions
5. **Political implication/transition:** Draw out the political significance or connect to the next argument

**Example of well-structured paragraph:**
> "Coca production locks households into economic dependence and highly informal work throughout the year, since the mature coca leaf is harvested every two months. [Topic] In addition, it achieves rapid production at low cost, prompt commercialisation, and an average net monthly income estimated at about 56% of the Colombian minimum wage (in 2018) per hectare cultivated. [Evidence] However, this figure masks wide variation and does not reflect how coercion, intermediaries, transport bottlenecks, and price volatility can further squeeze what growers take home. [Complication] Our joint research with the National Coordination of Coca, Poppy, and Marijuana Growers (COCCAM) found that not only is production labour intensive and time consuming, but the rate of return for peasants is negligible. [Implication with institutional positioning]"

### D.2 Cohesion Devices

**Reference patterns:**
- Use full names and organisational titles on first reference; use shortened names or acronyms thereafter
- Use demonstratives ("this," "these," "such") for backward reference to analytical concepts
- Use definite article "the" to signal established referents and shared knowledge
- Use lexical repetition of key terms (do not vary terminology for the sake of elegant variation when precision demands consistency: "imperialism" should remain "imperialism," not rotate through "hegemony," "domination," etc., unless a distinct concept is intended)

**Topic maintenance:**
- Begin sentences with given information (what has been established)
- End sentences with new information or the key analytical point (end-focus)
- Maintain thematic threads across paragraphs through lexical repetition and demonstrative reference

**Discourse markers for transitions:**

| Function | Markers |
|----------|---------|
| Introducing topics | "This dossier seeks to...," "The question of...," "At its root lie..." |
| Advancing argument | "Furthermore...," "Moreover...," "In addition...," "Beyond this..." |
| Contrasting | "However...," "Yet...," "Nevertheless...," "But...," "On the other hand..." |
| Conceding | "Although...," "Despite...," "While it is true that...," "Notwithstanding..." |
| Exemplifying | "For example...," "For instance...," "Such as...," "Among them..." |
| Specifying | "In particular...," "Specifically...," "Notably...," "Precisely because..." |
| Concluding/summarising | "Taken together...," "In sum...," "In the long run...," "What is apparent..." |
| Temporal framing | "From [date] onwards...," "By the [decade]...," "In the years after...," "Over the past [period]..." |
| Reformulating | "In other words...," "That is...," "More correctly...," "Put differently..." |

### D.3 Information Structure

**Given-New progression:** Begin with established facts, historical context, or previously stated claims; introduce new analysis, evidence, or political implications at sentence end for emphasis.

**Example:**
> "The nature of illicit commodities like cocaine [given] is such that demand is largely price-inelastic [new-structural claim], especially among dependent users, since addiction keeps users coming back regardless of price [new-causal explanation]."

**Introducing new concepts:** When introducing a new concept, term, or framework, provide a definitional clause, a brief gloss in parentheses, or an extended explanation in the following sentence.

**Example:**
> "He and his comrades had led the Cuban Revolution's victory over the United States and its Central Intelligence Agency in the 1961 Bay of Pigs Invasion."
>
> "Originary accumulation is not a one-off prelude that sets in place a perpetual motion machine. It can be understood as periodic: originary accumulation resumes once more whenever capital, starved of liquidity, seeks out new reservoirs to satisfy its need."

### D.4 Argumentation Patterns

**Structure arguments dialectically.** The Tricontinental dossier follows a pattern of: (1) naming the dominant/false narrative, (2) exposing its structural function, (3) presenting the material reality from the standpoint of the oppressed, and (4) drawing political conclusions.

**Argumentative template:**

1. **Name the dominant narrative:** "The dominant narrative of the War on Drugs is revealed as a profound misdiagnosis"
2. **Expose its function:** "This narrative rests on the claim that the drug trade is somehow separate from 'legitimate' capitalism"
3. **Present counter-evidence from below:** "From the standpoint of these communities..." / "Campesinos know that..."
4. **Draw structural-political conclusions:** "If the objective is to end the violence... then the starting point must be..."

**Argumentative verbs:**

| Function | Approved Verbs |
|----------|---------------|
| Asserting the institute's position | argue, contend, maintain, emphasise, insist |
| Presenting evidence | demonstrate, show, reveal, make visible, expose, illuminate |
| Critiquing dominant narratives | undermine, challenge, refute, unmask, lay bare |
| Attributing claims to others | claim, assert, insist, allege (for adversaries); note, observe, explain, point out (for allies) |
| Drawing conclusions | follow that, suggest, indicate, underscore, confirm |

**Handling counterarguments:** The Tricontinental voice does not give equal weight to oppressor and oppressed perspectives. Counterarguments from dominant powers are named, quoted (often with scare quotes), and then systematically dismantled through evidence and structural analysis.

**Example:**
> "Far from what the media would have us believe, and far from being the result of a 'criminal association' between large drug cartels and peasant communities, Colombia's coca economy is rooted in the country's historical development and the evolution of its armed conflict."

## Section E: Citation and Attribution

### E.1 Citation Density

**Cite sources frequently and substantively.** Every major empirical claim, historical fact, and data point should be sourced. The dossier uses endnotes (numbered, placed at the end of each section or document) rather than in-text author-date citations.

**Citation guidance:**
- Historical claims: Always cite primary or scholarly sources
- Empirical data (statistics, prices, percentages): Cite institutional sources (UNODC, World Bank, government reports)
- Testimonial evidence: Attribute to named organisations or individuals, with context
- Political-theoretical claims: Cite foundational texts (Marx, Fanon, Cabral) when building on their frameworks
- Self-evident structural arguments do not require citation if they follow logically from cited evidence

### E.2 Citation Style

**Use superscript numerals for endnote references.** Place the numeral at the end of the sentence or clause to which the citation refers, after punctuation.

**Introducing cited authors:**

For allied thinkers and comrades:
- "As Fidel Castro warned in his speech at the 1992 UN Conference..."
- "As Vijay Prashad rightly pointed out..."
- "As one COCCAM leader put it..."
- "Cabral told his fellow revolutionaries..."
- "In _The Wretched of the Earth_ (1961), Frantz Fanon mused about..."

For adversaries and official sources:
- "According to the US's own Drug Enforcement Administration..."
- "Nixon told reporters that..."
- "Israeli Defence Minister Yoav Gallant delivered what would become a defining statement..."

**Reporting verbs:**

| For allies and movements | For adversaries and official discourse |
|--------------------------|---------------------------------------|
| argued, explained, emphasised, noted, pointed out, underscored, warned, observed, affirmed, recalled | claimed, alleged, insisted, declared, stated, announced, promoted, touted |

### E.3 Quotation Patterns

**Use direct quotation generously, especially for:**
- Testimony from workers, peasants, and movement leaders (preserves their voice)
- Key political declarations and speeches (historical documents)
- Foundational theoretical texts (Marx, Fanon, Cabral, Mao, Kanafani)
- Adversary statements that expose their ideology (Israeli officials, US officials)
- Poetry and literary texts (cultural resistance)

**Integrate quotations by:**
- Using a reporting clause followed by a colon for extended quotations
- Using block quotes (indented) for passages of 3+ sentences or for poetry
- Embedding short quotations within sentences using quotation marks

**Example of testimony integration:**
> As one COCCAM leader put it:
>
> > The peasantry has been judged and labelled as if we _chose_ to cultivate the coca leaf, when it has been the circumstances, the lack of involvement of local and national governments in the territory...

**Example of adversary quotation:**
> Israeli Defence Minister Yoav Gallant delivered what would become a defining statement of the genocide: 'We are fighting human animals, and we are acting accordingly'.

**Scare quotes:** Use single quotation marks around terms whose validity is being challenged or whose usage is attributed to the adversary: 'War on Drugs', 'civilisation', 'legitimate' capitalism, 'sustainability', 'free' trade.

## Section F: Document Structure

### F.1 Section Organisation

**Standard dossier architecture:**

1. **Title** (declarative or thematic, often with subtitle using colon)
2. **Epigraph** (optional; a poem, speech excerpt, or foundational quotation -- often from Marx, Fidel Castro, Mahmoud Darwish, or other revolutionary figures)
3. **Introduction / Framing section** (without heading, or with a brief thematic heading; establishes the argument, names the dominant narrative, and positions the dossier's intervention)
4. **Body sections** (3--5 major sections with ## headings, sometimes with ### subsections)
5. **Conclusion** (explicit or folded into the final section; draws political conclusions and points toward alternatives or further struggle)
6. **Notes** (endnotes numbered sequentially)

**Section heading conventions:**
- Use sentence case for headings
- Headings may be thematic phrases, declarative claims, or evocative titles
- Examples: "The Political Economy of Illicit Drugs," "How the Campesinos See the 'War on Drugs'," "Green Capitalism: Supposed Alternatives to the Environmental Crisis," "Two, Three, Many Vietnams"
- Use ## for major sections, ### for subsections
- Number subsections with Roman numerals or Arabic numbers when presenting policy agendas

### F.2 Introduction Conventions

**The introduction (often the first 2--4 paragraphs before any section heading) must:**
1. Establish the political-historical context for the dossier's subject
2. Name the dominant narrative or ideological framework being challenged
3. State the dossier's argument or thesis clearly
4. Position the dossier within the institute's broader work and collaborations
5. Preview the structure or method of the analysis

**Example introduction elements:**
> "This dossier seeks to undermine the dominant narrative that presents the 'War on Drugs' as a genuine moral crusade by the capitalist states..." [Thesis]
>
> "Our text draws on historical research on the past decades of the War on Drugs, key United Nations (UN) documents, and our own work with _campesino_ (peasant) organisations in Colombia." [Method and collaboration]

### F.3 Conclusion Conventions

**Conclusions must:**
1. Synthesise the structural argument established throughout the dossier
2. Restate the argument from the standpoint of the oppressed
3. Name what genuine transformation would require (even if currently politically foreclosed)
4. End with an affirmation of struggle, resistance, or the necessity of continued organising

**Example conclusion elements:**
> "The peasantry knows this truth intimately. Their experience shows that the drug economy persists not because of the coca leaf itself, but because capitalism requires the constant replenishment of new reservoirs of wealth -- licit or illicit -- to sustain itself." [Synthesis from below]
>
> "Any genuine solution must therefore begin with the campesinos and with the recognition that the frontier between legal and illegal capitalism is far thinner, and far more politically useful, than the architects of the War on Drugs will admit." [Political conclusion]

**Avoid:**
- Endings that suggest the problem is merely technical or requires only better policy
- Defeatist conclusions; always affirm the possibility and necessity of struggle
- Summaries that merely recapitulate without advancing the argument

## Section G: Specific Constructions and Phrases

### G.1 Approved Formulations by Function

**Introducing the dossier's argument:**
- "This dossier seeks to..."
- "This dossier argues that..."
- "In this dossier, we analyse..."
- "In light of this reality, this dossier seeks to popularise..."
- "With that in mind..."

**Naming the dominant narrative to be challenged:**
- "The dominant narrative that presents..."
- "This narrative rests on the claim that..."
- "Far from what the media would have us believe..."
- "The paradigm of the War on Drugs has allowed..."

**Stating the institute's position:**
- "We argue instead that..."
- "Our starting point is..."
- "We must not forget that..."
- "It is evident that..."
- "What is apparent is..."
- "One thing is clear:"

**Presenting structural analysis:**
- "At its root lie..."
- "The persistent [problem] lies at the core of..."
- "The nature of [phenomenon] is such that..."
- "These dynamics reproduce a long history of..."
- "The link between [X] and [Y] is not confined to..."

**Introducing evidence and testimony:**
- "As [name] explained..."
- "As [name] put it..."
- "According to one COCCAM leader..."
- "In an interview with Tricontinental, [name] explained that..."
- "As [authority] rightly pointed out..."
- "According to [institution]..."
- "Recent data shows that..."
- "A [year] study by [institution] found that..."

**Making historical connections:**
- "From the [date] to today..."
- "In the years after..."
- "This was not a singular historical moment but an ongoing event of..."
- "The era of [X] began with..."
- "Though that [X] could not yet [Y], it would remain..."

**Drawing political conclusions:**
- "If the objective is to [end X], then the starting point must be..."
- "Any genuine solution must therefore begin with..."
- "The struggle for [X] must combat not only [Y] but also..."
- "Unless we challenge the logic of capital... we will not be able to..."
- "This is the task of the rural and urban working classes"

**Expressing historical necessity:**
- "Our movement is not born out of a choice. It is a necessity."
- "There are no capitalist solutions to a crisis created by capitalism."
- "Eradication, criminalisation, and militarisation will not end..."
- "Without transforming [conditions], the cycle will simply reproduce itself"
- "Without confronting [institutions], [the system] will continue to function as..."

**Transitioning between sections:**
- "Taken together, these dynamics..."
- "In this context..."
- "Against this backdrop..."
- "What is at stake is nothing less than..."
- "With the signing of [agreement]..."
- "Over the past two years..."

**Incorporating foreign-language terms:**
- "_Campesino_ (peasant)"
- "_sumud_ (steadfastness)"
- "_ursprungliche Akkumulation_, or 'originary' accumulation"
- "_buen vivir_ (good living)"

### G.2 Epigraph and Extended Quotation Conventions

**Epigraphs:** Place at the opening of the dossier or at the start of major sections. Use block quote formatting. Include full attribution (name, title/source, date).

**Example:**
> > We cannot leave this part of the subject without singling out one flagrant self-contradiction of the Christianity-canting and civilisation-mongering British Government...
> >
> > Karl Marx, 'Free Trade and Monopoly', 25 September 1858.

**Extended testimony:** Set apart in block quotes. Introduce with a reporting clause that names the speaker, their role, and the context.

**Example:**
> Another COCCAM member, a 26-year-old grower, said:
>
> > You may need between six and ten workers per hectare. That needs to be emphasised, because coca leaf generates a lot of employment.

### G.3 Constructions to Avoid

| Construction | Reason | Alternative |
|--------------|--------|-------------|
| "It is interesting to note that..." | Filler, weakens claim | State the finding directly |
| "Both sides have valid points..." | False equivalence | Name the structural asymmetry |
| "The international community..." | Euphemism for Western power | Specify which states, institutions, or blocs |
| "Developing countries" | Modernisation theory framing | "Global South," "dependent countries," "the periphery" |
| "Natural resources" (uncritically) | Implies nature exists for extraction | "Commons," or specify the element |
| "Stakeholders" | Corporate/NGO jargon | Specify: workers, peasants, communities, movements |
| "Sustainable development" (uncritically) | Co-opted by green capitalism | "Agroecology," "food sovereignty," "popular agrarian reform" |
| "Failed state" | Imperialist framing | Describe the specific conditions and their causes |
| "Terrorist" / "terrorism" (for liberation movements) | Imperial labelling | "Resistance," "armed struggle," "liberation movement" |
| "Conflict" (without specifying agents) | Obscures agency | Name who attacked whom and why |
| "I think that..." | Individual voice inappropriate | Use institutional "we" or state the claim directly |
| "It could be argued that..." | Weakens the argument | State the argument and support it |
| Scare quotes on progressive terms ("democracy," "rights") | Undermines the very concepts being fought for | Reserve scare quotes for adversary framings |

## Section H: Graphology and Visual Conventions

### H.1 Typography

**Headers:**
- H1 (#): Document title (once per document)
- H2 (##): Major sections
- H3 (###): Subsections (including "Notes," "Acknowledgments")
- Bold: Key terms within lists, emphasis on critical phrases
- Italics: Foreign-language terms, book and publication titles, emphasis within quoted material

**Punctuation:**
- Use serial (Oxford) comma
- Use en-dash (--) for parenthetical insertions and ranges: "1839--1842"
- Place periods and commas inside quotation marks for full quotations; use logical punctuation for scare quotes and partial quotations
- Use British spelling conventions throughout (criminalisation, organisation, defence, labour, centre)
- Use single quotation marks for scare quotes and terms being critiqued: 'War on Drugs', 'free' trade
- Use double quotation marks for direct speech within the analytical voice

### H.2 List Formatting

**Numbered lists:**
- Use for sequential steps (commodity chains), enumerated policy demands, historical periodisations
- Begin each item with bold keyword or category name when presenting policy agendas
- End items with periods if they are complete sentences (which they usually are in the Tricontinental style)
- Sub-items use secondary numbering (1.1, 1.2) or lettering (a, b, c)

**Bulleted lists:**
- Use for acknowledgments, enumerated organisations, and parallel items
- Begin each item with a capital letter
- Maintain grammatical parallelism across items

**Policy demand formatting:**
- Number demands with Roman numerals for major categories (I, II, III...)
- Use bullet points within each category for specific demands
- Begin each demand with a strong verb in the infinitive or imperative

### H.3 Endnote Conventions

- Number endnotes sequentially through the entire document
- Place under a ### Notes heading at the end of the document
- Include full bibliographic information: Author, _Title_ (Place: Publisher, Year), page numbers
- Include URLs in angle brackets: <https://example.com>
- Note translations of non-English titles in square brackets: _Titulo en espanol_ [Title in English]
- Use "our translation" when translating from non-English sources

## Part III: Quick Reference Checklists

### Pre-Writing Checklist

- [ ] Identify the structural-systemic argument the dossier will make
- [ ] Determine the dominant narrative being challenged
- [ ] Identify the standpoint (whose perspective grounds the analysis: peasants, workers, liberation movements)
- [ ] Gather historical evidence, data, and testimony
- [ ] Identify collaborating organisations and movements
- [ ] Plan the dialectical movement from dominant narrative through structural analysis to political conclusion

### Self-Editing Checklist

- [ ] Every structural claim grounded in historical evidence or data
- [ ] Agency made visible: who exploits, who resists, who benefits, who suffers
- [ ] Passive/active voice ratio approximately 20-30%/70-80%
- [ ] Sentence length varies but averages 30--45 words
- [ ] Foreign-language terms italicised and glossed on first use
- [ ] Scare quotes used appropriately for adversary framings
- [ ] Endnotes complete with full bibliographic information
- [ ] British spelling conventions maintained throughout
- [ ] Conclusion affirms necessity and possibility of struggle
- [ ] No false equivalences between oppressor and oppressed

### Style Violations to Catch

- [ ] Simulated neutrality or false objectivity
- [ ] Euphemism for imperial violence
- [ ] "Developing/developed" country terminology
- [ ] "Stakeholder" language
- [ ] Corporate/consultancy jargon (ecosystem, landscape, leverage, unlock value)
- [ ] Hedging of political-structural claims that should be stated with conviction
- [ ] Passive voice hiding the agent of oppression
- [ ] Missing attribution for empirical data
- [ ] Uncritical use of "sustainable development" or "green" without qualification
- [ ] Absence of the standpoint of the oppressed in the conclusion
