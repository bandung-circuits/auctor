# Research Questions

## News Lead
https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c15x1dxp3wpo — US launches Section 301 probe into trading partners including the EU, China and India, after Supreme Court struck down Trump's tariffs. USTR Jamieson Greer announced the investigation could lead to new levies by summer. Countries targeted include China, EU, India, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia, Cambodia, Singapore, Indonesia, Bangladesh, Switzerland, and Norway. Canada was notably excluded. The probe follows the Supreme Court ruling that Trump's April 2025 tariffs were unlawful, after which Trump imposed a new 10% global tariff. US-China talks are set for Paris this weekend ahead of a potential Trump-Xi meeting in Beijing.

## Category A: Questions Inferred from Existing Research

[Questions that the initial summary and research already answer or address. These are listed for completeness — they do NOT need further research.]

### Legal and Constitutional Framework
1. What statutory authority does Section 301 provide, and how does it differ from IEEPA and Section 122 in scope, duration, and procedural requirements?
2. What did the Supreme Court rule in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, and on what constitutional and doctrinal bases (major questions doctrine, separation of powers)?
3. What is the administration's two-stage strategy for rebuilding the tariff regime after the IEEPA ruling (Section 122 bridge + Section 301 replacement)?
4. What is the historical trajectory of Section 301 usage — from its 1974 origins through 130 cases, the WTO era, and the Trump-era resurgence?
5. Does Section 301 have practical limits on tariff rates or duration? How does this compare to Section 122 (15% cap, 150 days)?

### Economic Data and Impact
6. What is the scale of the U.S. goods trade deficit ($1.24 trillion in 2025), and how is it distributed across the 16 targeted economies?
7. What is the empirical evidence on tariff pass-through to consumer prices (76-106%), and who bears the burden?
8. What has been the effect of tariffs on U.S. employment, GDP, and wages (Penn Wharton: -6% long-run GDP, -5% wages; 320,000 job losses from 2018-19 tariffs)?
9. How have trade deficits shifted among countries over the past five years — specifically the decline with China (-34%) and surges with Vietnam (+156%), Taiwan (+386%), and Thailand?
10. What is the fiscal scale of the invalidated IEEPA tariffs (~$130-168 billion in collected revenue potentially subject to refund)?

### The "Overcapacity" Framing
11. What specific sectors does the USTR allege suffer from "structural excess capacity" (aluminum, automobiles, batteries, semiconductors, solar modules, steel, electronics, etc.)?
12. How internally consistent is the USTR's own evidence for overcapacity (e.g., Singapore included despite a trade surplus with the U.S.; Ireland's pharmaceutical transfer pricing; Norway's seafood)?
13. What is China's official position on the "overcapacity" framing ("false proposition" used for "political manipulation")?

### Government Responses and Diplomatic Context
14. How has the EU responded (suspended trade deal ratification, accused U.S. of breach, placed Anti-Coercion Instrument on the table)?
15. What are Japan's and South Korea's official positions (seeking to preserve existing bilateral agreements, not be disadvantaged)?
16. How are ASEAN countries responding (divergent strategies: Malaysia maintaining deals, Thailand cautious, Vietnam leveraging diplomatic proximity)?
17. What is the context of the U.S.-China Paris talks and the upcoming Trump-Xi summit, and how does the Section 301 probe function as leverage?
18. Why is Canada excluded from the probe despite being the second-largest U.S. trading partner?

### Historical Parallels
19. How does the current 16-country Section 301 probe compare to the Smoot-Hawley tariff of 1930 (retaliation by 9 nations, protests from 35 governments, 66% trade collapse)?
20. What parallels exist between the current probe and the 1980s U.S.-Japan semiconductor trade war (100% tariffs, Section 301 usage, similar rhetoric)?
21. What happened when protectionist measures and subsidies were used against Japan in the 1980s (Sematech's failure, market competition succeeding instead)?

### Contradictions in U.S. Trade Policy
22. How does the revenue-versus-reshoring contradiction manifest (tariffs cannot simultaneously reduce imports and generate import-based revenue)?
23. How does targeting allies (EU, Japan, South Korea) with the same coercive tool used against adversaries risk fracturing the alliance architecture?
24. How does targeting Southeast Asian countries for "overcapacity" contradict the earlier U.S. push for supply chain diversification away from China?
25. What is the WTO's position on Section 301 tariffs (found inconsistent with GATT obligations in 2020), and why is the ruling unenforceable (dormant Appellate Body)?

### Expert and Industry Assessment
26. To what extent are the Section 301 investigations genuinely investigative versus procedurally confirmatory ("checking the boxes on process")?
27. What legal risks does the administration face if the combined use of multiple statutory authorities is seen as a "transparent sham" to reconstruct the IEEPA tariff wall?

### Research Group Q1: Chinese Industrial Overcapacity — Material Reality, Global South Impact, and Counter-Narratives
**Priority**: High
**Rationale**: Both experts independently raise this theme. Irvin Jim grounds it in the concrete experience of South African metalworkers facing competitive pressure from Chinese automotive exports. Xiong Jie frames it through the lens of leverage asymmetry. The initial summary treats "overcapacity" as primarily a U.S. political construction, but Jim's testimony complicates this by confirming real impacts on Global South industries. The commentary must engage honestly with this tension — neither accepting the U.S. framing nor dismissing the material reality of competitive pressure on Global South industrialization. This also addresses REF-MRX-003 Contradiction 2 (G7 ruling classes vs. Global South capitalist elites) and Contradiction 5 (popular classes vs. domestic elites in Global South).

1. What is the scale and trajectory of Chinese automotive exports to Africa, specifically South Africa? What has been the concrete impact on South African auto manufacturing employment and production volumes over the past 3-5 years?
2. Beyond South Africa, which other Global South countries and specific industrial sectors are experiencing competitive pressure from Chinese manufacturing exports? Are there documented cases from Latin America, other parts of Africa, or South/Southeast Asia where Chinese production has displaced local industry?
3. What concrete proposals or models exist for managing the tension between Chinese industrial expansion and Global South industrialization — e.g., technology transfer frameworks, joint ventures, local content requirements, or capacity-building agreements within BRICS or Belt and Road contexts?
4. Do other Global South labor movements or trade unions share NUMSA's "two-front" critique — opposing U.S. coercion while also pressing China on its production and export strategies? What is the state of Global South labor solidarity on this question?
5. How does China's official position on "overcapacity" as a "false proposition" engage (or fail to engage) with the concerns of Global South countries whose industries face competitive pressure from Chinese exports?

### Research Group Q2: Leverage Asymmetry and Global South Collective Response Mechanisms
**Priority**: High
**Rationale**: Xiong Jie's core insight is that China has rare earth leverage to negotiate with the U.S., but other Global South countries lack comparable bargaining chips and "will find it very difficult to resist." The initial research documents individual country responses but does not systematically analyze what leverage different countries actually possess, nor whether collective resistance is feasible. This addresses REF-MRX-003 Contradiction 3 (popular classes of Global South vs. U.S.-led imperial elite) and REF-MRX-004's emphasis on the need for multilateral resistance to unilateral hegemonic pressure.

1. What specific forms of leverage do different Global South countries targeted by the Section 301 probe possess — resource control (rare earths, critical minerals, agricultural commodities), market access, supply chain positioning, or diplomatic assets? Which countries have meaningful bargaining chips and which do not?
2. What is the current state of China's rare earth export controls and critical mineral restrictions (antimony, gallium, germanium)? How effective have these been as negotiating leverage in the U.S.-China trade talks, and what is their impact on U.S. industrial supply chains?
3. Does the simultaneous targeting of 16 economies create conditions for collective bargaining or coalition-building? Are there any signs of coordination among targeted countries — within BRICS, ASEAN, or other multilateral frameworks — to collectively respond to the Section 301 probe?
4. What historical precedents exist for multilateral resistance to unilateral U.S. trade pressure? How did targeted countries respond collectively (or fail to) during the 1980s Section 301 era or the 2018-2019 tariff escalation?
5. What countermeasures are realistically available to small and mid-sized economies (Cambodia, Bangladesh, Malaysia, Indonesia) facing Section 301 pressure? What is the difference in options between countries that signed bilateral trade agreements and those that did not?

### Research Group Q3: U.S. Crisis Externalization as Structural Pattern — Historical and Theoretical Dimensions
**Priority**: High
**Rationale**: Xiong Jie frames the Section 301 probe not as a discrete policy event but as an instance of a structural pattern — crisis externalization by a declining hegemon — that is becoming the "new normal." The initial research provides some historical parallels (Smoot-Hawley, 1980s Japan) but does not systematically examine crisis externalization as a recurring feature of hegemonic decline. This addresses REF-MRX-004's core thesis on hyper-imperialism and the declining economic hegemony driving intensified coercive action, and REF-MRX-003 Contradiction 1 (moribund imperialism vs. emerging socialism led by China).

1. What are the historical patterns of crisis externalization by declining hegemonic powers? How did the British Empire externalize its economic crises in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, and what parallels exist with current U.S. behavior?
2. Beyond tariffs, what other forms of U.S. crisis externalization are currently active — dollar weaponization, sanctions regimes, forced technology decoupling, security pressure linked to trade compliance? How do these interact with and reinforce the Section 301 probe?
3. What is the empirical basis for characterizing the U.S. as in structural economic decline — falling share of global GDP, deindustrialization metrics, infrastructure deficit, debt trajectory — and how does the tariff regime relate to these underlying structural weaknesses?
4. How does the $130-168 billion in IEEPA tariff revenue potentially subject to refund interact with the broader U.S. fiscal crisis? What is the current state of refund litigation, and how does fiscal pressure drive the urgency of new tariff instruments?
5. What are Global South scholars and institutions (beyond Xiong Jie) saying about the structural nature of U.S. trade aggression? Are there emerging analytical frameworks from the Global South that characterize the current period as a "new normal" of hegemonic coercion?

### Research Group Q4: Class Dimensions — Workers, Farmers, and Small Businesses in Targeted Countries
**Priority**: High
**Rationale**: The initial summary explicitly identifies "domestic class impact within targeted countries" as a key gap. The research documents government and elite responses but lacks coverage of how workers, farmers, and small businesses in the targeted economies experience U.S. tariff coercion. Irvin Jim's testimony partially fills this gap for South Africa, but the picture remains incomplete for the 16 directly targeted economies. This addresses REF-MRX-001 Layer 2 (productive forces and class relations), REF-MRX-003 Contradiction 5 (popular classes vs. domestic elites in Global South), and the editorial line's emphasis on centering material conditions over elite perspectives.

1. How are workers in export-dependent sectors of Southeast Asian countries (Vietnam's electronics assembly, Cambodia's garment industry, Bangladesh's textiles, Thailand's automotive) affected by tariff uncertainty? Are there reports of layoffs, wage pressure, or factory closures linked to the 2025-2026 tariff regime?
2. What are labor unions and workers' organizations in the EU, Japan, and South Korea saying about the Section 301 probe? How do European metalworkers, Japanese auto workers, and Korean electronics workers view the trade conflict?
3. How do tariffs and trade uncertainty affect small and medium enterprises (SMEs) in targeted countries, as distinct from large corporations? Are there specific data on SME exposure in sectors like textiles, agriculture, and light manufacturing?
4. What is the impact on agricultural producers in targeted economies — particularly in countries like India, Mexico, and Vietnam where rural livelihoods are tied to export agriculture?
5. How do the forced labor investigations (covering 60+ countries) interact with the overcapacity probe to create compounding risks for labor-intensive manufacturing exporters in the Global South, particularly Cambodia and Bangladesh?

### Research Group Q5: Environmental and Climate Dimensions of the Tariff Regime
**Priority**: Medium
**Rationale**: The initial summary explicitly identifies "environmental and climate dimensions" as a key coverage gap. The probe targets sectors directly relevant to the energy transition — solar modules, batteries, EVs, energy goods — but the research does not examine how tariffs affect climate policy and green industrialization. This addresses REF-MRX-003 Contradiction 8 (Western capitalism vs. planet and humanity) and REF-MRX-001's emphasis on the metabolic rift and ecological degradation as fundamental contradictions of capitalism.

1. How do tariffs on solar modules, batteries, and EVs affect the pace and cost of the energy transition — both in the United States and globally? What are the specific price impacts of existing tariffs on renewable energy equipment?
2. How does the "overcapacity" framing in green technology sectors (solar, batteries, EVs) interact with global climate commitments? Is Chinese "overcapacity" in solar production actually accelerating global decarbonization by driving down costs?
3. What are the climate policy implications of the Section 301 probe for targeted countries that have built green industrial capacity — particularly China (solar, batteries, EVs), India (solar modules), and Southeast Asian countries hosting green supply chains?
4. How are climate-focused organizations, environmental groups, and energy policy experts assessing the tension between tariff policy and climate goals? Are there specific analyses of the carbon cost of trade fragmentation?
5. Does the tariff regime's targeting of green technology sectors reveal a contradiction between the stated U.S. goal of energy independence and the practical effect of making clean energy more expensive for American consumers?

### Research Group Q6: Congressional Dynamics and the Future of U.S. Tariff Authority
**Priority**: Medium
**Rationale**: The initial summary identifies "Congressional dynamics" as a key gap. While the constitutional separation-of-powers question is well covered by the Supreme Court ruling analysis, the actual Congressional response — legislative proposals to reclaim tariff authority, political dynamics between executive and legislative branches, and the prospects for statutory reform — remains largely unexamined. This matters because the long-term trajectory of U.S. trade coercion depends on whether Congress reasserts its constitutional prerogative or continues to acquiesce to executive tariff unilateralism.

1. Are any members of Congress introducing legislation to reclaim tariff authority in the wake of the Supreme Court ruling? What specific bills have been proposed, and what are their prospects?
2. What is the political dynamic between Congressional Republicans and the Trump administration on trade? Is there any significant Congressional opposition to the Section 301 probe, or is the tariff consensus bipartisan?
3. What is the legal vulnerability of the Section 122 bridge tariff (10% global tariff requiring a "balance-of-payments emergency")? Are legal challenges already underway, and what are experts predicting?
4. How does the upcoming November 2026 midterm election affect the trade policy calculus? Could a shift in Congressional control alter the tariff trajectory?
5. What institutional reforms to U.S. trade authority are being discussed in legal and policy circles — e.g., requiring Congressional approval for tariffs above a threshold, sunset clauses, or mandatory economic impact assessments?
