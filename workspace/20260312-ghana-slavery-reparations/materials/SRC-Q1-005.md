Chasing copper and cobalt: China's mining operations in Peru and the DRC

By Lea Thome, Katherine Walsh, Jacqueline Zimmerman, and Brooke Escobar
February 27, 2025

To power the clean energy transition, China is securing minerals critical for producing advanced technologies. Through its Belt and Road Initiative, Beijing has ramped up related investments in resource-rich countries. While China already dominates midstream processing and downstream product development, AidData finds that its state-backed financing is strategically targeting upstream extraction in low- and middle-income countries.

Over the past two decades, Chinese state-owned lenders have bankrolled the acquisition, development, and operations of numerous copper and cobalt mines globally, with a sharp focus on Peru and the DRC. These two countries received $29.8 billion between 2000 and 2021, or more than half of all Chinese lending to developing countries for these minerals.

In total, Beijing has directed nearly $57 billion in loans and grants to 19 BRI participant countries for the extraction and processing of copper, nickel, lithium, cobalt, and rare earth elements.

Key findings:

1. Project companies: Rather than directing funding to host government agencies, Beijing's state-backed financiers have extended the majority of funding to project companies—special purpose vehicles (SPVs) or joint ventures (JVs)—that manage the financing, development, and operation of mining investments. SPVs and JVs shield parent companies from financial risks.

2. Chinese-owned mines: 97% ($16.1 billion) of China's state-directed transition mineral lending to Peru and 95% ($12.5 billion) to the DRC between 2000 and 2021 supported mining operations which are partially or wholly Chinese-owned.

3. Relationship banking: China directed multiple loans over time to each mining site. Mines targeted by Chinese state-directed serial lending received an average of 3.6 loans.

4. Acquisition lending: An aggressive program during the BRI period to lower barriers to entry for Chinese firms.

DRC Mining Operations:

Tenke Fungurume: The world's fifth-largest copper mine and second-largest cobalt mine. In 2016, CMOC acquired its first stake in the operating entity Tenke Fungurume Mining S.A. (TFM), which is jointly owned by TF Holdings (80%) and the DRC state-owned company Gecamines (20%). By 2017, CMOC had fully acquired TF Holdings, supported by $2.68 billion in credit from Chinese banks.

Kinsenda: World's second highest-grade active underground copper mine. Congolese state-owned company Sodimico and South African Metorex started operations in 2007. In 2011, Chinese state-owned Jinchuan Group acquired Metorex and its 77% stake. Between 2013-2022, $526 million in financing from China Development Bank.

Sicomines: At the center of a resource-for-infrastructure (RFI) deal. Chinese companies CREC, Sinohydro, and Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt hold a joint stake of 68% in Sicomines, with $7.8 billion in lending from Chinese creditors between 2008-2013.

Major operators in the DRC include Glencore, CMOC Group, Ivanhoe Mines, Eurasian Resources Group, and Zijin Mining Group. Chinese companies already control an estimated 80% of the Congolese cobalt market.

DRC's 2018 mining code requires companies to reserve 5% of their capital for Congolese employees, and set aside an additional 5% of shares for purchase by private Congolese individuals. Mining projects must cede a 10% free-carried interest to the state, with a further 5% payable upon renewal of a mining permit.

Environmental, social, and governance challenges: At the Sicomines site, environmental pollution from mining operations led to the destruction of agricultural infrastructure and local fields, with the local population fearing eviction and resettlement from their communities.
