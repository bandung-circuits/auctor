# Initial Event Summary

## News Lead
Ghana intends to propose a United Nations resolution recognising transatlantic slavery as the "gravest crime in the history of humankind" and calling for reparations, and expects broad support despite resistance in Europe. (Source: https://www.straitstimes.com/world/europe/ghana-to-submit-un-resolution-on-slavery-reparations-eyes-broad-support)

## Event Summary

On March 25, 2026 -- the International Day of Remembrance of the Victims of Slavery and the Transatlantic Slave Trade -- Ghana will formally present a resolution at the United Nations General Assembly titled "Declaration of the Trafficking in Enslaved Africans and Racialised Chattel Enslavement of Africans as the Gravest Crime Against Humanity" [M-F-002]. The resolution seeks formal global recognition that the transatlantic slave trade constitutes the gravest crime in human history, taking into account "its scale, duration, legalisation and enduring consequences" [M-A-001]. It also calls for reparatory justice, though Ghana's President John Dramani Mahama has emphasised that the immediate aim is recognition and truth-telling rather than financial compensation [M-G-010, M-A-013].

The initiative did not emerge in a vacuum. Ghana first announced it at the 80th Session of the UNGA in September 2025 [M-A-005]. A year of institutional preparation followed: the African Union designated 2025 the Year of Justice for Africans through Reparations, established a Coordination Team on Reparations, a Committee of Experts, and a Legal Reference Group [M-D-005]. At the 39th AU Assembly in Addis Ababa on February 14-15, 2026, African heads of state formally adopted the resolution, recognising slavery, deportation, and colonialism as crimes against humanity [M-A-007]. Forty of the AU's 55 member states have expressed support [M-A-009]. The AU has also declared 2026-2036 a Decade of Reparations [M-A-017].

Mahama acts in his capacity as AU Champion for Advancing the Cause of Justice and the Payment of Reparations [M-A-004]. He has built the resolution on three pillars: historical accuracy, legal defensibility, and continental and diaspora alignment [M-I-014]. The legal foundation rests on the argument that slavery constitutes a violation of jus cogens -- a peremptory norm of international law from which no derogation is permitted [M-A-016]. Extensive consultations have involved UNESCO, the Pan-African Lawyers Union, academic institutions, the Global Group of Experts on Reparations, and the AU's own expert committees [M-A-015]. The diaspora was engaged through the Ghana Diaspora Summit in December 2025 [M-D-008].

The coalition behind the resolution extends beyond Africa. CARICOM, the 15-member Caribbean Community, is expected to support it [M-A-003]. CARICOM's own Reparations Commission has been operational since 2013, working from a detailed Ten-Point Plan that encompasses formal apology, debt cancellation, technology transfer, and investment in health and education [M-D-004]. The Africa-Caribbean alignment was cemented through the 2025 Addis Ababa Declaration on Transcontinental Partnership in Pursuit of Reparatory Justice [M-E-004]. Diplomatic outreach has also targeted the Non-Aligned Movement, the Group of 77 and China, and even the European Union itself [M-I-004]. Ghana's Foreign Minister Samuel Okudzeto Ablakwa addressed the 26th Commonwealth Foreign Affairs Ministers Meeting in London, appealing for support ahead of the March 25 vote [M-A-018].

The quantitative dimensions of the crime are staggering. Over approximately 400 years, between 12.5 and 15 million Africans were forcibly transported across the Atlantic; roughly 1.8 million perished during the Middle Passage [M-B-003, M-C-002]. An additional 10 million were taken through the trans-Saharan, Red Sea, and Indian Ocean slave trades [M-B-004]. By 1800, Africa's population was estimated to be half of what it would have been without the slave trades [M-B-004]. In the Caribbean, over 10 million Africans were imported during 400 years of slavery; fewer than 2 million remained at emancipation [M-B-007]. The Brattle Group estimated total reparations at $100-131 trillion, covering 19 million people over four centuries representing 802 million years of life [M-B-001]. Country-by-country breakdowns apportion obligations across Britain ($24 trillion), Portugal ($20 trillion), Spain ($17 trillion), the United States ($26 trillion), and France ($9 trillion), among others [M-B-002].

The economic legacy is equally measurable. According to research by Nathan Nunn, if the slave trades had not occurred, 72% of the average income gap between Africa and the rest of the world would not exist; 99% of the gap between Africa and other developing countries would be eliminated [M-B-005]. Africa's mean per capita income stands at $1,834 compared to $8,809 globally and $4,868 for other developing countries [M-B-006]. Today, Africa remains a net creditor to the world, losing more than $500 billion annually through illicit financial flows, unfair trade practices, and debt servicing [M-E-015]. Ghana itself exported $9.58 billion in gold in 2024 but retained only 14% of the value [M-D-014].

European resistance is entrenched. Britain's Prime Minister Keir Starmer has declared the UK will not pay reparations "now or in the future" [M-F-008, M-J-003]. France recognised slavery as a crime against humanity in 2001 but President Macron ruled out reparations in 2017 [M-F-012]. Germany claimed in August 2025 that colonial atrocities did not violate international law at the time, making reparations "not applicable" -- a position starkly at odds with its own Holocaust reparations practice [M-C-016]. The Netherlands apologised but set up only a 200-million-euro fund [M-F-010]. Portugal's president suggested reparations but his government refused to act, and the far-right party Chega proposed charging him with treason [M-F-009, M-I-002]. This pattern of refusal extends back to the 2001 Durban Conference, where Britain, Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands collectively blocked a formal apology for slavery, choosing instead the language of "regret" to avoid legal implications [M-C-005, M-E-002].

The vote arithmetic at the UNGA presents a challenge. The AU's 55 members and CARICOM's 15 members together fall short of the 97 votes needed for a simple majority [M-G-006]. Outreach to the G77+China bloc, with roughly 70 additional voting members, will be decisive [M-G-006]. The resolution does not require Security Council approval, avoiding the veto power of permanent members. Ghana has also appealed to the 56-nation Commonwealth, with Commonwealth Secretary-General Shirley Botchwey -- herself a former Ghanaian foreign minister -- publicly backing reparations and signalling movement toward multilateral negotiations [M-F-017].

The reparations movement has deep institutional roots. The Organization of African Unity established a Group of Eminent Persons in the 1990s to seek reparations, producing the 1993 Abuja Proclamation declaring an "unprecedented moral debt" owed to African peoples [M-C-006]. The 2001 Durban Declaration recognised slavery as a crime against humanity but stopped short of calling for reparations. The 2023 Accra Reparation Conference produced the Global Reparation Fund concept [M-C-010]. What distinguishes the current moment is the convergence of continental institutional backing, Caribbean partnership, growing legal infrastructure, and a specific vehicle -- a UNGA resolution -- through which to press the claim. African and Caribbean nations are also seeking to establish a special UN reparations tribunal, citing precedent from previous tribunals created by resolution or Security Council action [M-A-003].

A significant historical irony pervades the entire debate: European governments found it feasible to compensate slave owners at emancipation -- Britain borrowed what would amount to roughly 2 billion euros in today's money to pay slave owners -- but never provided a penny to the enslaved themselves [M-B-017, M-E-005]. Haiti was forced to pay France approximately $617 million (in 2025 terms) for the "loss" of enslaved people, a debt that crippled the nation for over a century [M-C-018, M-E-005].

## Contradictions and Tensions

### Principal Contradiction

The principal contradiction is between the peripheral nations -- predominantly African and Caribbean -- demanding reparatory justice for the foundational crimes that enabled the accumulation of wealth in the core capitalist states, and those same core states' refusal to acknowledge or remedy this historical debt. This is not merely a diplomatic disagreement about history but a structural confrontation over the material basis of the existing global economic order. The wealth that financed the Industrial Revolution, built the insurance sector at Lloyd's of London [M-E-024], and underwrote European infrastructure was directly extracted through the slave trade. The contemporary global architecture -- from unequal trade terms to inflated borrowing costs for African nations [M-I-007] -- perpetuates the same extractive logic. The reparations demand therefore challenges not just a historical wrong but the continuing structures of accumulation and dispossession that define the relationship between formerly colonising and formerly colonised nations. European resistance is not merely about avoiding financial liability; it is about preserving the legitimacy of an international order built on the proceeds of this crime.

### Secondary Contradictions

1. **The contradiction between European rhetoric of partnership and the refusal of material accountability.** European states are simultaneously seeking to "relaunch" partnerships with Africa on "more equitable terms" to compete with China, Russia, and Turkey for African engagement, while categorically refusing reparations [M-H-008]. This performative partnership is untenable: as African nations increasingly recognise the hollowness of European offers that exclude any reckoning with the material basis of inequality, they turn toward alternative partners who carry no comparable colonial debt. The EU's approach is pushing African nations toward precisely the geopolitical realignments Europe seeks to prevent [M-J-004].

2. **The contradiction between the legal recognition of slavery as a crime and the refusal to apply the legal consequences.** France recognised slavery as a crime against humanity in 2001 [M-F-012]. The 2001 Durban Declaration affirmed it as a crime against humanity. Belgium's highest court in 2024 found the state responsible for colonial-era crimes against humanity [M-D-018]. Yet European governments simultaneously argue that because slavery was not "illegal at the time," no reparations obligation exists [M-D-016]. Germany's position is most revealing: it has paid $89 billion in Holocaust reparations since 1952 [M-J-017] while claiming in 2025 that colonial atrocities create no legal obligation because they occurred before modern international law [M-C-016]. This selective application of legal principle exposes a racial hierarchy in the application of international justice.

3. **Intra-African divisions between rhetorical commitment and operational capacity.** While 40 AU member states formally support the resolution, the September 2025 reparations summit in Addis Ababa saw low turnout of African heads of state [M-I-018]. Some member states privately prefer bilateral diplomacy over collective action [M-I-009]. The AU's capacity to translate declarations into binding action remains limited [M-I-009]. The historical precedent from Durban 2001, where African states "broke with [their] own diaspora" and sided with former enslavers in diluting the conference statement [M-I-010], is a warning that continental unity cannot be assumed.

4. **The tension between reparations as systemic transformation and reparations as redistribution within existing structures.** Academic debate identifies a core question: should reparations seek "large redistributive gains" within global capitalism, or should they aim for structural transformation that rejects the existing order entirely [M-E-012]? Ghana's resolution currently emphasises recognition and truth-telling rather than specific financial demands. But the UN Special Adviser's position -- that reparations must transform "the very rules of the game" including trade, finance, and governance systems [M-E-016] -- points to a far more radical horizon. This tension between pragmatic diplomacy and structural transformation remains unresolved within the movement itself.

### Narrative Contradictions

The dominant Western media framing of reparations as an impractical, backward-looking demand collides with the material evidence at multiple points.

First, the argument that "today's states should not be held responsible for historical wrongs" [M-A-002] is contradicted by the well-documented continuing harm: Africa loses $500 billion annually through structural economic extraction [M-E-015]; Ghana retains only 14% of its gold export value [M-D-014]; African nations inherited colonial debts that continue to constrain development [M-C-024]. The harm is not historical -- it is ongoing and structurally embedded.

Second, conservative British media (notably the Telegraph and Daily Mail) frame the issue through national victimhood, emphasising Britain's role in abolishing slavery and the Royal Navy's campaign against the slave trade [M-G-005]. This framing erases the prior centuries of British profit from enslaving Africans and the fact that Britain compensated slave owners -- not the enslaved -- at emancipation [M-B-017]. The abolitionist narrative is deployed as a shield against accountability for the preceding crime.

Third, the Telegraph highlights the omission of the "Arab slave trade" as evidence of selective targeting [M-G-004]. This deflection serves to dilute the specific accountability of European states by universalising culpability. It also obscures that the Arab slave trade, while significant (8-18 million people), operated on a fundamentally different economic model than the transatlantic chattel slavery system that created the modern capitalist world economy.

Fourth, the UnHerd "but for" argument -- that descendants of enslaved people are "better off" in America than they would be in Africa [M-G-012] -- attributes Africa's current conditions to inherent failure rather than to the extractive processes that produced that underdevelopment. This reasoning is circular: it uses the consequences of the crime to argue against remedying the crime.

Fifth, the framing of reparations as "politically impossible" in Western democracies [M-I-011] naturalises the resistance of electorates shaped by centuries of racial ideology. It presents the political will of populations that benefited from slavery as an immovable constraint, rather than as itself a product of the ideological structures that justified slavery.

## Questions for Expert Input

1. How does the Ghana resolution's focus on recognition and truth-telling rather than immediate financial compensation affect its prospects for passage at the UNGA, and does this incremental approach risk being co-opted by European states who will offer symbolic acknowledgement without material commitment -- as the Netherlands has done with its 200-million-euro fund?

2. What is the significance of the jus cogens legal argument in practical terms? If the UNGA resolution passes, does it create any binding legal obligation, or is its value primarily political and symbolic -- a foundation for future ICJ advisory opinions or treaty negotiations?

3. How should we understand the relationship between this resolution and the broader restructuring of global governance -- particularly the demand for African permanent membership on the UN Security Council [M-I-013] and the reform of international financial institutions? Are these parallel struggles or interdependent ones?

4. What role does China play in this dynamic? Ghana is consulting the G77+China bloc [M-I-004], and African states are increasingly turning to non-Western partners [M-J-004]. Does China's support for the resolution serve its own geopolitical interests in the competition with Western powers for African partnership, and how does this shape the resolution's meaning?

5. The historical precedent of Haiti -- where the formerly enslaved were forced to pay reparations to their former enslavers, crippling the nation economically for over a century [M-C-018] -- and the more recent example of France's support for the 2004 coup against Aristide after his government demanded reparations [M-E-011] suggest that reparations movements face not just diplomatic resistance but active destabilisation. What mechanisms exist to protect the integrity of the reparations process from such interventions?

6. How should we assess the internal coherence of the reparations movement given the tension between collective state-to-state claims (the AU/CARICOM model) and individual or community-level claims? The CARICOM Ten-Point Plan focuses on collective development, while some scholars argue for individual compensation. Does this unresolved question weaken the legal and political case?

7. Germany's double standard -- paying $89 billion in Holocaust reparations while claiming colonial atrocities create no obligation [M-C-016] -- appears to be the single most damaging piece of evidence against European legal arguments. How central should this comparison be to the analytical framing, and what are its limitations?

8. Given the low turnout of African heads of state at the September 2025 summit [M-I-018] and reports that some AU members prefer bilateral over collective action [M-I-009], how robust is the continental consensus behind this resolution, and what are the risks of fragmentation during UNGA negotiations?
