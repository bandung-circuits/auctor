# Historical Context — Relevant Excerpts

## [M-C-001] Scale and Duration of the Transatlantic Slave Trade
**Source**: SRC-C-002
**Relevance**: Establishes the historical magnitude of the transatlantic slave trade — the foundational facts that underpin the current reparations push.

> There is broad historical consensus that the transatlantic slave trade between the 15th and 19th centuries, which involved the kidnapping, enslavement and transport of millions of Africans to the Americas and the Caribbean, was one of the biggest tragedies in human history.
>
> It's estimated that over 400 years, 15 million men, women and children were forcibly transported. Other sources say 12 to 12.8 million were enslaved. About 10.7 million arrived in the Americas alive, while between 1.5 and 2 million died during the crossing, also known as the Middle Passage.

## [M-C-002] Middle Passage Attrition Data
**Source**: SRC-C-005
**Relevance**: Quantitative record of the human toll of the Middle Passage, drawn from SlaveVoyages.org — a key historical database used in reparations arguments.

> 12.5M Embarked
> 10.7M Survived Disembarkation
> 1.8M Perished at Sea
>
> Between 1501 and 1867, the "Middle Passage" claimed roughly 14.4% of all those forced into the Atlantic trade. The 1.8 million individuals did not survive the brutal conditions of the crossing.
>
> Source: SlaveVoyages.org / Equal Justice Initiative

## [M-C-003] The 1926 Slavery Convention as Early Legal Milestone
**Source**: SRC-C-005
**Relevance**: Traces the long history of international legal efforts to address slavery, starting nearly a century ago — showing how the current UN resolution is part of a long trajectory.

> International efforts to address slavery began as far back as the 1926 Slavery Convention. While that treaty aimed to stop future trade, it did not focus on punishing the historical crimes of the Transatlantic trade. For a long time, the legal world treated slavery as a tragic part of history rather than a criminal act requiring compensation. This lack of accountability allowed the wealth gap between colonizers and the colonized to grow unchecked.

## [M-C-004] The 2001 Durban Declaration — Landmark Recognition
**Source**: SRC-C-007
**Relevance**: The 2001 Durban Declaration is the single most important precedent for Ghana's current UN resolution — it first established that slavery was a "crime against humanity."

> In the Durban Declaration and Programme of Action (DDPA), states agreed that slavery and slave trade are a crime against humanity and should always have been so. They also recognized that colonialism has led to racism and racial discrimination, and that Africans and people of African descent, people of Asian descent and indigenous peoples continue to be victims of its consequences.

## [M-C-005] European Refusal to Apologize at Durban 2001
**Source**: SRC-C-010
**Relevance**: Documents the historical resistance from European powers at the 2001 Durban Conference — a pattern that directly parallels the current expected resistance to Ghana's UN resolution.

> There was an outright refusal by certain countries from the European Union to apologize for slavery. This push was led by Britain and joined by Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands who believed that formally apologizing for slavery and colonization (as requested by African, Caribbean, Latin American and Asian countries) would create legal implications that would force their countries to pay reparations. In order to appease these countries, the European Union released a draft statement noting its "regret" about the Trans-Atlantic slave trade. The statement read in part, "The European Union profoundly deplores the human suffering, individual and collective, caused by slavery and the slave trade. They are among the most dishonourable and abhorrent chapters in the history of humanity. The [EU] condemns these practices, in the past and present, and regrets the suffering they have caused[.]"

## [M-C-006] The 1993 Abuja Proclamation — Pan-African Roots
**Source**: SRC-C-005
**Relevance**: Traces the reparations movement back to the Organization of African Unity in the 1990s — showing this is not a new demand but one with decades of institutional history.

> The Pan-African movement also played a vital role during the 1990s. The Organization of African Unity formed the Group of Eminent Persons to push for recognition. This group helped create the 1993 Abuja Proclamation. That document declared that an "unprecedented moral debt" was owed to African people. It argued that this debt must be paid to ensure future global stability. These earlier movements laid the groundwork for President Mahama to take his current lead at the United Nations.

## [M-C-007] CARICOM's Historical Framing — European Colonial Guilt
**Source**: SRC-C-006
**Relevance**: CARICOM's Ten-Point Plan provides the most comprehensive historical framing of European responsibility — including the critical detail that slave owners were compensated at emancipation while the enslaved received nothing.

> The CARICOM Reparations Commission (CRC) asserts that European Governments:
> - Were owners and traders of enslaved Africans.
> - Instructed genocidal actions upon indigenous communities.
> - Created the legal, financial and fiscal policies necessary for the enslavement of Africans.
>
> Refused compensation to the enslaved with the ending of their enslavement.
>
> Compensated slave owners at emancipation for the loss of legal property rights in enslaved Africans.
>
> Imposed a further one hundred years of racial apartheid upon the emancipated.

## [M-C-008] CARICOM's Characterization of Slave Trade Scale
**Source**: SRC-C-006
**Relevance**: CARICOM's institutional characterization of the transatlantic slave trade as the largest forced migration in human history, underscoring its unparalleled nature.

> Over 10 million Africans were stolen from their homes and forcefully transported to the Caribbean as the enslaved chattel and property of Europeans. The transatlantic slave trade is the largest forced migration in human history and has no parallel in terms of man's inhumanity to man.

## [M-C-010] The 2023 Accra Reparation Conference
**Source**: SRC-C-009
**Relevance**: The 2023 Accra conference is the most important direct predecessor to the current UN resolution — it produced the Global Reparation Fund concept and the Accra Proclamation.

> Delegates at a reparations summit in Ghana agreed Thursday to establish a Global Reparation Fund to push for overdue compensation for millions of Africans enslaved centuries ago during the trans-Atlantic slave trade.
>
> "It is time for Africa — whose sons and daughters had their freedoms controlled and sold into slavery — to also receive reparations," said Ghana's President Nana Addo Akufo-Addo at the conference, attended by senior government officials from across Africa as well as the diaspora community.
>
> Slave reparations have become an issue the world "must confront and can no longer ignore," said Akufo-Addo, calling out the British and other European nations who enriched themselves during the slave trade while "enslaved Africans themselves did not receive a penny."

## [M-C-012] The AU's February 2026 Resolution — Immediate Precedent
**Source**: SRC-C-003
**Relevance**: The 39th AU Assembly resolution in February 2026 is the direct precursor to the UN resolution, providing it with continental backing.

> African heads of state adopted a historic resolution recognizing slavery, deportation, and colonialism as crimes against humanity, marking a major step in Africa's long-running struggle for historical justice and reparatory redress.
>
> The decision was taken at the conclusion of two days of high-level deliberations during the Thirty-Ninth Ordinary Session of the Assembly of the African Union, held at the African Union Headquarters in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia from February 14-15. Leaders considered a comprehensive study examining the legal and moral implications of describing colonization as a crime against humanity and certain acts committed during the era of slavery, deportation, and colonial rule as genocide against African peoples.

## [M-C-015] International Legal Precedent — Factory at Chorzow
**Source**: SRC-C-010
**Relevance**: Documents the international legal precedent for reparations, showing the principle predates the current slavery reparations movement.

> Reparations have long been seen as the best way to atone for international wrongs. Factory at Chorzów made this international precedent. In that case the Permanent Court of International Justice ("PCIJ") stated, "reparation must, as far as possible wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act[.]" Additionally, the ILC's Articles on State Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts state in Article 31, "The responsible State is under an obligation to make full reparation for the injury caused by the internationally wrongful act." With regards to slavery and colonization, this is complemented by Article 14 which notes, "The breach of an international obligation by an act of a State having a continuing character extends over the entire period during which the act continues and remains not in conformity with the international obligation."

## [M-C-016] Analogous Reparations Precedents — Germany's Double Standard
**Source**: SRC-C-008
**Relevance**: Documents the critical historical precedent where Germany applies different standards to Nazi-era reparations versus colonial-era reparations — a key analogous case.

> In 2021, the German government formally apologized for what it recognized as the colonial-era genocide in Namibia. The apology, however, was framed as part of a "historical and moral responsibility," not a legal responsibility. Germany maintains that the affected Nama and Ovaherero peoples do not have a right to reparations.
>
> On August 14, 2025, the German government claimed that, as German colonial atrocities, at the time, did not violate international law, the concept of reparation was "not applicable in the context of Germany's colonial past." This starkly differs to its position paving the way for reparations for Nazi-era atrocities.
>
> Germany appears to apply a double standard by selectively invoking the principle, which risks setting a dangerous precedent that other governments may follow.

## [M-C-017] Belgian Court 2024 — Positive Reparations Precedent
**Source**: SRC-C-008
**Relevance**: A recent positive legal precedent showing that courts can find colonial-era atrocities give rise to present-day reparation claims.

> A positive example of recognition of a right to reparation to address harm inflicted by colonial atrocities is a 2024 Belgian high court decision in which the court found that the ongoing impacts on victims of crimes against humanity ensuing from Belgian racist colonial policies gave rise to victims' claims for compensation by today's Belgian government.

## [M-C-018] Haiti — Historical Analogy of Colonial Debt Trap

## [M-C-019] Jus Cogens Legal Theory — No Statute of Limitations
**Source**: SRC-C-005
**Relevance**: Explains the key legal theory underlying the resolution — that as a Jus Cogens violation, the slave trade has no statute of limitations for reparations claims.

> In international law, Jus Cogens refers to fundamental principles that are universally binding. These norms protect the most basic human values, such as the prohibition of genocide and slavery. They are so high in authority that no nation can ignore them. Any treaty that goes against these norms is considered void. Ghana is pushing to have the historical slave trade recognized under this highest tier of law.
>
> If the slave trade is accepted as a Jus Cogens violation, it changes everything. Standard laws usually have a "statute of limitations." This means that after a certain amount of time, a crime cannot be prosecuted. However, crimes against humanity do not have such limits. By elevating the trade to this status, legal scholars argue that the obligation for restitution becomes permanent.

## [M-C-021] African Complicity — Historical Nuance
**Source**: SRC-C-002
**Relevance**: Provides historical context on African participation in the slave trade — a factor often raised to complicate reparations claims but contextualized here.

> It's often pointed out that some African elites benefited from the slave trade. Historians estimate a majority of people captured in Africa were enslaved by other Africans and then sold on to European slavers.
>
> Forms of slavery already existed in African societies well before the transatlantic slave trade. Yet while the transatlantic slave trade was not the only form of slavery, Asante says this trade had a disproportionately large economic, political and societal impacts along Africa's Atlantic coast.

## [M-C-023] Slave Owner Compensation vs. Enslaved Receiving Nothing
**Source**: SRC-C-008
**Relevance**: A historically ironic precedent — governments found it feasible to compensate slave owners but never the enslaved themselves, undermining the argument that reparations are "impossible."

> Governments that are called upon for reparations often treat these calls as impossible or unrealistic. It is noteworthy that similar assessments did not apply to decisions to compensate former owners of enslaved people — not the enslaved people themselves — based on allegations that they had "lost" property after the abolition of chattel enslavement.

## [M-C-024] Colonial Debt as Continuing Harm
**Source**: SRC-C-010
**Relevance**: Academic analysis linking colonial-era debts to ongoing economic destabilization — supporting the "continuing character" argument under international law.

> Many former colonies were forced to pay colonial debts when they gained their independence. These payments served to further destabilize and undermine growth and development and have left these countries playing catch-up to this day. Economist Jeffrey Sachs has suggested that Africa's US $200 billion debt be cancelled or be obstructed. He stated, "Africa should say: 'Thank you very much, but we need this money to meet the needs of children who are dying, right now, so, we will put the debt-servicing payments into urgent social investment in health, education, drinking water, the control of AIDS, and other needs.'"

## [M-C-025] The Demographic Devastation of Africa
**Source**: SRC-C-005
**Relevance**: Historical analysis of how the slave trade's demographic impact left Africa vulnerable to the subsequent "Scramble for Africa" — showing cascading historical consequences.

> The continent lost millions of its healthiest people during the peak of the trade. This loss made African nations more vulnerable during the "Scramble for Africa" in the late 19th century. During this time, European powers divided the continent without any African representation. This history shows how the slave trade and colonialism worked together to strip the continent of its wealth and agency.
>
> The trade primarily targeted people between the ages of 16 and 35, which decimated the continent's workforce.
