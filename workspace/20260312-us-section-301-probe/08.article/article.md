# The Overcapacity Doctrine: How a Declining Hegemon Turns the World's Factories into Trade Violations

## The Singapore Paradox: When a Trade Surplus Becomes "Overcapacity"

On March 11, 2026, the United States Trade Representative announced Section 301 investigations into sixteen economies for "structural excess capacity and production in manufacturing sectors."[^1] Among the targets: Singapore, a city-state that runs a $3.5 billion trade surplus with the United States.[^2] The USTR cited Singapore's "excess global capacity in semiconductors" as justification. Norway was targeted for its seafood and fuel exports. Ireland's pharmaceutical sector, dominated by transfer pricing arrangements of U.S. multinationals, was treated as evidence of European Union "overcapacity."[^3]

The list spans economies from Bangladesh, which runs a $7 billion deficit, to the EU, at $219 billion. It includes countries that built their manufacturing sectors at Washington's urging during the first wave of "de-risking" from China, only to find that the supply chains they absorbed are now classified as threats. Vietnam's electronics exports to the U.S. grew by 35% in 2025; its trade deficit with the U.S. surged 156% over five years. Taiwan's deficit jumped 386% over the same period. Meanwhile, the U.S. deficit with China fell 34%.[^4] The tariffs rerouted trade. They did not reduce it. And the countries that caught the diverted flows are now being investigated for doing precisely what Washington wanted.

At the World Economic Forum in Davos, Trump explained why Switzerland's tariff was set at 39%. The Swiss president, he said, "kept saying the same thing over and over" and "rubbed me the wrong way." He decided the rate after hanging up the phone.[^5] The anecdote is revealing. The USTR cannot define "overcapacity" consistently because the designation does not describe a market condition. It designates a political target.

## A Familiar Playbook: From Reagan's Semiconductors to Trump's Section 301

"The health and vitality of the U.S. semiconductor industry are essential to America's future competitiveness." That was Ronald Reagan in 1987, announcing 100% tariffs on Japanese semiconductors. A year earlier, Washington had forced Tokyo into an agreement limiting chip sales to the American market. Unsatisfied, Reagan escalated.[^6]

The outcome is instructive. The tariffs and a government-backed consortium called Sematech failed to revive American chipmaking. Later reviews found that federal subsidies caused member companies to cut their own R&D spending. American semiconductor manufacturing boomed during the 1990s anyway. "U.S. firms prospered because of their ability to innovate and compete effectively, not because of such techno-nationalist or protectionist measures," concluded James Schoff of the Carnegie Endowment.[^6]

The same logic now produces the same instruments. In September 2024, the Biden administration raised Section 301 tariffs on Chinese imports to 25-100% across critical minerals, electric vehicles, and solar cells.[^7] The tool is bipartisan. The framing is interchangeable. Swap "Japan" for "China," "semiconductors" for "electric vehicles," and the policy architecture remains identical.

What changed on February 20, 2026, was the legal ground beneath the tariff regime. The Supreme Court, in a 6-3 ruling, struck down Trump's IEEPA tariffs. Chief Justice Roberts wrote: "The Framers did not vest any part of the taxing power in the Executive Branch."[^8] Nineteen days later, the Section 301 probe launched. Treasury Secretary Bessent told CNBC his "strong belief" was that tariff rates would return to pre-ruling levels "within five months."[^9] The probe is not investigative. It is reconstructive.

Even within China, the picture is more complex than either side's public framing allows. At the Central Economic Work Conference in December 2023, senior policymakers identified overcapacity as one of the country's foremost economic challenges. Premier Li Qiang referenced the problem twice in his 2024 Government Work Report. State media, meanwhile, insisted no overcapacity problem existed.[^10] The internal contradiction matters: it reveals an economic challenge that the U.S. framing converts into a political weapon.

## The Double Squeeze: Workers from Dhaka to Durban Caught Between Two Forces

In Bangladesh, garment orders froze. Gap, Walmart, and Levi's demanded that suppliers absorb tariff costs in their entirety.[^11] Most Bangladeshi garment workers are women, already on below-subsistence wages, with no savings. When orders pause, the economic hit shows up fast: overtime cuts, delayed wages, temporary layoffs, then at home, in food choices and school fees.[^12] During the COVID-19 shock, Bangladesh saw a 13% surge in child marriage. The tariff disruption threatens to repeat that pattern across a sector that employs four million people and coincided with decades of social progress: fertility fell from six children per woman in the 1980s to around two by 2020, and the average female marriage age rose from 14.6 to 17 years.[^13]

Bangladesh secured a 19% tariff deal. The terms: $15 billion in purchases of U.S. energy products, 14 Boeing aircraft, U.S. military equipment, and a commitment to "limit purchases from certain countries."[^14] The concessions require no commentary. They speak for themselves as the price a country with a $7 billion trade surplus pays to protect its garment workers' jobs.

In Durban, the squeeze comes from two directions at once. Thirteen automotive component plants closed over 24 months, wiping out 4,500 skilled jobs. Goodyear shut its Eastern Cape tyre plant; ZF Lifete, which supplies steering wheels, seatbelts, and airbags, stands on the verge of closure.[^15] Chinese and Indian vehicle imports surged from under 1% of the South African market in 2018 to 26% in 2025.[^16] Simultaneously, South African auto exports to the United States fell 73% in the first quarter of 2025, then 80% and 85% in the months that followed.[^17]

Irvin Jim, general secretary of the National Union of Metalworkers of South Africa, occupies a position that neither Washington nor Beijing can assimilate. "We are not against BRICS. We are not against China," he told reporters. "But if Chinese companies are investing here, then they must do proper investment, embark in proper manufacturing, actually create jobs in the value chain."[^18] He opposes U.S. sanctions. He also acknowledges that "China's capacity overflow does indeed put great pressure on African industries such as South Africa's automobile manufacturing."[^19] His position is grounded on the factory floor, where metalworkers face a two-front war.

In Thailand, between one and two million workers in export industries face uncertainty across seven sectors, with several million more in supply chains.[^20] India's $5.62 billion in agricultural exports to the U.S. sits at risk, directly linked to the livelihoods of farmers in a sector providing 46% of employment.[^21] At GM Korea, workers voted to strike with 88.2% support, the highest in the union's history, after the company used Trump tariffs as justification to reject wage demands despite $990 million in operating profits.[^22]

## The Structural Compulsion: Why a Declining Hegemon Externalizes Its Crises

The Supreme Court ruling created an immediate fiscal emergency. Penn Wharton projects that reversing the IEEPA tariffs will generate up to $175 billion in refunds. IEEPA tariffs represented approximately half of all customs duties, collecting roughly $500 million per day before the ruling.[^23] The March 11 Section 301 announcement emerged from this fiscal hole. It was not an objective assessment of global trade but an urgent need to rebuild a revenue stream.

The fiscal crisis is a symptom. The U.S. share of global GDP has halved since 1950, falling from 27% to approximately 15%. National debt stands at $36.2 trillion, or 122% of GDP, growing by roughly $1 trillion every three months.[^24] The American Society of Civil Engineers awards the country's infrastructure an overall grade of C, with nine of eighteen categories earning D or D+, and a $9.1 trillion investment gap. Some 12.7 million households lack broadband.[^25]

The trade deficit hit a record $1.24 trillion in 2025, despite the highest effective tariff rate since 1947.[^26] Empirical research from Yale's Budget Lab finds that 76-106% of tariff costs pass through to domestic consumers. Foreign producers are not absorbing the cost.[^27] Penn Wharton projects the tariff regime reduces long-run GDP by 6% and wages by 5%, losses "twice as large as a revenue-equivalent corporate tax increase from 21% to 36%."[^28] The coercion harms the hegemon's own population.

Princeton historian Harold James has written that "no other country's recent past offers a clearer preview of America's potential future" than Britain's loss of global preeminence.[^29] The parallel is structural, not incidental. Giovanni Arrighi's framework of systemic cycles of accumulation identifies the current period as "a lasting interregnum," defined by stagnation, debt, and inequality.[^30] Malaysian scholar Christopher Choong Weng Wai, writing from the Khazanah Research Institute, frames the current disorder in blunter terms: "What is at stake is essentially US hegemony. This hegemonic order is ultimately behind the current disorder."[^31]

The dollar itself has become a weapon. Washington froze approximately $300 billion in Russian central bank reserves. The dollar's share of global foreign exchange reserves has fallen below 47%, down from over 70% in the early 2000s. Eighty-five percent of central bankers express concern about the implications.[^32] The weaponization has paradoxically accelerated the very de-dollarization that undermines future U.S. dominance.

Xiong Jie, a scholar at the Global South Academic Forum, provides the structural synthesis: "The US is constantly trying to externalize its own crises during its decline phase, and this situation is likely to become the new normal for the foreseeable future, posing a difficult challenge for the Global South."[^33] The March 11 probe is not a temporary policy aberration. It is a structural feature of the current period.

## The Leverage Gap: Why China Can Negotiate and ASEAN Cannot

China controls 70% of global rare earth mining, 90% of processing, and 93% of magnet manufacturing.[^34] Its grip on gallium is tighter still: approximately 98% of global production capacity, with no near-term alternatives and new mining projects five to seven years away.[^35] The Paris trade talks on March 15 addressed rare earth export controls as the central friction point. Washington wanted full restoration of supply. Beijing viewed the controls as its most potent bargaining chip.[^36]

For the remaining fifteen economies under investigation, the calculus is grimmer. ASEAN's initial solidarity lasted weeks. Malaysia's Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim announced on April 13 that ASEAN would act as a bloc on U.S. tariffs. But Vietnam had already preemptively cut tariffs on March 26, two and a half weeks before Anwar's announcement. Cambodia followed the same day. Indonesia announced tariff cuts on April 8; Thailand followed hours later. Vietnam cut a bilateral deal on July 3, Indonesia on July 16, the Philippines on July 24.[^37] Each successive defection destroyed whatever collective bargaining power remained. Trump's tactic was simple: drop a massive tariff bomb, pull back, and let targeted countries bid against each other for relief. Each bilateral deal made the next country's position weaker.[^38]

Indonesia carved out a middle path. It put rare earth access on the table as a sweetener, and its tariff rate fell from 32% to 19%.[^39] The lesson is straightforward: leverage comes from controlling something the hegemon needs, not from concessions. Bangladesh's deal terms confirm the inverse. Military equipment purchases, Boeing aircraft, and energy contracts are the price of bilateral negotiation without leverage.

The coordination failure extended beyond Southeast Asia. Mercosur did not present a united front. South Asian institutions collapsed as Pakistan and India clashed. Even BRICS, at its Rio de Janeiro summit in July 2025, failed to produce a coordinated response.[^40]

The EU, by contrast, negotiated as a bloc and remained on a lower tariff rung while offering few concrete concessions. It activated its Anti-Coercion Instrument for the first time in history.[^41] The contrast is instructive. ASEAN's combined GDP exceeds that of most individual EU member states. Its failure was not economic but institutional: the absence of binding mechanisms that could prevent individual members from defecting.

Xiong Jie identifies the asymmetry with precision: "China should have the ability to use rare earths as leverage to negotiate with the US; but other Global South countries will find it very difficult to resist American pressure."[^33] The structural question remains open. The EU model shows collective bargaining works, but replicating it requires institutional trust that bilateral coercion is actively destroying.

## Green Capacity Under Fire: Tariffs Against the Climate

Measured against Net Zero 2050, current Chinese solar production capacity is not excessive. It is what the energy transition requires. Bruegel's analysis finds that China can already produce enough renewable products for the world's most aggressive decarbonization scenario. The "overcapacity" exists because global demand remains far below what net-zero demands.[^42] Elizabeth Thurbon, professor at the University of New South Wales, puts it plainly: "There is no overcapacity in cheap renewables until we reach 100% renewables globally."[^43]

China's 60%-plus solar cost reductions made the goals of the Paris Agreement achievable.[^44] The March 11 probe targets this production capacity. India offers the concrete case. Under its production-linked incentive scheme, solar manufacturing capacity grew 13-fold since 2020. Then the U.S. imposed a 126% preliminary countervailing duty, effectively shutting India out of the American market.[^45] Vietnam and Cambodia, which host the supply chains producing over 54 GW of the solar panels the U.S. imports annually, face tariffs of 46% and 49%.[^46]

Every successive U.S. tariff on solar since 2012 has failed to revive domestic manufacturing. Imports grew despite tariffs while domestic production lost market share.[^46] The current round is uniquely counterproductive. It arrives paired not with manufacturing support but with its opposite: IRA and BIL funding has been frozen, and IRA tax incentives face repeal. In the first quarter of 2025, $7.7 billion in clean manufacturing projects were canceled, up from $1.8 billion in all of 2024.[^46]

BloombergNEF projects a bifurcated outcome. As tariff walls rise around the United States, China redirects cheap solar and battery exports to countries the World Bank classifies as lower or middle income. Decarbonization may accelerate outside the U.S. while it stalls within.[^47] The probe targets precisely the industrial capacity the planet needs.

## The Hegemon's Own Courts Say No: The Fragile Legal Architecture

"The Framers did not vest any part of the taxing power in the Executive Branch."[^8] The February 20 Supreme Court ruling did more than invalidate IEEPA tariffs. It exposed the constitutional strain of an executive branch that has outgrown its delegated trade powers.

The administration's scramble across statutory authorities reveals the improvisation. Section 122, designed for a fixed-rate currency system that ended in 1976, now anchors a 10% global tariff. Milton Friedman once noted that floating exchange rates "completely eliminate the balance of payments problem."[^48] The statute addresses an economic condition that has not existed for fifty years. A coalition of 24 state attorneys general sued on March 5, arguing the actual balance-of-payments deficit is 0.2% of GDP, "essentially a rounding error."[^48] The solicitor general's own arguments in the IEEPA case undermine the Section 122 claim: having told the Supreme Court the president could not use Section 122 as an IEEPA replacement, the administration proceeded to do exactly that.[^49]

Alan Wolff, former senior fellow at the Peterson Institute for International Economics, warned that combining multiple statutory authorities "in a transparent sham to largely restore the tariff wall" would "almost certainly be seen as illegitimate."[^50] The footwear industry is less diplomatic. Matt Priest, head of the Footwear Distributors and Retailers of America, described the investigations as "just checking the boxes on process." He added: "I don't think there's going to be a whole lot of Sherlock Holmes activity."[^51]

The bipartisan Trade Review Act of 2025, modeled on the War Powers Resolution, has 13 Senate cosponsors including Mitch McConnell. The bill would require the president to notify Congress within 48 hours of imposing tariffs, with an automatic 60-day sunset unless Congress votes to approve.[^52] In the House, Representative Don Bacon revealed the gap between public votes and private sentiment: "I was in the cloakroom and I heard people saying 'I don't like tariffs but I voted no.'" He estimated the number opposed would have been "five or six times higher" without presidential pressure.[^53] Representative Jeff Hurd framed the constitutional principle starkly: "What do we say to the future president who declares a climate change emergency, and says that there is unlimited unilateral authority to implement tariffs?"[^53] Trump withdrew his endorsement of Hurd in retaliation. The coercive apparatus is contested from within, and those who contest it pay a political price.

For Global South countries, the legal instability cuts both ways. An improvised, arbitrary coercive regime is more dangerous than a rules-based one. But an apparatus built on contested statutory foundations, facing lawsuits from 24 state attorneys general and bipartisan Congressional opposition, may also be more vulnerable to challenge than it appears.

## The New Normal: What Solidarity Can the Global South Build?

If crisis externalization is the new normal, what forms of collective action can survive the divide-and-conquer tactics that shattered ASEAN's unity in weeks?

The evidence offers fragments of an answer. South-South trade has doubled from $2.3 trillion in 2007 to $5.6 trillion in 2023.[^54] Brazil's three-level strategy, combining presidential diplomacy, a legislative reciprocity framework, and corporate lobbying, provides a partial model for larger economies. The Brazilian Congress approved the Economic Reciprocity Law in April 2025, creating a legal mechanism for calibrated retaliation.[^55] Turkey and Thailand have developed conditional EV subsidy frameworks that channel Chinese investment into genuine local production: imports must be matched by locally manufactured vehicles.[^56]

Irvin Jim's demand returns here as more than testimony. "Proper investment, proper manufacturing, actual jobs in the value chain"[^18] offers the principled basis for a position that is neither capitulation to U.S. coercion nor uncritical acceptance of Chinese industrial expansion. It is a position grounded in the material interests of workers on the factory floor, and it refuses to be assimilated into the narratives of either great power.

The unresolved tension is institutional. The EU model demonstrates that collective bargaining works when a bloc holds together. ASEAN demonstrated that it collapses when individual states defect. ASEAN governments have begun accelerating FTA negotiations with the EU and Mercosur, diversifying away from dependence on the U.S. market.[^41] Vietnam has explored an FTA with the Mercosur bloc in South America. These are the early moves of an adjustment that may outlast any single administration in Washington.

The sixteen economies targeted on March 11 share a common condition: their productive capacity has been designated a violation by a power whose own courts have said its tariff architecture is unlawful. Whether these countries can build institutions of solidarity resilient enough to resist the divide-and-conquer tactics, or whether each will be picked off in bilateral negotiations that extract Boeing purchases and military equipment contracts, is the defining challenge of the period that Xiong Jie has named the new normal.
