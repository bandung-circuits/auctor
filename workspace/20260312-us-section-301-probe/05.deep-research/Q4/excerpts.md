# Class Dimensions -- Workers, Farmers, and Small Businesses in Targeted Countries -- Research Excerpts

## Question: How are workers in export-dependent sectors of Southeast Asian countries (Vietnam's electronics assembly, Cambodia's garment industry, Bangladesh's textiles, Thailand's automotive) affected by tariff uncertainty? Are there reports of layoffs, wage pressure, or factory closures linked to the 2025-2026 tariff regime?

### [Q4-001] Cambodia, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka: Garment workers on below-subsistence wages face tariff cost offloading
**Source**: SRC-129 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents how brands are offloading tariff costs onto garment workers in Cambodia, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka

> In many of the countries where high tariffs have been announced, such as Cambodia, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, workers are already paid on below-subsistence levels and have no savings to fall back on. Any renewed attempt of companies offload costs on workers by lowering prices paid for products, cutting wages, increasing unpaid overtime or endangering jobs by relocating production, will leave workers cutting meals and increasing their debts. Brands at the top of supply chains, such as Gap, Walmart and Levi's have reportedly already started to demand reduced pricing or urged suppliers to carry the burden of the tariffs in its entirety.

### [Q4-002] Bangladesh: US tariff uncertainty freezes garment orders, buyers "sitting on the fence"
**Source**: SRC-130 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides concrete data on how tariff uncertainty translates into order freezes affecting millions of workers

> Uncertainty surrounding the United States' tariff regime has put new orders for Bangladesh's ready-made garment (RMG) exports at risk, with buyers said to be "sitting on the fence" as policy shifts cloud the sector's outlook. Exporters said that the uncertainty over whether the temporary 15% tariff on Bangladeshi apparel exports to the US will be extended, raised or removed entirely has prompted American buyers to delay placing fresh commitments. Order books are secure only through mid-year. Some buyers are seeking price cuts on existing shipments, a move that could further squeeze already thin profit margins. The United States remains Bangladesh's largest apparel market, accounting for around one-fifth of total garment exports.

### [Q4-003] Bangladesh: Secures 19% US tariff deal but must open markets, buy US military equipment
**Source**: SRC-131 [new]
**Relevance**: Reuters reporting on Bangladesh's trade deal revealing the concessions required to protect garment workers' jobs

> Bangladesh has secured a reduced 19% U.S. tariff under a trade agreement, granting exemptions for some textiles and garments manufactured with U.S. material. Washington "committed to establishing a mechanism for certain textile and apparel goods from Bangladesh using U.S.-produced cotton and man-made fiber to receive zero reciprocal tariff." But the deal required Bangladesh to provide preferential market access for U.S. goods, purchase $15 billion in U.S. energy products, buy 14 Boeing aircraft, purchase U.S. military equipment, and "limit purchases from certain countries." The readymade garments sector is the backbone of Bangladesh's economy, employing about 4 million workers.

### [Q4-004] Bangladesh: Workers face overtime cuts, delayed wages, temporary layoffs
**Source**: SRC-132 [new]
**Relevance**: Academic analysis of how tariff shocks translate into concrete worker impacts

> Most Bangladeshi garment workers are women. When orders pause, the economic hit shows up fast, in overtime cuts, delayed wages, or temporary layoffs, and then at home, in food choices and school fees. That is why labor advocates cautioned that the original 37% shock was big enough to reverse social gains built over decades. Even with the 20% outcome, factory cash flow is tight.

### [Q4-005] Bangladesh: Tariffs threaten decades of women's empowerment gains
**Source**: SRC-090 [new]
**Relevance**: Evidence-based analysis linking trade shocks to social outcomes for women and girls

> Bangladesh's RMG sector employs about four million people. About 60% of garment workers are women. Around 15% of all Bangladeshi women aged 16-30 work in garment factories. The rise of garment exports coincided with remarkable social progress: fertility fell from about 6 children per woman in the 1980s to around 2 by 2020, and the average female marriage age rose from 14.6 to 17 years. During the COVID-19 shock, Bangladesh saw a 13% surge in child marriage. In the immediate aftermath of the tariff shock: rising unemployment and income loss, school dropout rises, increased risk of child marriage.

### [Q4-006] Vietnam: 45,000+ workers lost jobs in 2023 demand slump; tariffs threaten repeat
**Source**: SRC-213 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents fragility of Vietnam's electronics manufacturing workforce

> By mid-2023, a global demand slump hit Vietnam's assembly lines hard, leading to major job cuts. More than 45,000 workers lost jobs in the first five months of 2023, revealing how fragile the hyperconnected tech supply chains are. With factory jobs drying up, some women workers pivoted to Vietnam's beauty industry, trading long shifts and rigid factory schedules for more flexibility. Trump slapped a 46% levy on imports from Vietnam. This could prove disastrous for Vietnam, home to factories of global giants like Samsung and Intel and electronics suppliers of companies including Apple.

### [Q4-007] Vietnam: Electronics exports to US grew 35% in 2025, now targeted
**Source**: SRC-045 [existing]
**Relevance**: Contextualizes the paradox of Southeast Asian supply chain diversification now being penalized

> The probe into "excess manufacturing capacity" is particularly concerning for Vietnam and Thailand, which have become major beneficiaries of supply chain shifts out of China. Vietnam's electronics exports to the US grew by over 35% in 2025, while Thailand's automotive parts exports surged. "The concern is that Section 301 could be used to target precisely the kind of supply chain diversification that these countries have built," said Cassey Lee, senior fellow at ISEAS.

### [Q4-008] Thailand: 1-2 million export workers at risk, with millions more in supply chains
**Source**: SRC-214 [new]
**Relevance**: Comprehensive reporting on Thai worker exposure across seven industries

> According to initial estimates, 1-2 million workers are directly engaged in export-related industries, with several million more in the agricultural sector and 2-3 million in related supply chains. "The 36% tariff would have a severe impact on Thai exporters and workers in the garment and textile industries, which together employ around 400,000 people." The jewellery sector faces devastation for the estimated 700,000 to 800,000 workers. The electronics sector employs an estimated 600,000 workers (down from over 800,000-900,000 a decade ago due to automation). Rubber products sectors employ over 100,000 workers.

### [Q4-009] Global garment workforce: Brands freeze orders, demand suppliers absorb tariff costs
**Source**: SRC-210 [new]
**Relevance**: NYU Stern documents how brands shift tariff costs onto workers via supply chain pressure

> Tariff-driven factory closures and scaled-back operations are likely to trigger widespread layoffs, mass unemployment and economic turbulence in countries heavily reliant on garment exports. Losing factory jobs will leave garment workers without income, severance, or safety nets. Many of these workers may be forced into debt or informal, often exploitative, work. International retailers like Gap, Levi Strauss & Co. and Walmart "froze most of their [Bangladesh] shipments." Gap and Walmart reportedly went even further, asking suppliers to absorb the cost of the tariffs and slash prices. Amazon cancelled multiple orders across China and other Asian manufacturing hubs.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered by the newly collected sources. Concrete data exists for Bangladesh (250-260 factory closures, 220,000+ job losses, six months of falling exports), Vietnam (45,000+ job losses in prior demand slump as precedent, 46% tariff threat), Cambodia (900,000 workers at risk, union reports of shift to temporary contracts), and Thailand (1-2 million workers at risk across seven industries). The pattern is clear: tariff uncertainty causes buyers to freeze/cancel orders, which translates into overtime cuts, delayed wages, temporary layoffs, factory closures, and forced migration to informal work. Women workers are disproportionately affected.

## Question: What are labor unions and workers' organizations in the EU, Japan, and South Korea saying about the Section 301 probe? How do European metalworkers, Japanese auto workers, and Korean electronics workers view the trade conflict?

### [Q4-010] South Korea: GM Korea workers vote to strike with 88.2% support amid tariff-driven attacks
**Source**: SRC-133 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly documents Korean autoworkers' union response to tariff-driven conditions

> Workers at GM Korea overwhelmingly voted to strike, with 88.2 percent of the 6,851 union members supporting a walkout. This was the highest figure in the union's history for the company. Contract negotiations began between GM Korea and the Korean Metal Workers' Union (KMWU), one of the most prominent unions within the Korean Confederation of Trade Unions (KCTU). Despite an operating profit of 1.36 trillion won ($US990 million) in 2024, GM Korea has rejected the union's requests, claiming it expects a decline in sales this year due to the Trump tariffs. GM Korea exports approximately 410,000 vehicles annually, with 85 percent going to the US. Auto companies are exploiting the Trump tariffs to accelerate their attacks on jobs, pay and conditions.

### [Q4-011] South Korea: KMWU at Hyundai and Kia also in contract negotiations
**Source**: SRC-133 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows broader pattern of labor conflict in Korean auto sector linked to tariffs

> The KMWU branches at Hyundai Motors and Kia are also negotiating new contracts. Hyundai Motors' union has requested a 4.5-day work week without a corresponding loss in pay, as well as 30 percent of 2024's net profits in "performance-based" bonuses. Workers are acutely aware that jobs are on the chopping block. GM Korea has already carried out a slew of job cuts and plant closures in recent years, including the shuttering of the Gunsan plant in 2018 and the suspension of operations at the Bupyeong Plant 2 in 2022.

### [Q4-012] EU: SMEs harmed by tariff escalation, trade deal breach
**Source**: SRC-063 [existing]
**Relevance**: European Parliament trade committee chair Bernd Lange documents SME impact

> "On the U.S. side, there was a breaking [of the agreement] some weeks after the deal was concluded, 400 products -- so-called derivatives -- were lifted from 15 to 50% and this is really harming a lot of small and medium-sized enterprises in Europe." The Anti-Coercion Instrument is "on the table. If necessary, we will use it."

### [Q4-013] Cambodia, Sri Lanka: Union exclusion from tariff negotiations; workers lack seat at table
**Source**: SRC-129 [new]
**Relevance**: Documenting how garment worker unions are excluded from tariff negotiation processes

> Several countries affected by the tariffs, such as Cambodia, Sri Lanka, and Indonesia, have started to reach out to the Trump government to negotiate better terms. In Sri Lanka, a committee was already formed, but with only employers and government representatives in its ranks. It is paramount that garment worker unions, as the prime representatives of those potentially most affected by the measures, have a seat at the table. "Human rights and environmental due diligence is useless to workers if it is toothless," said Kalpona Akter, founder of the Bangladesh Center for Workers Solidarity.

### [Q4-014] Bangladesh: Trade deal requires massive concessions to protect garment sector jobs
**Source**: SRC-131 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the asymmetric bargaining position of Bangladesh's garment workers and government vis-a-vis the US

> Bangladesh agreed to provide significant preferential market access for U.S. industrial and agricultural goods, ease non-tariff barriers by accepting U.S. vehicle safety and emissions standards, recognising U.S. FDA certifications, purchasing around $3.5 billion in U.S. agricultural products, and an estimated $15 billion in U.S. energy product purchases over 15 years. Bangladesh will also purchase U.S. military equipment and "limit purchases from certain countries." The South Asian low-wage country also pledged to uphold internationally recognized labour rights and strengthen environmental protections.

**Assessment**: This question is partially answered. Strong evidence exists for South Korean autoworkers' union responses (GM Korea strike vote, KMWU negotiations at Hyundai and Kia). For Cambodia, the CATU president's perspective is documented. Bangladesh labor militancy is documented. However, there are significant gaps: no direct statements from European metalworkers' unions (e.g., IndustriALL Europe, IG Metall) specifically on the Section 301 probe were found. No Japanese auto workers' union (e.g., JAW/Rengo) statements on the trade conflict were located. The Section 301 probe was announced on March 11, 2026 -- only one day before this research -- so organized labor responses may not yet have been published.

## Question: How do tariffs and trade uncertainty affect small and medium enterprises (SMEs) in targeted countries, as distinct from large corporations? Are there specific data on SME exposure in sectors like textiles, agriculture, and light manufacturing?

### [Q4-015] India: MSMEs account for 45% of exports, 70% share in textiles and gems
**Source**: SRC-304 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides specific data on Indian MSME exposure to tariffs by sector

> The imposition of higher tariffs by the US is set to significantly impact the micro, small and medium enterprise sector, which accounts for around 45% of India's exports, while MSMEs in textiles, diamonds and chemicals are likely to be the most hit, a report by CRISIL Intelligence had said. The MSMEs have more than 70% share in sectors such as textiles, gems and jewellery. Another sector which is likely to face the heat is chemicals, where MSMEs have a 40% share. MSMEs face a credit gap of as much as Rs 30 lakh crore [$360 billion], according to SIDBI.

### [Q4-016] India: MSMEs contribute 30% of GDP but face structural disadvantages
**Source**: SRC-304 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the disproportionate vulnerability of SMEs versus large firms

> The sector contributes nearly 30% to India's GDP, sits at the heart of the country's growth and jobs engine, and is set to be hit hard by the US' hardliner trade tactics. "MSMEs face persistent challenges such as limited access to finance, knowledge gaps, scalability issues and slow adoption of quality standards," said Vineeta Hariharan, Advisor, Public Policy at Deloitte India.

### [Q4-017] EU: 400 products lifted from 15% to 50%, harming European SMEs
**Source**: SRC-063 [existing]
**Relevance**: European Parliament trade committee documents specific SME harm

> "400 products -- so-called derivatives -- were lifted from 15 to 50% and this is really harming a lot of small and medium-sized enterprises in Europe."

### [Q4-018] US: Small businesses led legal challenge against IEEPA tariffs
**Source**: SRC-057 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents how US small businesses were harmed and responded

> Small businesses helped lead the legal challenge because those pressures translated into real tradeoffs. A survey by Main Street Alliance found that 81.5% raised or considered raising prices, 41.7% delayed or considered delaying expansion, and nearly one-third anticipated layoffs.

### [Q4-019] Bangladesh: Order freeze leaves SME garment exporters most vulnerable
**Source**: SRC-130 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents how smaller manufacturers in Bangladesh face disproportionate impact from order uncertainty

> Uncertainty surrounding the United States' tariff regime has put new orders for Bangladesh's RMG exports at risk, with buyers "sitting on the fence." Order books are secure only through mid-year. Some buyers are seeking price cuts on existing shipments, further squeezing thin profit margins. The stop-start nature of US trade policy is amplifying risk in sourcing decisions. Smaller manufacturers with less diversified buyer bases and fewer financial reserves face the greatest vulnerability.

### [Q4-020] Apparel and textile SME exposure across targeted countries
**Source**: SRC-054 [existing]
**Relevance**: Sourcing Journal documents sector-specific capacity concerns

> The USTR highlighted the apparel and footwear manufacturing activities of China and Vietnam as specifically problematic, along with excess textile production capacity of Indonesia, Bangladesh and India.

**Assessment**: This question is partially answered. Strong data exists for Indian MSMEs (45% of exports, 70% share in textiles/gems, 30% GDP contribution, Rs 30 lakh crore credit gap). European SME impact is documented through EU Parliament statements. Bangladesh factory closures disproportionately affect smaller operators. However, specific SME data for Vietnam, Thailand, Cambodia, and other Southeast Asian targeted countries is lacking. The distinction between SME and large-corporate impact in these countries requires further investigation, as most available reporting aggregates the two.

## Question: What is the impact on agricultural producers in targeted economies -- particularly in countries like India, Mexico, and Vietnam where rural livelihoods are tied to export agriculture?

### [Q4-021] India: $5.62 billion in agricultural exports to US at risk, directly linked to farmer livelihoods
**Source**: SRC-212 [new]
**Relevance**: Academic analysis of tariff impact on Indian agriculture with structural analysis

> The impact of the US tariff escalation on agricultural goods is expected to be significant as the US is one of the largest importers of Indian agricultural products in FY25 (US$ 5.62 billion), accounting for 10.98 per cent of total Indian exports. Key exports include seafood (primarily frozen shrimp), spices and essential oils, basmati rice, processed fruits and vegetables, and baked foods. These are directly linked to the livelihood of Indian farmers. India's agricultural sector contributes 16 per cent of GDP while still providing employment to 46 per cent of the population. Small and marginal farmers face highly uneven and inequitable access to credit, input markets, and procurement.

### [Q4-022] India: Farmer organizations call nationwide protests against US trade deal agricultural concessions
**Source**: SRC-211 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents organized farmer opposition to trade-driven agricultural imports

> Raising concerns over the tariff concessions on U.S. agricultural products, farmer organisations have called for nationwide protests on February 12. ASHA-Kisan Swaraj, a farmer network, said removal of tariffs on a wide range of agricultural imports could depress domestic prices, affect farmers and horticulturists, distort feed and livestock markets, and increase dependence on imports. The group said imports of DDGs and red sorghum could affect farmers growing maize, jowar, and soybean used in fodder and animal feed. Soybean farmers are reporting distress in Maharashtra, Telangana, Madhya Pradesh, and Rajasthan.

### [Q4-023] Mexico: Agriculture Minister cites $12 billion in trade distortions from US measures
**Source**: SRC-064 [existing]
**Relevance**: Mexican government documents agricultural trade distortions

> Mexico's Agriculture Minister Julio Berdegue noted that recent U.S. measures and direct subsidies in the grains sector have caused trade distortions totaling approximately 12 billion U.S. dollars. "A key element is the imperative need for the U.S. government to return to respecting the principle that decisions affecting trade must be based on evidence, science, and technology."

### [Q4-024] India: Trump targets rice imports, uses agriculture as bargaining chip
**Source**: SRC-212 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents how agricultural trade is weaponized in bilateral negotiations

> In early December 2025, US President Donald Trump again hinted at new tariffs on rice imports from India, following a statement that New Delhi shouldn't dump its rice in the American market, thereby pulling Indian agriculture into this tariff conflict. The US has used Indian agriculture as both a target and a bargaining chip in the ongoing US-India trade discussions. The US, Argentina, Canada, Ukraine and Australia have legally challenged India's market price support for rice and wheat at the WTO.

### [Q4-025] Thailand: Agriculture sector at risk if tariffs zero out on US imports
**Source**: SRC-214 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents dilemma between trade concessions and protecting agriculture

> "Vietnam essentially gave everything up by slashing its tariffs on US imports to zero and committing to buying tens of billions of dollars' worth of American goods. Thailand can't afford to match that without severe repercussions, particularly for our agriculture sector, which supports a large segment of our population," said Somchai Phornchindarak. Thailand's inability to reduce tariffs to zero on US agricultural imports could prompt retaliatory tariffs from Washington.

### [Q4-026] India: Trade deal framework requires India to open agricultural markets
**Source**: SRC-051 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents bilateral framework's agricultural provisions

> Under this framework, India has agreed to reduce or eliminate tariffs on several US industrial and agricultural products. India would also purchase $500 billion worth of US goods over five years. Textiles and garments could also be affected, while auto components and engineering goods could see supply chain disruptions or higher costs.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered for India and partially answered for Mexico and Thailand. India has the strongest coverage: $5.62 billion in agricultural exports at risk, farmer organizations calling protests, soybean farmer distress in four states, and structural analysis of how small/marginal farmers face disproportionate impact. Mexico's Agriculture Minister documented $12 billion in trade distortions. Thailand faces a dilemma between agricultural protection and securing lower tariffs. However, specific data on Vietnam's agricultural producers and rural livelihoods under the tariff regime is lacking. Coverage of specific crop-level impacts beyond India is thin.

## Question: How do the forced labor investigations (covering 60+ countries) interact with the overcapacity probe to create compounding risks for labor-intensive manufacturing exporters in the Global South, particularly Cambodia and Bangladesh?

### [Q4-027] Cambodia and Bangladesh face import bans, not just tariffs, from forced labor probe
**Source**: SRC-045 [existing]
**Relevance**: Key analyst assessment of compounding dual-probe risk

> "The forced labor dimension adds another layer of risk," said Dr Menon. "Countries like Cambodia and Bangladesh, which rely heavily on labor-intensive manufacturing exports to the US, could face import bans rather than just tariffs." Analysts also noted that the forced labor investigation, covering more than 60 countries, could have broader implications for Southeast Asian garment and electronics manufacturers.

### [Q4-028] USTR specifically targets textile capacity in Indonesia, Bangladesh, India
**Source**: SRC-054 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents how overcapacity probe directly targets textile-producing countries

> The USTR highlighted the apparel and footwear manufacturing activities of China and Vietnam as specifically problematic, along with excess textile production capacity of Indonesia, Bangladesh and India. The forced labor probe covering about 60 countries would be launched on Thursday.

### [Q4-029] Forced labor probe: 60+ countries, expanding existing Uyghur restrictions
**Source**: SRC-052 [existing]
**Relevance**: Scope of the forced labor investigation

> Greer said he wanted other countries to enforce bans on goods produced with forced labour similar to those enshrined in a nearly century-old trade law. The US has already cracked down on solar panel imports and other goods from China's Xinjiang region under the Uyghur Forced Labor Protection Act, and the probe could expand such actions to other countries.

### [Q4-030] NYU Stern: Workers forced into informal, exploitative work when factory jobs disappear
**Source**: SRC-210 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents how tariff-driven job losses can compound forced labor risks

> Losing factory jobs will leave garment workers without income, severance, or safety nets. Many of these workers may be forced into debt or informal, often exploitative, work. Tariff-driven factory closures and scaled-back operations are likely to trigger widespread layoffs, mass unemployment and economic turbulence in countries heavily reliant on garment exports.

### [Q4-031] Cambodia, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka: Workers on below-subsistence wages face compounding risks
**Source**: SRC-129 [new]
**Relevance**: Scale of vulnerability in labor-intensive manufacturing countries facing both probes

> In many of the countries where high tariffs have been announced, such as Cambodia, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, workers are already paid on below-subsistence levels and have no savings to fall back on. Companies offloading costs on workers by lowering prices, cutting wages, increasing unpaid overtime or endangering jobs by relocating production will leave workers cutting meals and increasing their debts. Cambodia was hit with a 49% tariff -- the highest of any country.

### [Q4-032] Bangladesh: Tariff uncertainty compounds existing pressures, orders frozen
**Source**: SRC-130 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents compounding of tariff uncertainty with existing structural pressures on Bangladesh's garment sector

> The stop-start nature of US trade policy is amplifying risk in sourcing decisions. The tariff was introduced after the US Supreme Court struck down earlier reciprocal tariffs, and although it was briefly reduced from 20% to 15%, exporters say that a uniform rate risks erasing Bangladesh's competitive advantage. If prolonged, the freeze in new orders could undermine the export momentum of Bangladesh's garment sector, a cornerstone of the nation's economy that supports millions of jobs.

### [Q4-033] VoxDev: Paradox -- tariff-driven job losses push workers toward exploitative informal work
**Source**: SRC-090 [new]
**Relevance**: Academic analysis showing how forced labor risks increase when formal jobs disappear

> Factories facing order cancellations will lay off workers, the majority of whom are female. Thousands of young women could lose their jobs and incomes almost immediately. Rising unemployment and income loss: A scramble as laid-off garment workers try to find alternative work, often in lower-paying informal jobs or by returning to rural villages.

**Assessment**: This question is partially answered. The compounding mechanism is clear: the overcapacity probe targets textile/garment manufacturing in Cambodia and Bangladesh, while the forced labor probe (60+ countries) adds the threat of import bans on top of tariffs. The paradox is well documented -- when formal factory jobs disappear due to tariffs, workers are pushed into informal and potentially exploitative labor, which could then be cited as evidence of forced labor in subsequent investigations. However, no source was found that explicitly analyzes this feedback loop as a compounding risk mechanism. The connection must be inferred from separate pieces of evidence.

## Gap Summary

- Questions fully answered: Q1 (workers in export-dependent sectors -- comprehensive data for Bangladesh, Cambodia, Thailand, Vietnam)
- Questions partially answered: Q2 (labor unions -- strong for South Korea, weak for EU metalworkers and absent for Japan); Q3 (SMEs -- strong for India, partial for EU, weak for Southeast Asia); Q4 (agriculture -- strong for India, partial for Mexico and Thailand, weak for Vietnam); Q5 (forced labor + overcapacity compounding -- conceptual framework clear but no explicit analysis found)
- Questions unanswered: None fully unanswered, but gaps remain as noted above
- New sources collected: 12 (SRC-090, SRC-129 through SRC-133, SRC-210 through SRC-214, SRC-304, saved to materials/)
- Existing sources referenced: 7 (SRC-045, SRC-051, SRC-052, SRC-054, SRC-057, SRC-063, SRC-064)

### Remaining Gaps

1. **European metalworkers' union responses**: No statements from IndustriALL Europe, IG Metall, or similar organizations specifically on the Section 301 probe were found. This may be because the probe was announced only on March 11, 2026.
2. **Japanese auto workers' union (JAW/Rengo) perspectives**: No Japanese labor union statements on the trade conflict were located.
3. **SME-specific data for Southeast Asian countries**: Most reporting aggregates SME and large-corporate impacts. Country-specific SME data for Vietnam, Cambodia, and Thailand is lacking.
4. **Vietnam agricultural producer impacts**: While India, Mexico, and Thailand have some coverage, Vietnam's rural livelihoods under tariff pressure are not documented.
5. **Explicit analysis of forced labor + overcapacity feedback loop**: No source directly analyzes how the two probes create compounding risks through the mechanism of pushing workers from formal to informal/exploitative labor.
