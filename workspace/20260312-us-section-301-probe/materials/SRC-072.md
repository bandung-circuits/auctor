The New Containment Doctrine: How the United States Is Using Trade to Stop Digital Regulation
Commentary by Duc Minh Nguyet (Moon) Nguyen and Philip Luck
Published March 9, 2026

In January 2026, the Department of State imposed visa restrictions on five European officials involved in drafting the EU Digital Markets Act (DMA) and Digital Services Act (DSA), an escalation in the Trump administration's campaign against digital regulation. At the same time, the administration has sought to embed anti-digital regulation clauses into bilateral trade agreements with other partners, including Malaysia, Indonesia, Cambodia, Argentina, Guatemala, and El Salvador.

The strategy marks a shift. Unable to roll back EU regulations at their source, the administration is trying to contain their spread. Using tariff threats as leverage, the United States is transforming what has been a domestic regulatory choice into a negotiable trade concession.

The United States has embedded anti-regulation clauses into bilateral trade agreements and frameworks with at least nine countries across Latin America, Southeast Asia, and Europe. These agreements state that countries shall not impose digital services taxes that discriminate against U.S. companies, and shall facilitate digital trade by refraining from measures that discriminate against U.S. digital services.

For smaller economies, the calculation is straightforward: accept restrictions on future digital regulation in exchange for immediate tariff relief. Indonesia, with a GDP of around $1.4 trillion in 2024, is the largest economy to accept such clauses so far.

The Trump administration has viewed trade policy as "a critical component to national security" and frames digital regulation as a competitive battleground, showing less concern for sustaining multilateral consensus. Even with the loss of IEEPA as a policy tool, digital trade objectives can still be pursued through credible threats of tariffs or investigations under Section 301 or Section 232.

Brazil is testing the limits — the USTR launched a Section 301 investigation into Brazil's trading practices, including digital trade. Despite this pressure, Brazil has not withdrawn the proposal. Tariff threats lose potency when regulatory stakes are high and domestic political constituencies support intervention.

South Korea's proposed Online Platform Fairness Act has surfaced repeatedly as a friction point in U.S.-South Korea trade conversations. The dispute strains the alliance: South Korea is a critical security partner, yet even narrowly tailored digital regulation triggers U.S. pressure.

The European Union itself remains out of reach. The European Commission made clear the DMA and DSA are not part of trade negotiations.

The approach treats digital regulation as purely a trade issue. Such a framing works for small economies seeking tariff relief, but breaks down when countries pursue what they see as legitimate governance goals. What the strategy does accomplish is establishing a precedent: digital regulation as a negotiable trade concession.
