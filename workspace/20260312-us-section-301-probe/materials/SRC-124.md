China's surplus and Western rules are strangling Asian industry. By Nischal Dhungel, Published 9 Mar 2026.

Developing nations are being squeezed between a Western-imposed regulatory ceiling and a China-induced industrial floor.

China recorded a record $1.2 trillion trade surplus in 2025, redirecting much of that export surge towards the Asia-Pacific to bypass Western tariffs. The influx of "good-enough" green technology leaves nations such as Nepal, Bangladesh, and Indonesia with a bruising choice.

The driver is China's domestic "consumption trap". With internal demand weak, Beijing has doubled down on supply-side dominance.

The result is "involution" — hyper-competitive, profitless production where Chinese zombie firms slash prices to capture foreign markets. This predatory pricing is dismantling the midstream manufacturing sectors — textiles, light machinery, components — that traditionally serve as the "industrial ladder" for developing states. In Nepal, Chinese brands captured more than 75% of the EV market in 2025.

As China pushes from the bottom, the West imposes a new "regulatory ceiling" from the top. Recent ART deals with Bangladesh and Indonesia prioritise market access over developmental assistance, requiring adoption of Western IP and technical standards.

Many ASEAN nations are experiencing "premature de-industrialisation." Indonesia's manufacturing contribution has stagnated at roughly 18-19% in 2026. Thailand's manufacturing production index risks contracting for the fourth consecutive year.

Without genuine technology transfer and support for local midstream manufacturing, the permanent hollowing of the developing world's economic future will become the defining legacy of this new trade war.

The solution lies in a "production-first" trade model — moving beyond simple tariff reciprocity into shared industrial capacity.
