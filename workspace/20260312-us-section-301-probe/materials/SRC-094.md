American Hegemony at a Critical Juncture, Lessons from History's Great Powers

By Ketan Patel, Christian Hansmeyer, Nandan Desai, Aditya Ajit
ARTICLE | December 8, 2025

Abstract: This study investigates whether the United States is experiencing a phase of hegemonic decline, signaling the end of its era as the world's leading great power. The quantitative model projects potential turning points in American global primacy between 2032 and 2067. The qualitative analysis reveals that the United States currently exhibits several key recurring features of great-power decline, including internal division, weakened alliances, reduced moral authority, and challenges from rising powers with competing rule-setting ambitions.

Key findings:

1. A hegemon has three essential attributes: exceptional material and political capacity giving the ability to invent the rules of the game; the will to lead the order and enforce the rules; and indisputable primacy of social capital leading to consented followership.

2. "It is self-evident that no state can maintain a pre-eminent position indefinitely." Systemic instability tends to increase when a rising challenger approaches parity with an established power.

3. Common markers of decline identified across 29 historical great powers include: internal polarization, military overreach, economic inequality/imbalance, loss of alliance cohesion, institutional decay, and diminished capacity to set international norms.

4. "America today undoubtedly stands alongside the Roman, British, Mongol and Persian Empires, among others, as one of history's great powers."

5. America's own rise as a global power (and Great Britain's fall) occurred during a period of greater disruptions and crises, amid two World Wars, where American intervention saw it emerge as a savior.

6. American structural power derives from: the US Dollar's role as global reserve currency, control of the SWIFT payment system, bilateral and multilateral security guarantees, and US-based tech giants controlling key layers of global digital infrastructure.

7. Cyclical shifts in material capabilities and the escalating costs of maintaining global leadership contribute to the weakening of hegemonic dominance, while changes in the capacity to maintain ideological legitimacy and institutional authority are further factors.

8. When subordinate states no longer accept or align with the dominant norms and institutions promoted by the hegemon, its ability to shape international behavior diminishes.

9. The study uses mixed-methods: quantitative modeling of historical power lifecycles of previous hegemons and qualitative analysis of recurring historical markers of decline such as strategic overreach, domestic polarization, and eroding global legitimacy.
