## Kwesi Pratt Jr.

*Ghanaian journalist, Pan-Africanist, author of* Reparations: History, Struggle, Politics and Law

From the fifteenth to the nineteenth century, over twelve million Africans were captured, shipped across the Atlantic, and nearly two million perished en route. The Atlantic became a graveyard without headstones. Those who survived built the modern world with their blood and sweat. Their labour produced the sugar that sweetened European teacups, the cotton that fed European textile mills, and the tobacco that filled European pockets. The ports of Liverpool, the docks of Bristol, the stock exchanges of London and New York — all were forged from the flesh and blood of African men, women and children. More outrageously, when slavery was finally abolished, it was not the enslaved who received reparations, but the slave owners. This vast fortune never flowed back to Africa. It was used to build roads, schools, and hospitals in the imperial motherlands. Europe's development was built precisely upon Africa's underdevelopment — not by accident, but through deliberate, systematic plunder. The poverty, technological stagnation, and institutional fragility Africa faces today are not deficiencies of its own capacity, but the direct consequence of this history. Reparations are not merely about money; they concern dignity, memory, language, sovereignty, and the transformation of a global system built upon the plunder of natural resources, the trampling of human rights, and environmental destruction. No sum can atone for the enslavers' crimes, yet the pursuit of justice is not charitable handouts but a historical obligation. Every African government should formulate a reparations policy; every parliament should debate it; every school should teach it.

## Fred M'Membe

*President of the Socialist Party of Zambia*

We gather in Accra today, following in the footsteps of Kenneth Kaunda, Julius Nyerere, Jomo Kenyatta, Patrice Lumumba and other pioneers of national liberation struggles, to renew a conviction born in the early twentieth century that refuses to die — Pan-Africanism. Six hundred years of slavery, colonialism and neocolonialism, six centuries of ideologies and institutions designed solely to perpetuate plunder, exploitation and the humiliation of our peoples, have taught us clearly who our friends are and who our adversaries. Consider Zambia: after independence, we had direction. I was among the first cohort of children to enrol in school after independence — free uniforms, books, pencils, and meals provided. The children of leaders shared dormitories with those of workers and farmers. Then neoliberalism arrived — privatisation, deregulation, dismantling the state — and poverty rates soared to 82 per cent in some regions, briefly making Zambia the world's fifth most hungry nation. This was no natural disaster, but the continuation of colonial legacies through structural adjustment policies. After the colonisers departed, economic control never truly passed to the people. New colonies led by puppets could not deliver the unity sought by Pan-Africanism. Without genuine sovereignty, there can be no genuine development. Without the resolve to break imperialist economic shackles, African unity remains an empty slogan.

## Irvin Jim

*General Secretary, National Union of Metalworkers of South Africa (NUMSA)*

To comprehend the plight of South Africa's working class today, one must first grasp the concept of "colonialism of a special type". South Africa was not a colony in the traditional sense, having achieved formal independence in 1910. Yet this independence was handed to a white minority, not the masses. All the evils of colonialism — the oppression of the non-white majority — were not only perpetuated but intensified. The oppressors and the oppressed coexist on the same land — this is its "special" nature. The economic foundations of this system persist to this day: a minority monopolises mines, banks, financial institutions, the vast majority of farms, and key industries. This monopoly is the cumulative result of over a century of colonial plunder, oppression, and exploitation. Post-1994 South Africa has not genuinely embarked upon a democratic path, for this particular form of colonialism remains operational, perpetuating the triple crisis of extreme inequality, mass unemployment, and poverty. Nationalisation is not an abstract slogan for us, but a means to redistribute the nation's wealth to all its people, to achieve socialised ownership and de-racialisation. We demand nationalisation not only of mining but of all economic commanding heights. The labour dispatch system constitutes a modern form of pernicious contract labour and slavery that must be abolished. Without dismantling the economic foundations of white South African racism and colonial capitalism, it is impossible to build a genuinely unified, non-racial democratic society.

## John Ross

*British economist, Senior Research Fellow at Chongyang Institute for Financial Studies, Renmin University of China*

To grasp the enduring impact of slavery and colonialism on Africa, one need only consider a fundamental economic fact: among major global economies, there exists a correlation coefficient of 0.91 between the ratio of net fixed capital formation to GDP and economic growth rates. For an economy to grow, it must continuously accumulate capital — infrastructure, factories, equipment, technological systems. From the fifteenth to the nineteenth centuries, the slave trade constituted the largest reverse transfer of capital in human history. Over twelve million people were forcibly removed from Africa — meaning Africa's most fundamental productive factor was systematically drained. The immense wealth generated by this labour in the Americas was entirely converted into European capital accumulation. A substantial portion of the fixed capital stock in Liverpool, Bristol, and the City of London was accumulated through the value of stolen African labour. Meanwhile, Africa's own capital stock not only failed to grow but drastically shrank due to population loss and social disintegration. The colonial era perpetuated this logic: the railways and ports built by colonisers served to transport raw materials to Europe, not to develop indigenous African manufacturing. At independence, Africa's fixed capital stock remained extremely low. Following independence, the structural adjustment programmes imposed by the International Monetary Fund further reduced already meagre public investment. Thus, what Africa confronts today is not a deficit of capability or a cultural issue, but the direct consequence of centuries of systematic capital extraction. What Africa genuinely requires is a substantial increase in the share of net fixed capital formation relative to GDP — this is the growth driver repeatedly validated by the data.
