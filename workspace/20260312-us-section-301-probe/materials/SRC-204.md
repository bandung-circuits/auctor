Autonomy and Its Limits: Brazil's Response to Trump's Threats
GIGA Focus Latin America, Number 2, 2026
By Tomas Costa de Azevedo Marques and Filipe Mendonca

Despite the power asymmetry between the United States and Brazil, Lula managed to negotiate, forcing Trump to back off from intervening in domestic affairs and to lower tariffs. But data shows Brazil has limited power to bargain, despite its will.

With Executive Order 14323 on 30 July 2025, a punitive 50 per cent tariff was set for Brazilian exports. The Trump administration cited: the Bolsonaro trial, censorship orders on US social media, "unfair" trade relationship, and attacks on digital trade activities. Contrary to Trump's claims, the US has accumulated a trade surplus of USD 96 billion since 2009.

Brazil responded on three levels: presidential diplomacy (a pivotal 40-minute phone call in December 2025 led to revocation of agricultural tariffs), legislative reaction (the Economic Reciprocity Law of April 2025 established criteria for suspending trade concessions), and corporate lobbying (the American Chamber of Commerce for Brazil and others opposed restrictive measures).

The current US strategy represents a significant departure from historical Section 301 investigations of the 1980s-1990s. The 2025 initiative is characterised by its diffuse and non-technical nature, using economic sanctions as a tool of political disciplining.

Key considerations: US trade policy signifies abandonment of the 70-year postwar rule-based consensus. Sovereign governments should strengthen domestic legislative frameworks for calibrated reciprocity. Business diplomacy can speak more forcefully than official diplomacy. Central to economic security is proactive diversification of partnerships. States must coordinate with like-minded partners to reconstruct collective mechanisms.

Pushing forward the EU-Mercosur agreement is a geopolitical strategy that transcends trade and helps move away from the false dichotomy of choosing between the US and China.
