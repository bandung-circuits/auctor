In re African-American Slave Descendants Litigation
375 F. Supp. 2d 721 (N.D. Ill. 2005)
United States District Court, N.D. Illinois, Eastern Division
July 6, 2005. Judge Norgle.

In the early 2000s numerous lawsuits were filed against modern American companies seeking reparations for the companies' alleged complicity in slavery. The cases were consolidated in federal court in Chicago, Illinois, before U.S. District Court Judge Charles Ronald Norgle Sr., who ultimately dismissed the lawsuits with prejudice. On appeal the dismissal order was modified and affirmed, in part, and reversed in part and remanded. 471 F.3d 754 (7th Cir. 2006).

This case arises out of the institution of human chattel slavery as it existed in the North American colonies and the later formed United States of America. Plaintiffs' Complaint asks the courts to reexamine a tragic period in our Nation's history and to hold various corporate defendants liable for the commercial activities of their alleged predecessors before, during, and after the Civil War in America.

The consolidated case involved nine individual plaintiffs who sued several corporations including Aetna Inc. (insurance company that insured slaves), CSX Corporation (predecessor railroads used slave labor), FleetBoston Financial Corporation (predecessor bank financed slave trade), and other companies. The plaintiffs brought claims including consumer fraud, unjust enrichment, and conspiracy.

The District Court dismissed all claims with prejudice. Key legal obstacles identified by the court included:
1. Standing: Plaintiffs could not demonstrate a concrete, particularized injury traceable to the defendants' conduct
2. Statute of Limitations: Claims based on conduct occurring over 140 years ago were time-barred
3. Political Question Doctrine: The court found aspects of the reparations claim presented non-justiciable political questions better addressed by the legislative branch
4. Lack of Direct Causal Connection: Plaintiffs could not establish a direct causal link between the defendants' historical conduct and their present injuries

On appeal, the Seventh Circuit (471 F.3d 754, 2006) upheld the dismissal on standing grounds for the individual plaintiffs but reversed the dismissal of certain consumer protection claims, finding that some plaintiffs might have standing to bring claims based on the defendants' failure to disclose their historical involvement with slavery. The case was remanded but ultimately did not proceed further.

This case became the landmark ruling establishing the legal barriers to corporate slavery reparations litigation in the United States.
