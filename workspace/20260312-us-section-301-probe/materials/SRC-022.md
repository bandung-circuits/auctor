Tariffs: Estimating the Economic Impact of the 2025 Measures and Proposals | Richmond Fed

By Marina Azzimonti, Zach Edwards, Sonya Ravindranath Waddell and Acacia Wyckoff
Economic Brief, April 2025, No. 25-12

Key Takeaways:
- In 2024, importers paid an estimated 2.2 cents in duties for every dollar of goods imported (AETR of 2.2%).
- Adding 20% tariffs on all Chinese imports and 25% on aluminum/steel increases the AETR to 7.1%.
- Adding 25% tariffs on Canada/Mexico outside USMCA raises the AETR to 10.4%.
- Applying 25% auto tariffs lifts the AETR to 12.4%.
- A 25% tariff on all EU imports raises the AETR to 17.0%, the highest in the analysis.

Empirical research has found that tariff pass-through rate is generally high (often near 100%), meaning the burden falls on domestic consumers and firms rather than foreign exporters.

Over 30% of surveyed firms identify trade and tariffs as their most pressing business concern, up sharply from 8.3% in the previous quarter.

Historical context: The U.S. relied heavily on tariffs exceeding 30% as its primary revenue source until income taxes in 1913. After WWII, GATT reduced tariffs from about 20% in 1947 to below 5% after the Uruguay Round in 1994. Since 1995, tariffs among WTO member countries have generally remained around 2.5%.

The Benefits of Free Trade: Greg Mankiw noted, "Few propositions command as much consensus among professional economists as that open world trade increases economic growth and raises living standards."

The Backlash Against Free Trade: The recent backlash largely stems from the "China shock" -- rapid growth in imports from China following its WTO entry in 2001. Although consumers broadly benefited from lower-priced goods and enhanced variety, the uneven distribution of economic gains and losses fueled public skepticism about globalization. The backlash reflects frustration over insufficient support for displaced workers.

The 2018-19 Tariffs: Each 10% increase in tariffs generally raises producer prices by about 1%. Tariffs generated approximately $51 billion in losses for consumers and firms, though factoring in job gains reduced the net loss to about $7.2 billion. Tariffs resulted in approximately 220,000 jobs lost in industries dependent on imported inputs. Including China's retaliatory tariffs, total employment reduction rises to approximately 320,000 jobs. Rather than returning production to the U.S., many firms shifted supply chains to other countries like Mexico and Vietnam.

Conclusion: The 2025 tariff proposals represent significant shifts in U.S. trade policy. The AETR could rise from 2.2% to as high as 17.0%. Proposed tariffs may raise input costs, disrupt supply chains, and result in higher consumer prices, potentially outweighing any targeted employment gains.
