Tense Threads Under Trump: What the 2025 U.S. Tariffs Really Mean for Bangladesh's Garment Workers

By Farheen Jamil, October 27, 2025

On April 2, 2025, the White House rolled out a broad set of "reciprocal" tariffs, part of a package the president branded "Liberation Day." Bangladesh, whose economy leans heavily on ready-made garments ("RMG"), was assigned a steep 37% add-on to existing U.S. apparel duties. After a tense spring and early summer of order pauses and hurried diplomacy, Dhaka negotiated a partial reprieve: as of August 1, the reciprocal layer on Bangladeshi apparel is 20% instead of 37%. Apparel already pays relatively high U.S. base ("MFN") tariffs, so the effective duty now lands in the mid-30s, a level that squeezes thin margins.

Bangladesh's RMG sector employs about four million people and generates more than 80% of export earnings. When a duty wall jumps, factories can't absorb it all. Buyers reprice, delay, or shift orders; suppliers cut overtime, defer payments, or idle lines. In July, reporting from Dhaka captured that stress in real time: managers warning of capacity cuts and workers worried about layoffs as U.S. brands took a "wait and see" approach.

The tariff climb-down didn't come free. Dhaka linked the trade ask to a set of U.S.-friendly moves: a five-year commitment to import 700,000 tons of U.S. wheat annually, additional purchases of U.S. cotton and soybean oil, and a 25-jet Boeing order.

Most Bangladeshi garment workers are women. When orders pause, the economic hit shows up fast, in overtime cuts, delayed wages, or temporary layoffs, and then at home, in food choices and school fees. That is why labor advocates cautioned that the original 37% shock was big enough to reverse social gains built over decades. Even with the 20% outcome, factory cash flow is tight.

Sourcing teams have three basic levers: absorb, pass through, or re-source. The August shift from 37% to 20% nudged many toward "absorb and optimize" -- renegotiating prices, consolidating vendor lists, placing larger POs to squeeze unit costs.

Bangladesh avoided the worst version of this tariff shock, but the effective duty is still heavy. Tariffs are blunt tools, supply chains are not.
