# Brazil's Regulatory Architecture & the Critical Minerals Bill — Research Excerpts

## Question 1: What are the specific provisions of the critical minerals bill sent to Congress — does it create a formal foreign investment screening mechanism?

### [Q2-001] The CMCE/CIMCE is a geopolitical review mechanism, not a CFIUS-equivalent screening body
**Source**: SRC-Q2-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides the most detailed analysis of whether the bill creates a formal screening mechanism, clarifying it is a review mechanism rather than a prohibition

> The CMCE does hold approval authority over changes in corporate structure involving critical mineral operators. In that narrow sense, a transaction that the committee rejects effectively cannot proceed. However, the framework this represents is a geopolitical review mechanism, not a prohibition on foreign ownership or private asset transfers.
>
> This is a model already familiar in other jurisdictions, including foreign investment review frameworks that scrutinise acquisitions of strategic assets without prohibiting them outright. The novelty in the Brazilian context is the application of this logic specifically to the mineral sector and its codification within a dedicated policy framework.

### [Q2-002] Final approved version replaced "prior approval" (anuência prévia) with "screening/homologation" mechanism
**Source**: SRC-Q2-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Critical clarification of the key legislative evolution — the original bill had prior government approval, but after industry pressure, this was replaced with a weaker screening/homologation mechanism

> Após pressão de mineradoras, parlamentares e de alas do próprio governo contrárias a um poder amplo de veto, o relator retirou a exigência de anuência prévia do Executivo sobre fusões, aquisições, reorganizações societárias e mudanças de controle em empresas detentoras de direitos minerários sobre minerais críticos e estratégicos.
>
> No lugar da análise prévia, o texto passou a prever uma etapa de homologação, feita por meio de um mecanismo de triagem, pelo CIMCE (Conselho Nacional para Industrialização de Minerais Críticos e Estratégicos) e pela ANM (Agência Nacional de Mineração), nos termos de regulamento que será publicado.
>
> Na prática, a mudança reduz a leitura de que o governo terá poder direto e amplo para barrar compras de mineradoras. Ao mesmo tempo, preserva uma etapa formal de validação e acompanhamento estatal sobre operações consideradas sensíveis.
>
> O alcance desse mecanismo ainda dependerá de regulamentação posterior.

### [Q2-003] The bill's four structural pillars: CMCE review, productive verticalization, guarantee fund, transformation program
**Source**: SRC-Q2-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Maps the full institutional architecture of PL 2780/2024

> Pillar One: Regulatory Governance Through the CMCE [...] The CMCE's authority is concentrated in two key functions: Analysing and approving or rejecting changes in corporate structure involving companies that operate with critical or strategic minerals; Monitoring international contracts to ensure that domestic supply is not compromised and that the country's geopolitical security is preserved.
>
> Pillar Two: Productive Verticalization [...] Priority sectors include: Lithium battery fabrication; Rare earth element processing; Technology-intensive transformation of mineral commodities before export.
>
> Pillar Three: The Mineral Activity Guarantee Fund [...] R$ 5 billion, structured to generate up to R$ 50 billion in new mineral project investment through credit guarantee mechanisms.
>
> Pillar Four: Federal Mineral Transformation Program [...] establishes a federal program to coordinate the transformation of mineral resources into higher-value industrial products.

### [Q2-004] Industry lobby (AMC) fought to strip veto power from the council
**Source**: SRC-Q2-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows the political dynamics that shaped the final bill — mining lobby wanted notification regime instead of approval regime

> Marisa Cesar, who chairs the Critical Minerals Association board, told Broadcast that the council should be informed of corporate transactions in advance but should not exercise approval rights that would interfere with business dynamics. Her proposed amendment would convert the CMCE's veto into a notification regime.

### [Q2-005] What the bill explicitly does NOT include: no state company, no export restrictions, no direct prohibition on sales
**Source**: SRC-Q2-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Clarifies the boundaries of the bill's provisions

> Legislative Element | Status in PL 2.780/2024
> Creation of state-owned mineral enterprise | Not included
> Direct prohibition on mining company sales | Not included
> Export restrictions on mineral commodities | Not included

### [Q2-006] Existing law allows foreign investors to take majority stakes without specific federal approval
**Source**: SRC-Q2-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Explains the current regulatory vacuum that the bill seeks to fill

> The current concession framework, dating to 1967, lets foreign investors take majority stakes in Brazilian mineral-rights holders without specific federal approval. The CMCE provision would change that for any deal touching critical and strategic minerals, a list to be defined by resolution.

### [Q2-007] Brazil lacks a CFIUS-equivalent — no cross-economy, security-based screening regime exists
**Source**: SRC-A-004 [existing]
**Relevance**: Previously documented comparison showing Brazil has no equivalent to CFIUS/FIRB/Investment Canada Act

> Country | Regulatory Mechanism | Critical Mineral Application
> Australia | Foreign Investment Review Board (FIRB) | Mandatory review for foreign acquisitions of critical mineral assets
> Canada | Investment Canada Act | National security review triggered for strategic resource transactions
> United States | Committee on Foreign Investment (CFIUS) | Authority to block foreign acquisition of US critical mineral producers
> Brazil | No equivalent body | Constitutional provisions invoked on an ad hoc basis via Supreme Court

### [Q2-008] No South American country operates CFIUS-style cross-economy screening for FDI
**Source**: SRC-Q2-005 [new]
**Relevance**: Academic confirmation that none of Brazil, Chile, or Argentina have cross-economy security-based FDI screening, explaining why the bill's approach is novel

> Unlike the CFIUS model in the US and similar regimes in Europe, none of these South American countries operate a cross-economy, security-based screening regime for foreign investment. This does not imply a regulatory vacuum; instead, investment control is exercised through a patchwork of longstanding, sector-specific restrictions — particularly in strategic areas, such as critical minerals — often involving ownership limits or prior authorization.

### [Q2-009] Key criteria and implementation details remain undefined — will be set by future regulation
**Source**: SRC-Q2-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Identifies the critical gap between legislative intent and operational reality

> What criteria govern CMCE approval or rejection of corporate changes? This is the most consequential undefined element for investment risk assessment. Clear, objective, and published criteria will determine whether the CMCE functions as a proportionate geopolitical safeguard or creates unpredictable transactional friction.
>
> Can CMCE decisions be appealed through Brazilian administrative or judicial process? The legal review pathway for rejected transactions has significant implications for investment security and rule-of-law assessments by international capital.

### [Q2-010] Left critique: the council's powers are already being watered down by industry pressure
**Source**: SRC-Q2-007 [new]
**Relevance**: Critical perspective arguing the CMCE/CIMCE provisions are insufficient and being deliberately weakened

> Em tese, isso poderia permitir ao governo condicionar ou até barrar operações societárias. Na prática, a própria tramitação do PL mostra que as mineradoras já pressionam para limitar esse poder, exigindo critérios "objetivos" na lei e reduzindo a margem de decisão do Executivo. Além disso, o governo nunca precisou desse conselho para intervir na recente venda da Serra Verde para a USA Rare Earth. Lula poderia ter barrado o negócio com uma canetada, mas não o fez e nem o fará.

**Assessment**: This question is now substantially answered. The bill (PL 2780/2024, consolidating 14 attached proposals including PL 4443/2025) does NOT create a formal CFIUS-equivalent foreign investment screening mechanism. It creates the CIMCE (Conselho Nacional para Industrialização de Minerais Críticos e Estratégicos), linked to the Presidency, with authority to review corporate structure changes involving critical mineral operators through a "screening/homologation" mechanism — NOT prior approval/veto. The original bill contained prior approval (anuência prévia) but this was removed after mining industry lobbying. The practical reach of the screening mechanism will depend entirely on future regulation. The bill's other pillars include: productive verticalization incentives, a R$5 billion Mineral Activity Guarantee Fund, and a Federal Mineral Transformation Program. Critically, it explicitly excludes: state-owned mineral enterprise, direct prohibition on mining company sales, and export restrictions.

**Source**: SRC-Q2-002 [new]
**Relevance**: The language of the provision determines its potential retroactive scope, but the language is ambiguous

### [Q2-021] Brazilian legal principle: laws generally do not apply retroactively unless explicitly stated
**Source**: SRC-C-007 [existing], general Brazilian legal principle
**Relevance**: Under Brazilian constitutional law, legislation generally does not have retroactive effect unless the law itself explicitly provides for it (Article 5, XXXVI, CF — "a lei não prejudicará o direito adquirido, o ato jurídico perfeito e a coisa julgada")

### [Q2-022] The Serra Verde deal was announced April 20, expected closing Q3 2026 — potentially overlapping with bill passage
**Source**: SRC-A-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: The deal's expected closing timeline (Q3 2026) means the transaction may not be fully completed when the bill becomes law

> The transaction is expected to close in the third quarter of 2026, subject to regulatory approvals and, now, the outcome of the Supreme Court challenge.

### [Q2-023] The homologation/screening mechanism's details will be set by future regulation — retroactive scope could be defined there
**Source**: SRC-Q2-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Even if the bill itself does not address retroactivity, the implementing regulation could potentially define the scope

> É esse regulamento que deverá definir quais operações serão submetidas à triagem, quais critérios serão usados, quais prazos deverão ser cumpridos e em quais situações o governo poderá questionar ou condicionar negócios envolvendo ativos minerais estratégicos.

**Assessment**: This question is only partially answered through inference. No source explicitly addresses retroactive application. Based on Brazilian constitutional law (Article 5, XXXVI), laws generally do not apply retroactively to acquired rights, perfected legal acts, or res judicata unless explicitly stated. The bill does not contain explicit retroactive language. However, the Serra Verde deal's closing is expected in Q3 2026, potentially overlapping with the bill's passage and 90-day regulation window. The key uncertainty: if the deal closing occurs after the bill becomes law, the CIMCE's screening mechanism could apply to the closing phase even though the announcement preceded the law. The implementing regulation will define which operations are subject to screening — it could potentially include transactions "in progress" at the time of enactment. This remains a significant gap requiring legal expert analysis.

## Question 4: How do existing Brazilian regulatory bodies (ANM, CADE, Ministry of Mines and Energy) currently evaluate foreign acquisitions of mining companies?

### [Q2-024] Current framework: no specific evaluation of foreign acquisitions — general mining and competition law apply
**Source**: SRC-C-007 [existing]
**Relevance**: Confirms that under current law, foreign companies can own mining rights through Brazilian entities without specific federal approval for acquisitions

> A foreign company cannot own a reconnaissance, exploration or mining permit in Brazil. However, a Brazilian company that holds such permit may be fully owned by a foreign company with the totality of its shares.

### [Q2-025] ANM evaluates mining viability only, not national interest or security implications
**Source**: SRC-H-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Critical gap identified — ANM's scope is limited to technical mining assessment, not strategic evaluation

> The developer first obtains the mining title from the National Mining Agency, which evaluates only mining viability, not environmental viability. [...] In a sector with maturation cycles of 10 to 20 years, the risk of a court decision during the construction or operation phase is just as relevant as geological risk.

### [Q2-026] CADE evaluates under competition law — no national security dimension
**Source**: SRC-A-006 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents CADE's narrow mandate and procedural flexibility

> CADE will look to assess whether the USAR-Serra Verde combination constitutes a "concentration act" under Brazilian competition law. [...] The agency stressed that the proceeding "does not necessarily mean that the acts must be notified or that there are competition issues."

### [Q2-027] MME's role: policy formulation, not transaction review
**Source**: SRC-D-007 [existing]
**Relevance**: MME's mandate is policy-level, not transaction-specific evaluation

> The MME is a federal public administration body responsible for formulating national policies, industry planning and standards in geology, mineral exploration and development, and mineral processing.

### [Q2-028] Interministerial set includes Defense, but no formal screening process
**Source**: SRC-F-002 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows the regulatory architecture is fragmented across multiple agencies without a central screening mechanism

> "Regulation involves an interministerial set: the National Mining Agency, the Ministry of Mines and Energy, MDIC, and also Defence — because many of these materials are used in defence."

### [Q2-029] Judiciary becomes the de facto final regulator of mining sector
**Source**: SRC-H-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Without a dedicated screening mechanism, courts fill the regulatory vacuum

> All of those factors converge into a system in which the judiciary becomes the final regulator of Brazil's mining sector, especially when it comes to critical minerals. "The Supreme Court rules on Indigenous lands. The Superior Court of Justice sets precedent on environmental liability. Local courts interfere in licensing."

### [Q2-030] The 1967 concession framework allows foreign majority stakes without specific federal approval
**Source**: SRC-Q2-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Confirms the regulatory vacuum in current law for foreign acquisitions of mining companies

> The current concession framework, dating to 1967, lets foreign investors take majority stakes in Brazilian mineral-rights holders without specific federal approval.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. Under current law, there is no formal foreign investment screening mechanism for mining acquisitions. ANM evaluates only mining viability (technical/geological), not national interest or security implications. CADE evaluates under competition law only — its mandate does not include national security dimensions. MME's role is policy formulation, not transaction review. The fragmented interministerial set (ANM, MME, MDIC, Defense) has no coordinated screening process. Foreign investors can acquire majority stakes in Brazilian mineral-rights holders without specific federal approval under the 1967 concession framework. Without a dedicated screening mechanism, the judiciary becomes the de facto final regulator, with courts filling the vacuum through constitutional petitions like Rede's ADPF. The bill seeks to partially fill this gap through the CIMCE, but its practical scope remains undefined.

## Question 5: What comparative models exist for critical mineral investment screening in other Global South nations (Indonesia, Chile, Mexico)?

### [Q2-031] Indonesia: no CFIUS-equivalent; uses industry-specific mechanisms — export bans and domestic processing requirements instead of screening
**Source**: SRC-Q2-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Indonesia does not screen FDI through a security lens; instead uses blanket ownership limits and domestic processing mandates

> Indonesia has historically limited any foreign investment into specific industries through blanket bans or limits on foreign ownership rather than screening investments individually. Instead of screening FDI through a strictly security lens, policies requiring more investment into post-extraction processes create new competitive advantages or preferences for certain companies.

> Indonesia progressively banned the export of nickel ore between 2009 and 2019. The move forced foreign partners dependent on supply from Indonesia to invest directly into beneficiation, processing and refining operations.

### [Q2-032] Indonesia nickel model: export ban drove domestic processing investment
**Source**: SRC-J-011 [existing]
**Relevance**: Existing excerpt documenting Indonesia's approach — export ban rather than screening

> China would be eager to work with Brazil, citing the example of Indonesia, which banned the export of unprocessed nickel ore in 2020. Its move prompted Chinese firms to relocate their processing facilities to Indonesia itself, moving in behind the export ban and shifting midstream activity to where the ore was being extracted.

### [Q2-033] Chile: "soft nationalization" of lithium — majority state ownership for new projects, but no general screening mechanism
**Source**: SRC-Q2-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Chile's approach for lithium is state participation requirements, not CFIUS-style screening

> Chile launched a "soft" nationalization of its lithium sector on April 20, imposing requirements that all future projects be majority-owned by a state-owned vehicle managed by Codelco and that future projects use direct lithium extraction (DLE) to minimize water use in the Atacama Desert.

### [Q2-034] Chile: investment facilitation with tighter policy coordination — no security-based screening
**Source**: SRC-Q2-005 [new]
**Relevance**: Academic analysis confirming Chile has no CFIUS-equivalent, instead uses state enterprises and partnership instruments

> Chile combines investment facilitation with tighter policy coordination. The state steers the sector via state-owned enterprises and partnership instruments that tie extraction to R&D, and domestic value-addition. Chile also has a progressive royalty regime that serves various social purposes.

> None of these South American countries operate a cross-economy, security-based screening regime for foreign investment.

### [Q2-035] Mexico: lithium nationalized — state exclusivity for exploration and processing, Supreme Court validated
**Source**: Search result (not fetched as full source, but confirmed through multiple search results)
**Relevance**: Mexico's approach is outright nationalization of lithium, not screening

> Mexico's Supreme Court validated the 2022 Mining Law reform reserving lithium exploration, exploitation, and processing exclusively to the state, settling a constitutional challenge and consolidating the legal framework under which LitioMx operates.

### [Q2-036] Bolivia cautionary tale: full nationalization left country out of the lithium value chain
**Source**: SRC-Q2-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Comparative case showing the risks of aggressive nationalization without screening

> Bolivia is a cautionary tale. Despite holding the world's largest estimated lithium reserves, the decision to fully nationalize the sector under state control more than a decade ago has left Bolivia almost entirely out of the lithium value chain. Only miners willing to accept the high level of risk have taken part in recent state-supported pilot projects, and none had any prior experience mining lithium on a commercial scale.

### [Q2-037] The "CFIUS dilemma" — adopting security-based screening reduces strategic flexibility for Global South nations
**Source**: SRC-Q2-005 [new]
**Relevance**: Academic analysis explaining why Global South nations resist CFIUS-style screening

> The dilemma is not simply whether to adopt investment screening, but whether adopting a security-based screening model akin to CFIUS would reduce the strategic flexibility these states seek to preserve. [...] In parts of South America, transplanting this model may carry a different political meaning: it may be read as a move toward U.S.-style geopolitical alignment, especially vis-à-vis China.

> For these states, the absence of a "Latin American CFIUS" may not be a technical failure but a sovereign choice. By maintaining what Sánchez describes as a flexible legal architecture, these countries avoid the "alignment trap" and preserve their "security of choice."

### [Q2-038] Some developing markets use industry-specific mechanisms; most of Latin America has no effective FDI screening
**Source**: SRC-Q2-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Broad comparative mapping of screening approaches worldwide

> Some developing markets such as Indonesia, Malaysia and Brazil use only industry-specific mechanisms based on the relative salience of a sector. [...] It does, however, remain the case that many countries have no effective FDI screening mechanisms, as is broadly the case in Africa and most of Latin America.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. No Global South nation has adopted a CFIUS-equivalent cross-economy, security-based screening mechanism for foreign investment. The approaches are:

1. **Indonesia**: Uses blanket foreign ownership limits and domestic processing mandates (nickel export ban, 2009-2020) rather than individual transaction screening. The approach forces value-chain investment domestically rather than blocking acquisitions.

2. **Chile**: Uses "soft nationalization" for lithium (state majority ownership via Codelco for new projects), progressive royalties, and state enterprise partnership instruments. For general mining, it relies on investment facilitation with policy coordination — no screening.

3. **Mexico**: Outright nationalization of lithium (2022 Mining Law reform, Supreme Court validated 2026) — state exclusivity over exploration, exploitation, and processing via LitioMx. No general screening mechanism.

4. **Bolivia**: Cautionary tale of full nationalization that left the country entirely out of the lithium value chain.

The CELIS Institute analysis explains the structural reason: Global South nations resist CFIUS-style screening because it would reduce strategic flexibility, signal geopolitical alignment with the US, risk Chinese trade retaliation, and carry toxic "National Security" associations linked to military dictatorship memory. Brazil's bill represents a novel middle position — neither CFIUS-style screening nor outright nationalization nor export bans, but a sector-specific review mechanism with undefined practical scope.
