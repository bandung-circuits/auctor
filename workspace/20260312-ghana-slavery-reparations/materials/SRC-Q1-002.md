A Survey of the Impacts of IMF Structural Adjustment in Africa: Growth, Social Spending, and Debt Relief

By Robert Naiman and Neil Watkins, Center for Economic and Policy Research (CEPR), April 1999

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The role of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) in managing the economies of developing countries has come under increasing criticism in the last two years, especially since the Asian financial crisis. This paper relies largely on the IMF's own data to consider the results of the IMF's intervention in the economies of sub-Saharan Africa. We examine the record of countries that have participated in the IMF's Enhanced Structural Adjustment Facility (ESAF).

Among this report's main findings:

- Developing countries worldwide implementing ESAF programs have experienced lower economic growth than those who have been outside of these programs. African countries subject to ESAF programs have fared even worse than other countries pursuing ESAF programs; countries in Africa subject to ESAF programs have actually seen their per capita incomes decline. It will be years before these populations recover the per capita incomes that they had prior to structural adjustment.

- While African countries urgently need to increase spending on health care, education, and sanitation, IMF structural adjustment programs have forced these countries to reduce such spending. In African countries with ESAF programs, the average amount of per capita government spending on education actually declined between 1986 and 1996.

- Neither IMF-mandated macroeconomic policies nor debt relief under the IMF-sponsored HIPC Initiative have sufficiently reduced these countries' debt burdens. Total external debt as a share of GNP for ESAF countries increased from 71.1% to 87.8% between 1985-1995. For sub-Saharan Africa debt rose as a share of GDP from 58% in 1988 to 70% in 1996.

AGGREGATE MEASURES:

1. Economic Growth:
- Annual real per capita GDP growth averaged 0.0% for all ESAF countries over 1991-1995, whereas non-ESAF developing countries experienced 1.0% annual real per capita GDP growth.
- Sub-Saharan African countries with ESAF programs experienced an average annual 0.3% decline in real per capita incomes over the period of IMF adjustment from 1991-1995.
- According to World Bank projections, it would take until 2006 merely to return to 1982 (pre-structural adjustment) levels of per capita income in sub-Saharan Africa.

2. Social Spending:
- In African countries with ESAF programs, per capita education spending actually declined by 0.7% a year on average between 1986 and 1996.
- Real per capita outlays on health in African ESAF countries increased on average by 2.5% a year during the same period, which was slower than the rate of growth of health spending in other, non-African, ESAF countries.

3. External Indebtedness:
- Total external debt as a share of GNP for all ESAF users increased from 71.1% to 87.8% between 1985-1995.
- The amount of total external debt outstanding for sub-Saharan Africa rose from $150.5 billion in 1988 to $227.1 billion in 1996. As a share of GDP, the region's debt increased from 58% in 1988 to 70% in 1996.
- By 1996, external debt per capita for sub-Saharan Africa (excluding South Africa) was $365, while GNP per capita was $308.

COUNTRY EXPERIENCES:

Zimbabwe:
- During the 1980s, Zimbabwe's economy grew briskly: real growth averaged about 4% per year.
- Zimbabwe began structural adjustment in earnest in 1991 with a $484 million IMF loan.
- Between 1991 and 1996: manufacturing output contracted 14%; real GDP per capita declined by 5.8%; total private investment declined by 9% in real terms; private per capita consumption fell by 37%.
- Spending on health care declined as a share of the budget from 6.4% to 4.3%, and as a share of GDP from 3.1% to 2.1%.
- Real per capita expenditure on primary and secondary education declined by 36% and 25% respectively between 1990/91 and 1993/94.

Cote d'Ivoire:
- IMF became involved in 1989. From 1989-1993, per capita GDP fell by 15%.
- Between 1988-1995, the incidence and intensity of poverty doubled, with the number of people earning less than $1/day increasing from 17.8% to 36.8%.
- During IMF structural adjustment (1990-1995), real per capita public spending on education declined by more than 35%.
- External debt burden increased from 132.4% to 210.8% of GDP.

Uganda:
- IMF first became involved in 1987. Real per capita GDP growth averaged 4.2% between 1992-1997.
- However, rural per capita private incomes increased just 4% over 1988/89 to 1994/95.
- Government spending grew but from a very low starting level: in 1986, government expenditure represented just 9% of GDP.
- Real per capita output in health care was lower in each of the years from 1992-1994 than it had been in 1989.
- A declining share of the recurrent budget was spent on education over the adjustment period.
- Less than one year after receiving HIPC debt relief, Uganda's debt burden had once again become unsustainable.

Mozambique:
- One of the poorest countries in the world. Began structural adjustment in 1987.
- A doctor on the government payroll earned $350/month in 1991, $175/month in 1993, and less than $100/month by 1996.
- Even after IMF debt relief, actual debt service paid by Mozambique would be as high or higher in each of the years from 2000-2003 as it was in 1997.
- UNDP estimated that relieved of debt payments, severely indebted countries in Africa could have saved the lives of 21 million people and provided 90 million girls and women with access to basic education by the year 2000.

NET TRANSFERS FROM IMF:
- Repayments by African governments to the IMF outpaced new resources in 1997 and 1998, resulting in a net transfer from Africa to the IMF of more than $1 billion.
- Between 1997 and 1998, Africa's debt increased by 3% to $226 billion even as African countries paid back $3.5 billion more than they borrowed in 1998.
- The "growth turnaround" was short-lived: real GDP per capita grew by 1.4% in 1996, slowed to 0.4% in 1997, and per capita incomes fell by 0.8% in 1998.
