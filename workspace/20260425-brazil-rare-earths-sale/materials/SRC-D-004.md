Brazil's Court Challenge to Serra Verde's Sale to USA Rare Earth

BY MUFLIH HIDAYAT
ON APRIL 26, 2026

When Resource Nationalism Meets Rare Earth Geopolitics

The global scramble for critical mineral supply has quietly rewritten the rules of cross-border investment. Nations that once competed to attract foreign capital into their extractive industries are now deploying constitutional mechanisms, nationalist political movements, and judicial intervention to assert control over the same resources they once freely licensed. The Brazil court challenge to Serra Verde sale to USA Rare Earth has become the latest flashpoint where these tensions are playing out at scale, with rare earth supply chains and Western industrial strategy hanging in the balance.

A Supreme Court filing lodged on 25 April 2026 by the left-wing Brazilian political party Rede Sustentabilidade has placed a proposed $2.8 billion acquisition of Serra Verde Group by USA Rare Earth Inc (NASDAQ: USAR) in legal jeopardy. Understanding why this challenge matters requires looking well beyond Brazilian domestic politics. It demands an examination of deposit geology, global supply chain mathematics, and the strategic calculus driving Western governments toward any available non-Chinese rare earth source.

The Legal Challenge: Constitutional Sovereignty Over Strategic Minerals

Rede Sustentabilidade's filing with Brazil's Supreme Court centres on a straightforward but profound argument: that existing Brazilian legislation is constitutionally inadequate when it comes to protecting the national interest in the exploitation of strategic mineral resources. The party's position holds that no transaction should proceed if it results in the direct or indirect transfer of significant economic control over strategic mineral assets to foreign entities.

This framing is deliberate. By grounding the challenge in constitutional language rather than standard foreign investment or antitrust objections, the party is attempting to invoke a higher legal threshold. Brazil's Constitution contains provisions embedding the concept of national interest into the governance of mineral exploitation, and the argument is that these provisions create a structural obligation the legislature has not yet fulfilled through specific statute.

What makes this legally significant is the absence in Brazil of a dedicated foreign investment screening body equivalent to those operating elsewhere. Furthermore, critical minerals geopolitics have made this institutional gap increasingly consequential:

Country | Regulatory Mechanism | Critical Mineral Application
Australia | Foreign Investment Review Board (FIRB) | Mandatory review for foreign acquisitions of critical mineral assets
Canada | Investment Canada Act | National security review triggered for strategic resource transactions
United States | Committee on Foreign Investment (CFIUS) | Authority to block foreign acquisition of US critical mineral producers
Brazil | No equivalent body | Constitutional provisions invoked on an ad hoc basis via Supreme Court

Because Brazil has no formal screening mechanism analogous to those operating in Australia, Canada, or the United States, challenges to foreign acquisitions of strategic resource assets have no prescribed institutional pathway. Instead, they arrive at the Supreme Court through constitutional petition, creating an unpredictable legal environment for acquirers.

Serra Verde's Pela Ema: Why This Deposit Is Strategically Exceptional

To understand the stakes, the geology of Pela Ema must be examined on its own terms. Located in Goias State in central Brazil, Pela Ema hosts an ionic clay rare earth deposit, a deposit type that carries disproportionate strategic significance relative to more commonly discussed hard-rock or carbonate-hosted rare earth formations.

What Makes Ionic Clay Deposits Different

Ionic clay rare earth deposits are distinguished by their mineralogy and extraction characteristics. In these deposits, rare earth elements are adsorbed onto clay mineral surfaces rather than locked within hard crystalline mineral structures. This means:

- Ore does not require crushing or grinding before processing, significantly reducing energy consumption and capital costs per tonne of output
- Heap leach or in-situ leach extraction methods can be applied, often at lower operating cost than hard-rock alternatives
- The deposit profiles at Pela Ema are geologically similar in character to the ionic clay formations found in China's Jiangxi Province, which have historically supplied the overwhelming majority of the world's heavy rare earth oxides
- Heavy rare earth elements such as dysprosium, terbium, and yttrium are disproportionately concentrated in ionic clay deposits compared to other deposit types

This geological affinity with Jiangxi-type deposits is precisely why Pela Ema has attracted the level of capital and strategic attention it has. The vast majority of global rare earth projects outside China are dominated by light rare earth mineralisation. Consequently, heavy rare earth supply from outside Chinese processing infrastructure is acutely scarce.

Production Scale and the Global Supply Gap

Serra Verde commenced commercial production at Pela Ema in 2024. Current annual output stands at approximately 100 metric tons of rare earth oxides. The operation is being actively upgraded, with production targeted to reach approximately 6,400 metric tons per year by the end of 2027, representing a 64-fold scaling of output over roughly three years.

If Pela Ema achieves its targeted production rate, it would represent one of the most consequential non-Chinese sources of heavy rare earth oxides on the planet, with the potential to supply a material portion of global demand outside Chinese-controlled processing chains.

The applications driving critical minerals demand for these outputs include permanent magnets for electric vehicle motors, guidance and actuation systems in defence hardware, and direct-drive generators in offshore wind turbines. These are not discretionary commodity markets. They represent procurement supply chains with multi-year lead times and very limited substitution options.

The Deal Structure and Its Strategic Architecture

USA Rare Earth's proposed acquisition of Serra Verde is valued at approximately $2.8 billion in combined cash and stock, representing one of the largest transactions in the rare earth sector's history. The scale of the consideration reflects the strategic scarcity premium attached to Pela Ema's deposit profile rather than its current revenue generation at 100 tonnes per year of output.

The transaction's architecture signals that this is not a purely commercial mining deal. The involvement of the US International Development Finance Corporation (DFC), which has committed approximately $565 million in financing to support the transaction, demonstrates that Washington views Pela Ema as a component of its industrial and defence supply chain strategy. This directly reflects the broader priorities shaping the US rare earth supply chain.

Additional structural features reportedly embedded in the deal include floor pricing protections, a mechanism designed to de-risk the US supply chain exposure by providing price support under scenarios where rare earth market prices might otherwise undermine project economics. The transaction is expected to close in the third quarter of 2026, subject to regulatory approvals and, now, the outcome of the Supreme Court challenge.

Under the proposed deal structure, legacy Serra Verde investors would retain approximately a 34% stake in the combined USA Rare Earth entity post-closing, providing economic continuity for existing shareholders while transferring operational control to the US-listed entity.

The TerraBras Rejection and Brazil's Policy Contradiction

The Supreme Court filing did not emerge in a policy vacuum. Just one day before Rede Sustentabilidade lodged its petition, Brazil's government formally rejected the concept of a state-run critical minerals entity proposed under the working title of "TerraBras." The government's stated position was that it saw no need for such a vehicle, even as broader US-Brazil minerals negotiations appeared stalled on policy grounds.

This creates a notable political contradiction. On one hand, Brazil's government has rejected the concept of state intervention in critical minerals management through a dedicated public entity. On the other hand, a party challenging the absence of adequate legislative protections is now seeking judicial intervention to achieve similar protective outcomes through constitutional means.

The rejection of TerraBras left a policy vacuum in Brazil's approach to strategic minerals governance. Unlike Chile, which has pursued a hybrid model for lithium through state participation requirements, or Indonesia, which mandated domestic processing before export in the nickel sector, Brazil lacks a coherent intermediate framework between full state control and unrestricted foreign acquisition. However, Brazil's finance minister has indicated the government seeks to regulate essential minerals without resorting to tax incentives, suggesting a regulatory approach remains under active consideration.

Three Scenarios: How the Court Challenge Could Resolve

Scenario 1: The Court Issues an Injunction
If Brazil's Supreme Court accepts jurisdiction and suspends the transaction pending full review, the Q3 2026 closing timeline becomes immediately unworkable. USA Rare Earth shares on the NASDAQ would face material downward pressure as deal certainty collapses. A prolonged suspension could force renegotiation of deal terms, introduction of mandatory Brazilian state participation conditions, or, in an extreme outcome, abandonment of the transaction entirely.

The precedent-setting consequences would extend well beyond Serra Verde. A successful injunction would establish that foreign acquisitions of Brazilian strategic mineral assets face genuine constitutional exposure even in the absence of dedicated foreign investment review legislation, fundamentally altering the risk profile of future critical minerals M&A in Brazil.

Scenario 2: The Court Declines to Intervene
If the Supreme Court rejects the petition or declines to issue an injunction, the transaction proceeds toward its Q3 2026 target close. Rede Sustentabilidade would likely pursue legislative channels, potentially campaigning for constitutional amendments or new statutes creating a formal foreign investment screening mechanism for strategic resources.

This outcome would set a permissive precedent for further foreign-controlled acquisition of Brazilian critical mineral assets, accelerating the country's integration into Western supply chain frameworks while potentially intensifying domestic political opposition to future transactions.

Scenario 3: A Negotiated Structural Modification
The most operationally complex but geopolitically pragmatic resolution would involve a court-mediated or government-brokered restructuring of the transaction terms. This could include mandatory Brazilian government co-investment, local processing and value-addition requirements, or supply reservation clauses ensuring domestic access to a proportion of Pela Ema's output.

Hybrid ownership models of this type have precedent in the critical minerals sector. Indonesia's nickel processing mandates and Chile's evolving lithium governance framework both demonstrate that resource-rich nations can assert national interest conditions on strategic minerals without categorically blocking foreign investment.

What This Means for Western Rare Earth Supply Diversification

The timing of this challenge is acutely uncomfortable for Western supply chain planners. Defence procurement and clean energy manufacturing timelines are creating specific demand inflection points for non-Chinese heavy rare earth supply in the 2027 to 2028 window. A prolonged legal suspension of Pela Ema's ownership transfer could delay the capital deployment and operational scaling needed to meet those timelines.

The US and European Union deepened their cooperation on critical minerals frameworks on 24 April 2026, with agreements to collaborate on price floors, subsidies, and trade measures designed to weaken Chinese market leverage. The Brazil court challenge to Serra Verde sale to USA Rare Earth is structurally at odds with that diplomatic objective. A judicial block would create a significant gap in the Western critical minerals diversification roadmap at precisely the moment that roadmap is being formalised.

China's rare earth strategy has long centred on leveraging its dominance in heavy rare earth processing, which is not a market outcome but a structural feature of decades of policy and investment concentrated in Jiangxi Province. The ionic clay deposits at Pela Ema represent one of the very few global asset bases capable of replicating that production profile outside Chinese jurisdiction. The geopolitical calculus is therefore stark: a blocked sale does not preserve Brazilian sovereignty in isolation. It maintains Chinese processing dominance by removing the most credible near-term alternative.
