# Statistical Data — Relevant Excerpts

## [M-B-001] Total reparations quantified at $100-131 trillion
**Source**: SRC-B-003
**Relevance**: The Brattle Group's landmark quantification provides the most comprehensive economic estimate of reparations for transatlantic chattel slavery, central to the data underpinning the reparations movement.

> Brattle's team quantified certain elements of reparations to be $100–131 trillion. These reparations are for harms separated into two categories of damages: harm during the period when chattel slavery was carried out ($77–108 trillion) and continuing harm post-enslavement ($23 trillion). According to Brattle's estimates, the harms during the period of enslavement were inflicted on 19 million people over four centuries, including Africans kidnapped and transported to the Americas and Caribbean and those born into slavery, for a total of 802 million years of life to be compensated.

## [M-B-002] Country-by-country reparations breakdown totaling $107.8 trillion
**Source**: SRC-B-002
**Relevance**: Provides the granular country-level breakdown of reparations owed by each former slave-owning state, essential quantitative data for the reparations debate.

> Robinson said that Britain is required to pay 14 countries a sum of US$24 trillion. [...] for Spain the amount owed is estimated at US$17 trillion and the United States is required to pay approximately US$26 trillion for its practice of TCS from 1776 to 1865, while France is required to pay approximately nine trillion US dollars and Portugal US$20 trillion to Brazil, which is also required to pay about four trillion dollars in respect to its practice of TCS in Brazil from 1822 to 1888. The amount for The Netherlands is estimated at five trillion dollars of which three trillion should be paid to Suriname and an estimated $52 billion to Guyana. Robinson said that the aggregate sum of reparations to be paid by all former slave-owning states totals US$107.8 trillion.

## [M-B-003] Scale of the transatlantic slave trade: 12-15 million people
**Source**: SRC-B-008
**Relevance**: Provides the key demographic statistics on the scale of the transatlantic slave trade -- the quantitative foundation for the "gravest crime" characterization.

> It's estimated that over 400 years, 15 million men, women and children were forcibly transported. Other sources say 12 to 12.8 million were enslaved. About 10.7 million arrived in the Americas alive, while between 1.5 and 2 million died during the crossing, also known as the Middle Passage.

## [M-B-004] Total slaves taken from Africa: close to 20 million across all slave trades
**Source**: SRC-B-005
**Relevance**: Comprehensive figure covering all four African slave trades, not just the transatlantic, providing broader statistical context.

> Between 1400 and 1900, the African continent experienced four sizeable slave trades. [...] In total, close to 20 million slaves were taken from the continent (Nunn 2008). According to the best estimates, by 1800 Africa's population was half of what it would have been, had the slave trades not occurred (Manning 1990).

---

## [M-B-005] Slave trade accounts for 72% of Africa's income gap with the world
**Source**: SRC-B-005
**Relevance**: Quantifies the long-term economic impact of the slave trade on Africa's development, a critical statistic for the reparations argument.

> According to the estimates from Nunn (2008), if the slave trades had not occurred, then 72% of the average income gap between Africa and the rest of the world would not exist today, and 99% of the income gap between Africa and other developing countries would not exist. In other words, had the slave trades not occurred, Africa would not be the most underdeveloped region of the world and it would have a similar level of development to Latin America or Asia.

## [M-B-006] Africa's per capita income gap quantified
**Source**: SRC-B-005
**Relevance**: Provides specific dollar figures illustrating the current economic disparity that the slave trade helped create.

> The mean level of average per capita income of the countries in Africa is $1,834 (measured in 2000). This is significantly lower than the income for the rest of the world (which is $8,809), and it is much lower even than the income of other developing countries (which is $4,868), where a developing country is defined as one with less than $14,000 in per capita income.

## [M-B-011] Slave trade profit rates and economic flows to Britain
**Source**: SRC-B-007
**Relevance**: Quantifies the economic profits flowing from the slave trade to European economies, crucial data for the reparations case.

> Ellora notes that scholars have put forward figures such as the slave trade bringing "over 1.6 million pounds into Britain each year" in the mid-1800s, and profit rates once thought to be as high as 33%. Recent work has revised those returns down to more like 8–10% – still highly profitable, but more modest than in the earlier narrative.

## [M-B-012] Econometric evidence: 10% increase in slave voyages = 1.1% increase in European city populations
**Source**: SRC-B-007
**Relevance**: Modern econometric evidence quantifying the direct economic benefit of slave trading to European port cities.

> One of her key estimates is that "a 10% increase in slaving voyages leads to a 1.1% increase in city populations." This suggests that, at least contemporaneously, participation in the slave trade was associated with faster development for the European cities involved.
