The real reasons for the slowdown of China's economy — and its relation to the US

By John Ross, Socialist Economic Bulletin, April 2019

[Key excerpts on capital formation and GDP growth correlation:]

Saving and Capital Creation:

China's net saving, equivalent to China's net capital creation, fell from a peak of 39.3% of Gross National Income (GNI) in 2007, to 36.7% of GNI in 2009, to 24.9% of GNI in 2016 — a decline of 14.4% of GNI in 2007-2016. This extremely large fall in the percentage of net saving in China's economy, a large decline in the capital supply, is necessarily a strongly negative supply side shock for China's economy.

The correlation of China's saving and economic growth:

Analysis shows that the correlation between China's capital creation/saving and its economic growth is even closer than between net fixed investment and economic growth.

Key correlation findings (for China specifically):

- Linear correlation of gross saving and economic growth: 0.84 (extremely high)
- Non-linear correlation of gross saving and economic growth: 0.87 (extraordinarily high)
- Linear correlation of the percentage of net saving in China's GDP with its economic growth: 0.88 (ultra-close connection)
- Non-linear correlation of the percentage of net saving in China's GDP and its economic growth: 0.91 (extraordinarily high correlation)

Ross argues: "this extremely high correlation shows that it is not possible to increase China's GDP growth without increasing the percentage of net saving in GDP, and that any decline in the percentage of net saving in GDP will be accompanied by an economic slowdown."

Note on fixed investment specifically: Ross also examines the correlation between net fixed investment and GDP growth. He finds this correlation is also "extremely high" but slightly lower than the saving-GDP correlation. The net fixed capital formation correlation with GDP growth is presented as very strong but the specific 0.91 figure refers to net saving (not net fixed capital formation) in the non-linear case for China specifically.

Conclusion: The overwhelming cause of the slowing of China's economy is a fall in capital/investment creation. This is shown in growth accounting by the fact that almost nine tenths of the decline in GDP growth is accounted for by the decline in capital inputs. The same trend is confirmed by national accounts data showing the decline in the percentage of net fixed investment in GDP and the even sharper fall in the percentage of net saving in GDP.

Capital creation and China's economic growth:

The difference between savings and fixed investment correlation is not of crucial importance for economic policy, as both correlations are extremely high, confirming that it is capital creation which is the dominant issue in China's economic growth. China's total supply of capital affects other features of the economy than fixed investment — for example, a reduction in capital creation will produce negative consequences such as upward pressure on interest rates, decreasing profitability of companies, and increase in leverage.

[Note: This article focuses specifically on China's economy, not a cross-country analysis. The 0.91 correlation refers specifically to the non-linear correlation of net saving as a percentage of China's GDP with China's GDP growth rate, not to net fixed capital formation across major economies.]
