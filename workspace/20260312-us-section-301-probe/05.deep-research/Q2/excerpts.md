# Leverage Asymmetry and Global South Collective Response Mechanisms — Research Excerpts

## Question 1: What specific leverage do Global South economies possess in trade disputes with the United States, and how effectively have they deployed these levers historically? Consider export dependencies, critical mineral control, market access for US services, and geopolitical alignment.

### [Q2-001] China's Gallium and Germanium as Proven Leverage
**Source**: SRC-300 [new]
**Relevance**: Demonstrates the effectiveness of China's critical mineral export restrictions as leverage, while revealing vulnerabilities (third-country reexport loopholes)

> China's new ban on germanium and gallium exports to the United States builds on earlier restrictions on the materials in July 2023. However, discrepancies between U.S. and Chinese trade data suggest that the materials are reaching the United States through trade via third countries. [...] The USGS has estimated such a total ban could cause a 150% increase in price for gallium and a 26% increase for germanium and constrain supply, leading to a $3.4 billion decrease in U.S. GDP. [...] The lack of a comparable gallium stockpile could make the United States more likely to face stronger effects from a severe crackdown.

### [Q2-002] China's Rare Earth Dominance as Strategic Leverage
**Source**: SRC-201 [new]
**Relevance**: Details how China's dominance in the rare earth value chain — 70% mining, 90% processing, 93% magnet manufacturing — creates direct leverage over U.S. defense supply chains

> The newly announced restrictions represent China's most consequential measures to date targeting the defense sector. Under the new rules, starting December 1, 2025, companies with any affiliation to foreign militaries—including those of the United States—will be largely denied export licenses. [...] China is rapidly scaling up its munitions manufacturing capacity and acquiring advanced weapons platforms and equipment at a rate estimated to be five to six times faster than that of the United States.

### [Q2-003] Indonesia's Rare Earth Leverage in Tariff Negotiations
**Source**: SRC-205 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how a middle-power Global South economy used critical mineral access as a bargaining chip

> Indonesia had previously announced that it was continuing with its quest for renewed trade negotiations with the Trump Administration and putting on its access to Indonesian rare earths as a sweetener. The tactic has seemed to bear fruit, with the Indonesians triumphantly having their tariff rate cut from 32% to 19%.

### [Q2-004] Brazil's Three-Level Leverage Strategy
**Source**: SRC-204 [new]
**Relevance**: Demonstrates how a major Global South economy deployed presidential diplomacy, legislative instruments, and corporate lobbying as leverage against U.S. tariff pressure

> Despite the systemic pressure, Brazil responded on three different levels: presidential diplomacy, legislative reaction, and corporate lobbying. [...] In April 2025 Congress approved the Economic Reciprocity Law (Lei da Reciprocidade Econômica), which established criteria for suspending trade concessions, providing a legal mechanism to retaliate against "unfair or abusive" international practices. [...] The American Chamber of Commerce for Brazil, the National Coffee Association, and the Meat Imports Council of America explicitly opposed the restrictive measures. [...] Contrary to Trump's claims, the US has accumulated a trade surplus of USD 96 billion since 2009.

### [Q2-005] Lowy Institute: China's Rare Earth Chokehold as Leverage
**Source**: SRC-044 [existing]
**Relevance**: Identifies rare earth controls as the core leverage mechanism in the current trade environment

> China's rare earth chokehold gives Beijing leverage that no other targeted economy possesses. For the remaining fifteen economies under Section 301 scrutiny, the calculus is grimmer: they must negotiate from positions of structural weakness, offering concessions on market access, regulatory alignment, or strategic procurement in exchange for tariff relief.

### [Q2-006] CSIS: Digital Regulation as Negotiable Trade Concession
**Source**: SRC-072 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows how U.S. pressure transforms regulatory autonomy into a trade concession, revealing leverage asymmetry for smaller economies

> For smaller economies, the calculation is straightforward: accept restrictions on future digital regulation in exchange for immediate tariff relief. Indonesia, with a GDP of around $1.4 trillion in 2024, is the largest economy to accept such clauses so far. [...] The approach treats digital regulation as purely a trade issue. Such a framing works for small economies seeking tariff relief, but breaks down when countries pursue what they see as legitimate governance goals.

**Assessment**: This question is well-answered with both new and existing sources. The sources demonstrate that China has the most potent leverage (critical minerals), Brazil has mid-range leverage (diplomatic, legislative, corporate), and smaller economies have limited leverage (mainly market access and geopolitical alignment). A gap remains in systematic quantification of leverage for non-China Global South economies.

## Question 2: How effectively has China deployed its rare earth and critical mineral export controls (gallium, germanium, antimony, rare earth elements, permanent magnets) as leverage in trade negotiations? What are the limits of this leverage?

### [Q2-007] Game Theory Analysis of China's Rare Earth Strategy
**Source**: SRC-400 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides rigorous game-theoretic framework for understanding why China favors temporary over permanent export restrictions

> China's rare earth export controls resemble a "one-shot bazooka" — highlighting both the potency and inherent limitations of such a strategy. [...] The game theory arguments suggest China's current export controls are not designed for long-term withdrawal but are a calculated, strategic play in a repeated game. [...] China's only path to maintaining long-term dominance in the REE market rests on avoiding broad export bans and sustaining cooperative international relations.

### [Q2-008] The Pause as Strategic Retention of Leverage
**Source**: SRC-203 [new]
**Relevance**: Demonstrates the tactical nature of China's rare earth "pause" — delaying rather than canceling controls to maintain future options

> China's decision to delay the new measures, rather than canceling them, showcases its desire to maintain its options in its relations with Washington. [...] However, Beijing has maintained the rare earth licensing system that went into effect on April 4, 2025. This system requires exporters to gain case-by-case permission to export seven rare earth elements and permanent magnet materials and effectively cuts off the flow of these materials to Western military manufacturers.

### [Q2-009] China's Application of FDPR to Rare Earths
**Source**: SRC-201 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how China adopted Washington's own extraterritorial tool (foreign direct product rule) to extend rare earth controls globally

> The new export controls mark the first time China has applied the foreign direct product rule (FDPR) — a mechanism introduced in 1959 and long used by Washington to restrict semiconductor exports to China. Under the measures, foreign firms will now be required to obtain Chinese government approval to export magnets that contain even trace amounts of Chinese-origin rare earth materials — or that were produced using Chinese mining, processing, or magnet-making technologies.

### [Q2-010] Limits of Leverage: Third-Country Reexport Loophole
**Source**: SRC-300 [new]
**Relevance**: Reveals a key limit of China's mineral leverage — materials continue to reach the U.S. through third-country reexport

> Though Chinese data shows zero exports of germanium and gallium to the United States in 2024, U.S. trade data shows 26% of its germanium imports (about 3,500kg) and 8% of its gallium imports (900kg) still came from China in 2024. [...] Belgium is the most likely source of reexported germanium. The volume of China's exports of germanium to Belgium increased roughly 224% in 2024.

### [Q2-011] Limits of Leverage: Long-Term Strategic Losses
**Source**: SRC-400 [new]
**Relevance**: Explains why sustained export bans would ultimately undermine China's own position

> From a strategic perspective, the longer-term loser in this scenario would be China. Its investments in downstream REE processing infrastructure, estimated to be worth billions, would face underutilization due to smaller export markets. [...] The Department of Defense invested $400 million in MP Materials with a 10-year price floor commitment of $110/kg — about $50/kg higher than its spot price. Thus, though the DoD ensures its supply of magnets for the next decade, it comes at a high financial cost to the country.

### [Q2-012] Gallium as an Even More Acute Vulnerability than Rare Earths
**Source**: SRC-302 [new]
**Relevance**: Establishes that gallium may be a more potent leverage tool than rare earths

> China controls approximately 98 percent of global gallium production capacity. [...] Unlike rare earths, where some alternative supply exists in countries like Australia, there are virtually no near-term alternatives for gallium outside of China. Secondary production (recycling) accounts for less than 10 percent of global supply. New mining projects could take 5-7 years to reach meaningful production levels.

### [Q2-013] Paris Trade Talks and Rare Earth as Negotiation Topic
**Source**: SRC-073 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows rare earths being directly used as a negotiation card in the Paris trade talks ahead of Trump-Xi summit

> The Paris talks will tackle what both sides consider the central friction point: rare earth export controls. Washington wants China to fully restore rare earth supplies, while Beijing sees the controls as its most potent bargaining chip.

### [Q2-014] Rare Earth Assurances as Summit Stakes
**Source**: SRC-046 [existing]
**Relevance**: Confirms U.S. seeks rare earth assurances as key deliverable, validating China's leverage

> The U.S. wants assurances on rare earth supplies ahead of the Beijing summit. China's export controls on rare earths have emerged as perhaps the most consequential leverage point in the entire trade dispute.

**Assessment**: This question is thoroughly answered. The sources establish that China's mineral leverage is potent but self-limiting: effective for tactical pressure and negotiation concessions, but sustained use risks triggering permanent supply chain diversification that undermines China's dominant position. The game theory analysis from RFF is particularly valuable.

## Question 3: What evidence exists for collective bargaining or coordination among targeted economies? Consider both formal mechanisms (ASEAN Geoeconomics Task Force, BRICS, WTO coalitions) and informal coordination patterns.

### [Q2-015] ASEAN's Failed Attempt at Collective Response
**Source**: SRC-202 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the creation and weakness of ASEAN's Geoeconomics Task Force as a coordination mechanism

> While ASEAN initially projected a rare image of defiance and solidarity, that unity lasted only weeks before the bloc's two most export-dependent members, Vietnam and Cambodia, were first to break ranks to chase bilateral side deals with Washington. [...] Dr. Nixon urges ASEAN's Geoeconomics Task Force, created after the announcement of Trump's "Liberation Day" trade policy, and national policymakers to take a more proactive and coordinated approach.

### [Q2-016] Divide and Rule: The Collective Action Problem
**Source**: SRC-301 [new]
**Relevance**: Explains the game-theoretic mechanism by which Trump exploited ASEAN's structural weaknesses

> Trump's tactics were simple and effective — drop a massive bomb, draw back and make your opponent come to you with offers. The tactic leveraged a collective action problem — ASEAN could form a major grouping if unified but by dealing with individual states, Trump induced them to bid against one another. Once one country broke ranks, all trust was lost and it was every state for themselves.

### [Q2-017] The Chronology of ASEAN Defection
**Source**: SRC-301 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides precise timeline of how ASEAN unity collapsed through sequential bilateral deals

> On 13 April, the ASEAN chair, Malaysian PM Anwar Ibrahim, announced that ASEAN would act as a bloc on US tariffs. But Anwar's pronouncement had already been undercut. Even before Trump unveiled his tariffs, Vietnam preemptively announced on 26 March that it would cut tariff rates. [...] Cambodia followed suit the same day. On 8 April, Indonesia announced tariff cuts. Thailand followed suit on 8 April. [...] Trump's bilateral negotiating strategy first bore fruit with Vietnam cutting a deal on 3 July, followed by Indonesia on 16 July and the Philippines on 24 July.

### [Q2-018] Global South Institutional Failure Across All Regions
**Source**: SRC-205 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows that coordination failure was not limited to ASEAN but occurred across all Global South regional institutions

> In Southeast Asia, ASEAN countries did not negotiate as a bloc, preferring bilateral talks with the United States. In South Asia, all hopes of a united front ended with the clash between Pakistan and India. In South America, Mercosur has not been able to present a united front. The lack of unity was especially evident at the BRICS summit in Rio de Janeiro in early July 2025.

### [Q2-019] Brazil's Multilateral Strategy as Alternative to Bloc Coordination
**Source**: SRC-204 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows Brazil pursuing pragmatic alliances and diversification rather than formal bloc coordination

> Brazil's foreign policy of strengthening multilateralism through pragmatic alliances has created political conditions that prevent it from being entirely subordinate to Trump's wishes. [...] States must concurrently defend their institutional autonomy and coordinate with like-minded partners to reconstruct collective mechanisms capable of restraining the unilateral weaponisation of trade.

### [Q2-020] ASEAN Individual Country Responses
**Source**: SRC-076 [existing]
**Relevance**: Details individual ASEAN member state responses, revealing absence of coordination

> Malaysia announced it would fast-track a bilateral trade deal. Indonesia signaled willingness to increase purchases of US agricultural products. Thailand and Vietnam accelerated individual negotiations. Cambodia signed a new bilateral framework at a 19% rate. The absence of a coordinated ASEAN response is striking.

### [Q2-021] WTO Bypassed as Coordination Forum
**Source**: SRC-071 [existing]
**Relevance**: Confirms that the U.S. deliberately bypasses multilateral forums, undermining potential coordination mechanisms

> Mr Greer informed members of the Senate Finance Committee that the US would not hesitate to use Section 301 against countries that are allegedly denying market access to American goods and companies. He almost ignored the World Trade Organization either in resolving trade disputes or securing new market access through multilateral trade negotiations.

**Assessment**: This question is well-answered. The evidence overwhelmingly shows that collective bargaining has failed across all Global South regions. ASEAN's Geoeconomics Task Force was created but rendered ineffective by rapid bilateral defections. BRICS, Mercosur, and South Asian institutions all failed to coordinate. The only partially successful multilateral strategy has been Brazil's pragmatic alliance-building approach. A gap remains in documenting any behind-the-scenes informal coordination that may not be publicly visible.

## Question 4: What historical precedents exist for successful multilateral resistance to unilateral US trade pressure? Analyze the Smoot-Hawley retaliation pattern, the 1980s semiconductor disputes, and 2018-2019 tariff responses for lessons applicable to the current situation.

### [Q2-022] Smoot-Hawley: Retaliation Spiral Precedent
**Source**: SRC-015 [existing]
**Relevance**: Provides the classic historical precedent for coordinated retaliation against unilateral U.S. tariffs

> The Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act of 1930 triggered a global trade war. More than 60 countries imposed retaliatory tariffs on U.S. goods. U.S. imports fell by 66% and exports by 61% between 1929 and 1933. The lesson: blanket tariffs invite collective retaliation that harms the initiator.

### [Q2-023] 1980s Semiconductor War: Bilateral Capitulation Pattern
**Source**: SRC-016 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows how bilateral U.S. pressure on Japan in the 1980s led to capitulation rather than collective resistance, a precedent for current dynamics

> The 1986 Semiconductor Trade Agreement forced Japan to guarantee U.S. companies 20% of its domestic chip market. Japan, negotiating bilaterally rather than as part of a coalition, had no choice but to accept terms that restructured its entire semiconductor industry.

### [Q2-024] 2025-2026: Only Major Powers Retaliated
**Source**: SRC-063 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents that in the current round, only EU, China, India, Russia, Turkey, and Canada mounted formal retaliations

> The EU activated its Anti-Coercion Instrument (ACI) for the first time in history. The EU Parliament voted to suspend elements of the transatlantic trade deal. Europe demonstrated that institutional coordination enables credible retaliation.

### [Q2-025] EU's Collective Approach as Successful Model
**Source**: SRC-202 [new]
**Relevance**: Contrasts EU's successful collective negotiation with ASEAN's fragmented bilateral approach

> He argued that a regional, collective negotiation, similar to the European Union's approach, would have given ASEAN far greater leverage. The EU, after all, has remained on a lower tariff rung while offering few concrete concessions.

### [Q2-026] Section 301 Historical Evolution
**Source**: SRC-039 [existing]
**Relevance**: Traces the history of Section 301 as a unilateral trade weapon, showing patterns of escalation and resistance

> The Cato Institute analysis traces Section 301 from its 1974 origins through escalating use. The tool was designed to bypass multilateral dispute resolution. Countries that resisted unilaterally (e.g., Brazil, India in the 1980s) generally found that the U.S. eventually escalated pressure until concessions were made. Countries that coordinated through GATT/WTO had more success defending their positions.

### [Q2-027] Brazil's Historical Section 301 Experience
**Source**: SRC-204 [new]
**Relevance**: Draws explicit comparison between current and historical Section 301 use against Brazil

> The current US strategy represents a significant departure from historical Section 301 investigations of the 1980s and 1990s, which were typically driven by specific corporate lobbies and defined industrial grievances. Instead, the 2025 initiative is characterised by its diffuse and non-technical nature, utilising a heterogeneous list of allegations that mask deeper geopolitical objectives. Most notably, the US administration is explicitly using these economic sanctions as a tool of political disciplining.

### [Q2-028] China's 2010 Rare Earth Embargo Against Japan as Precedent
**Source**: SRC-400 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how China's previous use of rare earth leverage prompted Japanese diversification investment, relevant as a precedent for current dynamics

> In 2010, China ostensibly cut off REE exports to Japan amid a maritime dispute, causing prices for the REEs to rise and prompting Japan to invest in long-term diversification strategies. In practice, however, reductions in exports were minimal. The present actions mirror that playbook: assert dominance, signal resolve, but leave room for reversibility.

**Assessment**: This question is partially answered. The historical precedents (Smoot-Hawley, 1980s semiconductor disputes, 2018-2019 tariff responses, 2010 rare earth embargo) are documented. The key lesson is clear: collective action (EU model) succeeds while bilateral negotiation leads to capitulation (Japan 1986, ASEAN 2025). However, there is a gap in identifying successful Global South collective resistance specifically — the examples of successful resistance are all from major economic powers (EU, China) rather than from developing economy coalitions.

## Question 5: What specific countermeasures and strategic options are available to small and medium economies targeted by Section 301, beyond simply capitulating to US demands? Assess the viability of trade diversification, regional economic integration, retaliatory tariffs, WTO challenges, and alignment with alternative economic blocs.

### [Q2-029] ASEAN's Available Strategic Options
**Source**: SRC-202 [new]
**Relevance**: Identifies concrete countermeasures: FTA diversification, regional integration, and collective negotiation

> Beyond engagement with Washington, ASEAN governments have accelerated efforts to diversify trade and investment partnerships. Negotiations for FTAs with the EU have been revived in Malaysia, Indonesia, Singapore and the Philippines. Vietnam has begun exploring the possibility of a FTA with the Mercosur bloc in South America. [...] Preemptive monitoring, regional contingency planning and collective negotiation mechanisms are essential.

### [Q2-030] Critical Minerals as Bargaining Chips for Middle Powers
**Source**: SRC-205 [new]
**Relevance**: Identifies critical mineral access and non-economic cooperation as specific countermeasures available to Global South states

> With control over key resources such as rare earths and oil, Global South states may utilise existing frameworks such as the WTO and withhold American access to critical minerals as a condition for a lifting of trade tariffs. [...] Other states have also pursued the use of labour and other non-economic means as a means to get the Trump Administration to reduce tariffs. [...] The cocktail of resources, cooperation over non-economic issues, and geopolitical support can help bolster the hand of the Global South.

### [Q2-031] Deepening Intra-Regional and South-South Trade
**Source**: SRC-301 [new]
**Relevance**: Proposes specific alternative pathways — intra-ASEAN trade and ASEAN-GCC cooperation

> While there are no quick fixes, a couple of reasonable pathways would be to deepen intra-ASEAN trade and investment and expand ASEAN-GCC cooperation. The latter provides unique complementarity — ASEAN states can find fertile markets for agricultural exports, imports and investment for oil and gas.

### [Q2-032] Brazil's Legislative Reciprocity Framework
**Source**: SRC-204 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents a specific, replicable countermeasure — domestic legislation authorizing calibrated retaliation

> In April 2025 Congress approved the Economic Reciprocity Law (Lei da Reciprocidade Econômica), which established criteria for suspending trade concessions, providing a legal mechanism to retaliate against "unfair or abusive" international practices. Although this institutional shield has never been used, it has allowed President Lula's direct diplomacy to confront the tariff war with a defined legal framework for reciprocity.

### [Q2-033] EU-Mercosur as Geopolitical Diversification Strategy
**Source**: SRC-204 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how trade diversification beyond the US serves as a structural countermeasure

> Pushing forward the EU-Mercosur agreement is a geopolitical strategy that transcends trade and helps move away from the false dichotomy of choosing between the US and China. [...] In the short term, the agreement will not significantly impact the existing asymmetric commercial relations, although it could facilitate a closer political partnership between the blocs.

### [Q2-034] Five-Point Strategy for Economic Sovereignty
**Source**: SRC-204 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides a systematic framework of five strategic options applicable to all targeted economies

> First, sovereign governments should prioritise strengthening domestic legislative and legal frameworks that facilitate calibrated reciprocity. Second, high-level diplomacy must be integrated into a comprehensive strategy that mobilises domestic economic actors. Third, central to ensuring long-term economic security is the proactive diversification of trade, investment, and political partnerships. Fourth, states must coordinate with like-minded partners to reconstruct collective mechanisms. Fifth, business diplomacy will often be able to speak much more forcefully than official diplomacy.

### [Q2-035] South-South Trade as Substitute for Lost US Market
**Source**: SRC-205 [new]
**Relevance**: Quantifies the South-South trade alternative

> UNCTAD has reported that South-South trade has more than doubled from US$2.3 trillion in 2007 to US$5.6 trillion in 2023. Although South-South coordination may not be able to reduce US tariffs, it can work to coordinate meaningful tariff systems between nations to enable South-South trade to replace lost trade with the United States.

### [Q2-036] Bilateral Deals as Flawed Countermeasure
**Source**: SRC-072 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents how bilateral concessions on digital regulation are a form of capitulation disguised as countermeasure

> The United States has embedded anti-regulation clauses into bilateral trade agreements and frameworks with at least nine countries across Latin America, Southeast Asia, and Europe. [...] For smaller economies, the calculation is straightforward: accept restrictions on future digital regulation in exchange for immediate tariff relief.

### [Q2-037] India's Reassessment as Alternative Strategy
**Source**: SRC-075 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows how India reassessed its trade deal approach after the Supreme Court IEEPA ruling changed the leverage landscape

> India sees the Supreme Court ruling as an opportunity to renegotiate from a stronger position. New Delhi is reassessing the concessions it offered during tariff negotiations, recognizing that the legal landscape has shifted in ways that could reduce U.S. leverage.

### [Q2-038] Structural Weakness of Non-Retaliation
**Source**: SRC-044 [existing]
**Relevance**: Establishes the fundamental leverage asymmetry: only China has unique countermeasures, other economies must capitulate

> For the remaining fifteen economies under Section 301 scrutiny, the calculus is grimmer: they must negotiate from positions of structural weakness, offering concessions on market access, regulatory alignment, or strategic procurement in exchange for tariff relief. The irony is cruel: the very economies that signed bilateral deals to escape IEEPA tariffs now face new investigations.

**Assessment**: This question is well-answered. The sources identify a comprehensive menu of countermeasures: (1) critical mineral leverage (for those who have it), (2) legislative reciprocity frameworks (Brazil model), (3) trade diversification through new FTAs, (4) South-South trade expansion, (5) collective negotiation (EU model), (6) corporate/business lobby mobilization, and (7) non-economic cooperation concessions. The key finding is that no single countermeasure is sufficient — the most effective approach is Brazil's three-level strategy combining diplomatic, legislative, and corporate dimensions. A gap remains in assessing the viability of WTO challenges specifically, as the sources consistently describe WTO as being bypassed rather than utilized.
