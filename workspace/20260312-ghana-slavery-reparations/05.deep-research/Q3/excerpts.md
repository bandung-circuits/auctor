# Working-Class, Grassroots, and Diaspora Perspectives on Reparations — Research Excerpts

## Question: How do organised labour movements across Africa (NUMSA, other trade unions, workers' federations) and the Caribbean engage with the reparations demand — do they frame it differently from governmental positions, and do they link it to domestic economic struggles like nationalisation and labour rights?

### [Q3-001] ITUC-Africa frames reparations as transformative justice for workers
**Source**: SRC-Q3-001 [new]
**Relevance**: Direct statement from Africa's largest trade union confederation on reparations, showing how labour frames the issue differently from governments

> "For African workers, this call for reparative justice resonates deeply. The African labour movement has always stood at the forefront of the fight for dignity, equity, and economic justice. We are resolute and committed to waging the struggle for justice because we see, know and feel the consequences of historical injustice that are still with us—in underdeveloped labour markets, weakened institutions, exploitative trade arrangements, modern slavery, neo-colonial relations and economies structured around extraction rather than empowerment."

> "Reparations must not be reduced to mere symbolic gestures or abstract debates. They must encompass concrete commitments to transform the structures that continue to disadvantage African people and their descendants across the globe. This necessitates access to fair wages, quality education, universal social protection, decent work opportunities, and control over Africa's resources."

> "As trade unions, we see reparations not as a historical footnote. As a transformative agency for justice and development, we are committed to ensuring that reparations become a tool for transforming systems, not maintaining them. Of course, we know that reparation is a struggle and not a tea party."

### [Q3-002] ITUC-Africa's specific demands link reparations to labour and industrial policy
**Source**: SRC-Q3-001 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how ITUC-Africa's reparations demands go beyond government positions to include labour-specific structural transformation

> ITUC-Africa's demands include: "Reparative economic justice: Through fair trade regimes, living wages, debt cancellation, and universal social protections"; "Reinforced industrial policies — should create decent employment for Africa's youth and women and empower local industries over extractive interests"; "Enhanced labour protection: We call on African governments and global institutions to end informalisation, uphold labour rights, and enable unions to organise and represent all categories of workers, including those in the informal and platform economies"; "Climate justice"; "Continental integration: Our development strategy must involve dissolving artificial colonial boundaries."

### [Q3-003] NUMSA's Irvin Jim links colonialism of a special type to demand for nationalisation
**Source**: SRC-Q3-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly shows how South Africa's most radical trade union links colonial economic structures to contemporary demands for nationalisation

> "The structure of South African capitalism is still the structure of colonialism of a special type," Jim said. "The white colonialists who cohabited with the colonised majority joined forces with imperial capital to super-exploit black and particularly African workers in order to extract and export the country's mineral wealth."

> "At NUMSA we have consistently maintained that the white racist and colonial capitalist economic foundation of South Africa needs to be destroyed if the most immediate needs of all the people of South Africa are to be met: the establishment of a truly non-racial and democratic South Africa. To do this requires fundamental change to take place in the South African economy and society."

### [Q3-004] Cosatu shop stewards survey reveals grassroots appetite for nationalisation
**Source**: SRC-Q3-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Rare survey data showing rank-and-file trade union members' views, linking worker consciousness to demands for structural transformation

> "The survey has found that Cosatu shop stewards want nationalisation, they have no confidence in the South African Communist Party, and they want Cosatu to form a labour party."

### [Q3-005] Caribbean radical critique: reparations movement must put capitalism on trial
**Source**: SRC-Q3-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how Caribbean working-class intellectuals frame reparations differently from CARICOM governments, linking it to class struggle

> "Why is the reparations movement in the Anglophone Caribbean not putting capitalism on trial in its campaign to force British imperialism to provide financial compensation for its industrial and agricultural capitalists' enslavement of Africans?"

> "Today, we are witnessing the unconscionable, but politically understandable behaviour of the neocolonial states in the Caribbean Community (CARICOM) in divorcing their call for reparations from measures aimed at throwing capitalism into the cesspool of history. These member states of CARICOM are all committed to the implementation of social, economic and political policies that have enshrined capitalism in the region. They are interested in reparations as a way to deal with their balance of payment, budgetary and development challenges."

> "Radical and revolutionary reparations activists and supporters have no business not putting capitalism on the stand in their activism... the capitalism that exploited the labour of enslaved Africans is the same capitalism that exploited them as wage slaves after the end of slavery."

### [Q3-006] ITUC-Africa formally joins Africans Rising pan-African grassroots movement
**Source**: SRC-Q3-001 [new], Africans Rising website [existing reference]
**Relevance**: Shows institutional linkage between organised labour and pan-African grassroots movements

> ITUC-Africa, the continent's largest organisation representing workers with 16 million declared members and 103 affiliated trade union centres in 51 African countries, joined the Africans Rising movement, stating: "ITUC-Africa believes that the Africans Rising Movement can make essential contribution to the attainment of our goals of decent work and decent life for all."

**Assessment**: This question is substantially answered. We have direct evidence from ITUC-Africa (Africa's continental trade union body), NUMSA (South Africa's most radical union), and Caribbean working-class critics showing that labour movements frame reparations differently from governments — linking them to structural economic transformation, nationalisation, labour rights, and anti-capitalist politics. The CARICOM governments' approach is explicitly criticised by Caribbean radicals for not challenging capitalism. A gap remains in documenting specific African national trade unions' positions beyond NUMSA and ITUC-Africa.

## Question: What do opinion surveys, public discussions, and grassroots organising reveal about how ordinary African citizens — not governments or intellectuals — view the reparations issue and what forms of justice they prioritise?

### [Q3-007] Senegal's young voters demand reckoning with colonial past
**Source**: SRC-H-003 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows how grassroots demands for reparations manifest in electoral politics in Africa

> "This trend extends beyond the Sahel's 'coup belt' to countries where disillusioned young voters are increasingly vocal in asking for a reassessment of relations with western powers. A case in point is Senegal: generally seen as the voice of younger generations asking for systemic change, Bassirou Diomaye Faye won the 2024 elections on a strongly sovereigntist platform, with an emphasis on rewriting the country's colonial history."

### [Q3-008] Sahelian junta governments channel popular anti-colonial sentiment
**Source**: SRC-H-003 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows how grassroots anti-colonial sentiment is channelled through non-democratic actors

> "In some Sahelian states, where a wave of coups brought new military juntas to power between 2021 and 2023, the request of reparations has featured among the recriminations moved against western partners... In Niger, for example, following the withdrawal of western troops, renegotiation of mining contracts, and closure of media outlets, junta leader Abdurahmane Tchiani called on France to pay compensation for 'over a century of colonial and neo-colonial plundering.'"

### [Q3-009] Reparations debate is more Caribbean than African at grassroots level
**Source**: SRC-G-002 [existing]
**Relevance**: Direct observation about the gap between government positions and grassroots engagement on the continent

> "Njahira Wangari, a Kenyan scholar and educator, noted that the reparation debate is more talked about in the Caribbean than in African countries and said the conversation was 'ripe' but ought to be sensitive."

### [Q3-010] Cultural festivals as spaces for grassroots reparations engagement
**Source**: SRC-G-002 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows how cultural events are being used to engage ordinary Africans on reparations

> "Kyeretwie Osei, the head of programmes at the Economic, Social and Cultural Council, the AU's civil society policy organ, said arts and culture would make more people care about the reparation debate and their involvement would help build momentum for the movement. 'You need pressure points from both sides,' he said. 'We need everyone on board and so people in the creative sector are really important in constructing the necessary narrative and building a story that will resonate among people.'"

### [Q3-011] Black diaspora social media reactions to Ghana resolution
**Source**: SRC-H-004 [existing]
**Relevance**: Captures how ordinary Black people online responded to the reparations push

> "Online, Black folks have expressed support for the initiative and dismay that it has taken so long. 'The fact that the transatlantic slave trade... is still not formally recognized as a crime against humanity is ridiculous,' Lisa Stevens wrote on X. The post was liked more than 2,500 times."

> "Another user, who went by Mrs. Cole, wrote about her trip to Ghana. 'I literally went to Ghana back in May, and when I tell you once you walk in, you can feel the death around you, it was stifling... and [people] have the nerve to say get over it.'"

### [Q3-012] UK public opinion: growing support despite knowledge gap
**Source**: SRC-Q3-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Most systematic polling data available on public attitudes toward reparations (UK population)

> "A vast majority (63%) now agree that Caribbean nations and descendants of enslaved people should receive a formal apology, up four percentage points from a similar poll. Support for financial reparations has also increased, with 40% now in favour, again a four-point increase on last year."

> "85% did not know that more than 3 million people were forcibly shipped from Africa to the Caribbean by Britain during the transatlantic slave trade. 89% were unaware that Britain enslaved people in the Caribbean for over 300 years."

> "90% of those in favour of financial reparations agree they should be directed towards long-term education, health, and infrastructure projects."

**Assessment**: This question is only partially answered. There is a significant gap: no systematic opinion survey of African citizens' views on reparations exists in the collected materials. The closest proxy is the Senegal election result and Sahelian anti-colonial sentiment channelled through military juntas. The UK poll provides useful comparative data but is not about African citizens. The Cosatu shop stewards survey touches workers' views but is about nationalisation, not reparations specifically. The Afrobarometer has not yet included reparations-specific questions. This is an honest gap in available evidence.

## Question: How has the African diaspora in Europe and the Americas engaged with the Ghana resolution — through what organisations, campaigns, and political demands, and is their framing aligned with or distinct from the AU's state-to-state approach?

### [Q3-013] Pan-Afrikan Reparations Coalition in Europe: grassroots diaspora framing
**Source**: SRC-Q3-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Detailed account of the principal diaspora reparations organisation in Europe, showing how its framing differs radically from the AU's state-to-state approach

> "In the Pan-Afrikan Reparations Coalition in Europe, we recognise the economics of reparations, but only insofar as 'receiving the financial component of reparations will be meaningful only if it serves the holistic purpose and strengthens the integral whole of our self-repair process'."

> "Concretely, this entails first and foremost the urgent need for Pan-Afrikan Reparations and other Global Justice Movements to compel the stopping of neocolonialism and its inbuilt manifestations of genocide, ecocide and extractivist plunder."

> The diaspora framing goes "beyond mere compensation which, as Robin Kelley argues, does not challenge the terms of racial capitalism, but rather reinforces neoliberalism and capitalism including the logic of property rights and compensation without radical transformation."

### [Q3-014] Stop the Maangamizi Campaign: UK diaspora's demand for parliamentary inquiry
**Source**: SRC-Q3-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows concrete political demand distinct from the AU approach — demanding a UK parliamentary commission of inquiry

> "A vital mechanism in achieving this is the demand for the establishment of the UK All-Party Parliamentary Commission of Inquiry for Truth and Reparatory Justice, as part of a global process of dialogue between Afrikan people and state institutions of perpetrators of the Maangamizi."

> "Building on this impetus... the All-Party Parliamentary Group for African Reparations (APPGAR) was launched on 20 October 2021. The significance of this parliamentary group is that it is the first space created within the state institutions of the UK for dialogue in pursuit of holistic Afrikan reparations."

### [Q3-015] Diaspora-driven local government motions in UK
**Source**: SRC-Q3-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows bottom-up political organising in Europe, distinct from AU's top-down approach

> "Reparations and Atonement for the Transatlantic Trafficking of Enslaved Africans motions... have now been passed by Islington and Lambeth Council and Bristol City Council. The key purpose of these motions is to build glocal support at the local and city council level for the Stop the Maangamizi Campaign's demand for the UK Parliament to establish the All-Party Parliamentary Commission of Inquiry for Truth and Reparatory Justice."

### [Q3-016] Diaspora crossover into climate justice movement
**Source**: SRC-Q3-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how diaspora reparations organising intersects with other social movements in ways the AU approach does not

> "The Pan-Afrikan Reparations Coalition in Europe took reparations into the Environmental Movement here in the UK through the Stop the Maangamizi Campaign which co-founded the Extinction Rebellion Internationalist Solidarity Network soon after the inception of XR in 2018."

### [Q3-017] The Repair Campaign: moderate diaspora ally focuses on education
**Source**: SRC-Q3-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows a different, more moderate diaspora-aligned approach working with CARICOM

> The Repair Campaign, "in partnership with the CARICOM Reparations Commission, is focused on securing reparatory justice for the Caribbean... the campaign works to amplify Caribbean voices, foster meaningful dialogue, and develop evidence-based socio-economic plans to address the enduring effects of slavery and colonialism."

### [Q3-018] Ghana's "13th ethnic group" designation for diaspora
**Source**: SRC-G-002 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows Ghana's specific approach to diaspora engagement as part of the reparations push

> "Ghana... which marked 2019 as the Year of Return to commemorate the 400th anniversary of the first enslaved Africans arriving in Virginia, has positioned the diaspora as its '13th ethnic group'."

### [Q3-019] CARICOM Reparations Commission visit to UK
**Source**: SRC-J-003 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows Caribbean institutional engagement with UK diaspora and government

> "In November, the Caricom Reparations Commission, led by Sir Hilary Beckles, visited the UK. The delegation met civil society groups, academics, churches, activists and some parliamentarians. The reception at Westminster was distinctly underwhelming."

### [Q3-020] Open Society Foundations supporting reparations movement infrastructure
**Source**: SRC-H-006 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows how diaspora engagement is being institutionally supported across continents

> "In 2021, the Open Society Foundations stepped into supporting the work on reparations... We created a new, multi-tiered funding approach that allowed us to support the strengthening of the global reparations movement's infrastructure, help make it sustainable, and resource efforts connecting work across and between Africa, Latin America, the Caribbean, the UK, and the United States."

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. The evidence shows a clear distinction between the AU's state-to-state approach and the diaspora's grassroots framing. The diaspora organisations (PARCOE, Stop the Maangamizi Campaign, APPGAR) frame reparations as inseparable from Pan-Afrikan liberation, anti-capitalism, and ecological justice — a much more radical framing than the AU's diplomatic resolution. The Repair Campaign represents a moderate, CARICOM-aligned approach. There is some alignment (both seek justice for slavery) but the diaspora organisations explicitly critique the state-centric approach as insufficient.

## Question: What is the relationship between domestic economic policy demands (nationalisation, land reform, industrial policy) and international reparations claims in countries like South Africa, where Irvin Jim argues colonial economic structures persist? Are these parallel struggles or parts of a single programme?

### [Q3-021] NUMSA explicitly links domestic nationalisation to colonial structure
**Source**: SRC-Q3-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Direct evidence that NUMSA treats domestic economic transformation and anti-colonial struggle as a single programme

> "At NUMSA we have consistently maintained that the white racist and colonial capitalist economic foundation of South Africa needs to be destroyed if the most immediate needs of all the people of South Africa are to be met."

> "The structure of South African capitalism is still the structure of colonialism of a special type... The white colonialists who cohabited with the colonised majority joined forces with imperial capital to super-exploit black and particularly African workers in order to extract and export the country's mineral wealth."

### [Q3-022] NUMSA critique links NDP to preservation of colonial structures
**Source**: SRC-Q3-005 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how NUMSA frames the government's economic plan as perpetuating colonial economic structures

> "According to Numsa, South Africa has fallen victim to a peculiar form of colonialism, where the largely black working class was colonised internally by a largely white elite, and these exploitative relations continue to this day."

> "They argue that the continued existence of exploitative practices from apartheid to a democracy '...serve as clear evidence that the NDR as the most direct route to socialism, is completely off track, and in fact has been abandoned in favour of a capitalist post-apartheid South Africa'."

### [Q3-023] Land dispossession and labour exploitation as interlinked colonial legacies
**Source**: SRC-Q3-007 [new]
**Relevance**: Academic analysis linking land reform and labour rights as two sides of the same colonial coin

> "The historical link between land loss and subjugation by means of the controls inherent to the contract of employment makes land reform a necessary first step to reversing this process."

> "The concentration of land ownership and wealth in the hands of the white minority remains largely intact, perpetuating socio-economic inequality."

> "It underpins current debates about social justice, reparations and the transformation of economic structures."

### [Q3-024] UN Special Adviser: reparations must transform existing economic systems
**Source**: SRC-D-007 [existing]
**Relevance**: International framing that bridges domestic and international dimensions

> "Reparations must not be seen merely as financial compensation for past events. They must be understood as a call to transform the very rules of the game—the international trade, finance, and governance systems that have perpetuated injustice for centuries."

> "Ghana exported $9.58 billion in gold in 2024, yet it only retained 14% of the value due to the nature of multinational agreements."

### [Q3-025] Caribbean radical: reparations must include worker self-management
**Source**: SRC-Q3-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how Caribbean working-class framing treats reparations and domestic economic transformation as inseparable

> "The struggle for reparations in the Caribbean should become a site of the class struggle and organizing the people for socialism or communism. Capitalism must be put on trial for aiding and abetting the enslavement of Africans and genocide against the Indigenous peoples."

> Proposals include: "Promote labour self-management and economic democracy through worker cooperatives"; "Develop comprehensive land reform programme"; "Establish working-class friendly labour laws."

### [Q3-026] ECA head: reparations must address Africa's position in global value chains
**Source**: SRC-I-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows institutional framing linking domestic industrial policy to international reparations

> "This means dismantling the incentives that consign African countries to export cocoa beans instead of chocolate, lithium instead of electric batteries, or crude oil instead of petrochemicals. Reparations must empower Africa to generate and retain value, not surrender it."

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. The evidence consistently shows that for labour movements and radical analysts, domestic economic demands (nationalisation, land reform, industrial policy) and international reparations claims are parts of a single programme, not parallel struggles. NUMSA explicitly treats the two as inseparable through the CST framework. Caribbean radicals make the same argument. The AU's institutional framing (through the ECA and UN advisers) increasingly converges on this view by arguing that reparations must transform existing economic structures, not merely transfer funds within them.

## Question: How does the concept of "colonialism of a special type" — where colonial economic structures persist after formal political transition — apply beyond South Africa to other African states with concentrated economic ownership?

### [Q3-027] SACP's original CST formulation
**Source**: SRC-Q3-005 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides the original theoretical framework for CST

> "According to Numsa, South Africa has fallen victim to a peculiar form of colonialism, where the largely black working class was colonised internally by a largely white elite, and these exploitative relations continue to this day. This is unlike other classic colonies, where an external invading force colonises an indigenous population: hence the South African version being called Colonialism of a Special Type (CST)."

### [Q3-028] Critique: CST may obscure capitalist relations
**Source**: SRC-Q3-005 [new]
**Relevance**: Important analytical critique of whether CST can be generalised to other African contexts

> "The CST analysis, which is also classic SACP doctrine, is also flawed as it misconstrues the nature of South African society. CST represented a tortured attempt by the SACP to shoe-horn the South African struggle into the Comintern's dictates for colonial countries. By arguing that racial divisions trumped all other, CST ignored the fact that the dominant relations in South Africa were capitalist."

### [Q3-029] Persistent colonial economic structures in other African states
**Source**: SRC-D-007 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows how the structural logic of CST applies across Africa even without the specific term

> "Africa remains trapped in a global economic system characterized by the permanent extraction of value through the so-called 'commodity trap'. Ghana exported $9.58 billion in gold in 2024, yet it only retained 14% of the value... The DRC produces over 70% of the world's cobalt, yet only 1% is refined in the country... Collectively, West Africa produces 70% of the world's cacao beans but contributes less than 1% of the global chocolate market."

### [Q3-030] Sahelian states frame continued French control as neocolonialism
**Source**: SRC-H-003 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows how the CST logic applies to Francophone Africa through the lens of the CFA franc and French economic control

> "In some Sahelian states, where a wave of coups brought new military juntas to power between 2021 and 2023, the request of reparations has featured among the recriminations moved against western partners... European countries, and especially France, received bitter criticism, for, among other things, perpetuating perceived neocolonial practices, including a persistent military presence on African soil and the use of the colonial-era CFA franc."

### [Q3-031] South African land ownership concentration persists
**Source**: SRC-Q3-007 [new]
**Relevance**: Concrete evidence of persistent colonial economic structures in South Africa

> "The concentration of land ownership and wealth in the hands of the white minority remains largely intact, perpetuating socio-economic inequality."

> "Dispossession created a large pool of cheap labour for white-owned farms and industries. Without access to land for subsistence or commercial farming, black South Africans had little choice but to work for low wages in the capitalist economy."

### [Q3-032] Africa as "net creditor" despite poverty — structural extraction continues
**Source**: SRC-D-007 [existing]
**Relevance**: Shows the structural continuation of colonial extraction across the continent

> "Africa is a net creditor to the world, losing more than $500 billion every year through illicit financial flows, unfair trade practices, exploitative investment frameworks, and debt servicing, while it is home to some of the world's poorest populations."

### [Q3-033] Nkrumah's concept of neocolonialism as precursor to CST beyond South Africa
**Source**: SRC-Q3-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows the intellectual lineage from Nkrumah's neocolonialism analysis to the CST concept, applicable continent-wide

> "From the late 1970s, when neocolonialism became the dominant form of the nation state in Afrika and the Diaspora, the reparatory justice process was expunged out of emerging nation states which became cogs in the wheel of neocolonialism, devoid of any truly self-repairing substance instead of them becoming building blocks of a truly independent Pan-Afrikan Union of States as envisaged by Nkrumah and others."

**Assessment**: This question is partially answered. The CST concept as formally defined applies specifically to South Africa's settler-colonial context where the coloniser and colonised inhabit the same territory. However, the underlying structural logic — that colonial economic relationships persist after formal political independence — is widely documented across Africa through the "commodity trap," illicit financial flows, CFA franc dependency, and persistent foreign control of key economic sectors. The concept does not translate directly to most African states (where the colonial power departed physically), but the analogous concept of neocolonialism (as articulated by Nkrumah) captures the same structural dynamic. A gap remains in finding explicit comparative analyses that test the CST framework against other African states' situations.
