Southeast Asia caught between relief and new risks after US court strikes down Trump tariffs - CNA

Published Feb 26, 2026

Southeast Asian countries could benefit from a temporary lowering of tariffs, but analysts warn there are other means United States could use to apply trade pressure.

SINGAPORE: Southeast Asian economies are caught between a sense of relief and growing uncertainty after the United States Supreme Court struck down President Donald Trump's sweeping tariff regime.

While the ruling temporarily lowers tariffs across the board, analysts warn the respite may be short-lived as Washington pivots to other trade enforcement tools.

"The fundamental direction of US trade policy hasn't changed," said Dr Jayant Menon, senior fellow at the ISEAS-Yusof Ishak Institute. "The administration remains committed to using tariffs as leverage. What's shifted is the legal pathway, not the intent."

The immediate effect of the ruling saw the administration impose a uniform 10% tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act. However, this rate is lower than many of the country-specific tariffs that had been in place — ranging from 24% for Japan to 46% for Vietnam under the reciprocal tariff regime.

"For countries like Vietnam and Cambodia that were facing much higher rates, this is a temporary reprieve," said Siwage Dharma Negara, senior fellow at ISEAS. "But the 301 investigations will determine the next phase."

Several Southeast Asian countries had already signed bilateral trade agreements with Washington that included acceptance of higher tariff rates. The status of those deals is now legally uncertain.

"Countries that rushed to sign deals may find themselves in a worse position," said Dr Menon. "They made concessions — on procurement, on digital trade rules — that may not be reciprocated if the tariff framework changes."

The probe into "excess manufacturing capacity" is particularly concerning for Vietnam and Thailand, which have become major beneficiaries of supply chain shifts out of China. Vietnam's electronics exports to the US grew by over 35% in 2025, while Thailand's automotive parts exports surged.

"The concern is that Section 301 could be used to target precisely the kind of supply chain diversification that these countries have built," said Cassey Lee, senior fellow at ISEAS. "If the US views Southeast Asian manufacturing growth as an extension of Chinese overcapacity, it could apply tariffs that would undermine the region's competitive advantage."

Singapore, despite running a trade deficit with the US, was also included in the probe list — a move that surprised many analysts. "Singapore's inclusion suggests this is as much about signaling as about specific trade grievances," said Dr Menon.

Indonesia and Malaysia face additional complications. Both had signed framework agreements with Washington, and Indonesia had committed to purchasing US agricultural goods and reducing certain non-tariff barriers. Whether those commitments remain enforceable is now an open question.

"The legal uncertainty is the worst outcome for business planning," said Negara. "Companies need predictability to make investment decisions. Right now, they're getting the opposite."

For the region as a whole, the longer-term trajectory depends on whether the US successfully rebuilds tariff walls using Section 301 and Section 232 authorities, and whether China is able to negotiate preferential treatment ahead of the Trump-Xi summit.

"If China gets a better deal than Southeast Asia, it could actually reverse the supply chain diversification trends we've seen over the past few years," said Lee. "That would be a significant setback for the region's industrialization strategy."

Analysts also noted that the forced labor investigation, covering more than 60 countries, could have broader implications for Southeast Asian garment and electronics manufacturers.

"The forced labor dimension adds another layer of risk," said Dr Menon. "Countries like Cambodia and Bangladesh, which rely heavily on labor-intensive manufacturing exports to the US, could face import bans rather than just tariffs."

Despite the uncertainties, some analysts see potential opportunities. If overall US tariff rates settle at lower levels than before the Supreme Court ruling, demand for Southeast Asian exports could actually increase.

"The net effect for ASEAN could be positive if tariffs stay lower overall," said Negara. "But that's a big 'if' given this administration's track record."
