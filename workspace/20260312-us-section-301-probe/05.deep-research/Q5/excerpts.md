# Environmental and Climate Dimensions of the Tariff Regime — Research Excerpts

## Question 1: How do tariffs on solar modules, batteries, and EVs affect the pace and cost of the energy transition — both in the United States and globally? What are the specific price impacts of existing tariffs on renewable energy equipment?

### [Q5-001] Tariff rates on Chinese solar panels have escalated to 175-195%
**Source**: SRC-109 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides specific tariff rate data for solar panels and components

> In January 2025, importers paid a 50 percent tariff on Chinese solar panels and components. Under the series of tariffs from the beginning of the new administration, these rates have increased to 175 percent for finished Chinese solar panels and 195 percent for polysilicon, wafers, and cells. Meanwhile, the new tariffs will apply to two of the United States' largest sources of solar: Vietnam at 46 percent and Cambodia at 49 percent, likely slowing U.S. deployment.

### [Q5-002] McKinsey: Highest tariff scenario reduces US solar capacity by 9% and stalls renewable adoption post-2035
**Source**: SRC-110 [new]
**Relevance**: Quantitative scenario analysis of tariff impacts on clean energy deployment

> Under sustained application of the highest-tariff scenario, by 2035, renewable-energy adoption could stall in the United States, and solar and wind adoption could soften in Europe. The global tensions escalate scenario could result in 9 percent less installed solar capacity in the United States and 7 percent less in the European Union by 2035. BESS capacity could be 4% less in the US and up to 10% less in the EU. Energy system costs could be approximately 2 percent more in the United States and approximately 3 percent more in the European Union by 2050. The adoption of clean-energy technologies will likely take longer and cost more the longer tariffs last and the higher they are.

### [Q5-003] US clean energy import dependency: $24B batteries, 54 GW solar, 70% of wind blades
**Source**: SRC-109 [new]
**Relevance**: Quantifies US dependence on imported clean energy components that tariffs affect

> The International Energy Agency estimates China accounts for between 40 and 98 percent of global manufacturing capacity for each key clean technology and component. China produces over 95 percent of the world's polysilicon wafers. In 2024, the United States imported over 54 gigawatts of solar panels, with the vast majority sourced from Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia, and Cambodia. Domestic manufacturers only supplied about 30 percent of the wind turbine blades installed in the United States in 2024. Last year, the United States imported almost $24 billion in battery cells, primarily from China, Japan, and South Korea.

### [Q5-004] Tesla's tariff cost: $200M in one quarter; 25% tariffs raise wind turbine costs 10%
**Source**: SRC-116 [new], SRC-115 [new]
**Relevance**: Specific cost impacts on companies and technologies

> Tesla's energy storage business tariff costs stood at roughly $200 million in Q3 2025 alone. [SRC-116] A 25% tariff on imports could raise the cost of building onshore wind turbines by 10% and renewable energy overall by 7%. Tariffs could add 15 percent to the average cost of vehicles. [SRC-115]

### [Q5-005] $7.7 billion in clean manufacturing projects canceled in Q1 2025
**Source**: SRC-109 [new]
**Relevance**: Direct evidence of tariffs chilling clean energy investment

> Across all clean manufacturing projects, a total of $7.7 billion was canceled in the first quarter of 2025, up from about $1.8 billion canceled in all of 2024. Similarly, only about $175 million in clean manufacturing projects were announced in January, well down from the typical $1 billion in new monthly projects announced over the last three years.

### [Q5-006] Tariffs delay competitiveness of low-carbon technologies by 1-3 years
**Source**: SRC-112 [new]
**Relevance**: Research-based estimate of how tariffs delay energy transition

> CSIS research showed that more restrictive trade policies can delay the competitiveness of low-carbon technologies against their market rivals: one year for solar compared to natural gas, two years for battery EVs compared to internal combustion engine vehicles, and three years for onshore wind compared to gas. Rising trade frictions didn't overcome the still-falling costs of clean energy.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. Multiple sources provide both specific price impacts (175-195% on Chinese solar, 126% on Indian solar, 46-49% on SE Asian solar) and quantitative assessments of how tariffs slow deployment (9% less solar capacity by 2035, 1-3 year competitiveness delays, $7.7B in canceled projects).

## Question 2: How does the "overcapacity" framing in green technology sectors (solar, batteries, EVs) interact with global climate commitments? Is Chinese "overcapacity" in solar production actually accelerating global decarbonization by driving down costs?

### [Q5-007] China's "overcapacity" exists because global demand remains far below NZE 2050 level
**Source**: SRC-111 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly addresses whether Chinese production capacity is excessive relative to climate goals

> China can already produce enough renewable products for the world's energy transition, even in the most aggressive scenario. In fact, China faces overcapacity because global demand remains far below the NZE 2050 level. In 2024, China supplied 91 percent of the solar modules and 39 percent of wind turbines installed outside China.

### [Q5-008] "There is no overcapacity in cheap renewables until we reach 100% renewables globally"
**Source**: SRC-113 [new]
**Relevance**: Expert rebuttal of overcapacity framing from climate perspective

> From a climate perspective, Professor Elizabeth Thurbon of UNSW argues: "There is no overcapacity in cheap renewables until we reach 100% renewables globally." She noted that the demands of the energy transition are so enormous that countries will inevitably find opportunities for themselves — or as partners with China — to support their own green transitions.

### [Q5-009] China's 60%+ solar cost reductions made Paris Agreement goals achievable
**Source**: SRC-112 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents how Chinese production drove down costs, enabling climate commitments

> The falling costs of clean energy have been crucial in accelerating the energy transition. Rapid reductions in the prices of solar panels, wind turbines, battery storage, and electric vehicles have enabled consumers and businesses to adopt cleaner options at lower costs. These falling costs have put the goals of the Paris Agreement in range. Much of these cost reductions have come from China, where industrial policies have led to a dominant position in the clean energy supply chain.

### [Q5-010] China invested $818 billion in energy transition in 2024 — more than double any other economy
**Source**: SRC-113 [new]
**Relevance**: Scale of China's green investment relative to its climate commitments

> In 2024, China accounted for over 40% of global renewable energy capacity and invested $818 billion in its energy transition — more than double any other economy. China sees the green energy transition as a "massive national security multiplier" — about strengthening energy, economic, environmental, social and geostrategic security.

### [Q5-011] China's "overcapacity" is a "false proposition" used for political manipulation
**Source**: SRC-067 [existing]
**Relevance**: China's official response to overcapacity framing

> China's Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun stated: China's position on China-US economic and trade issues is consistent and clear — opposing all forms of unilateral tariff measures. The so-called "overcapacity" is a false proposition, and China opposes using it as a pretext for political manipulation.

### [Q5-012] Despite 900 GW of renewable capacity 2020-2024, China failed to meet carbon intensity targets
**Source**: SRC-111 [new]
**Relevance**: Complicating factor — Chinese overcapacity doesn't automatically translate to decarbonization

> From 2020 to 2024, China installed an unprecedented 900 GW of renewable capacity, yet failed to reduce energy and carbon intensity as called for in the 14th Five Year Plan. The root cause is widespread underutilisation of installed renewable power capacity and increased curtailment, due to China's underinvestment in the power grid.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. The evidence strongly suggests that Chinese production capacity, when measured against the NZE 2050 scenario, is not excessive but rather necessary for global decarbonization. The "overcapacity" framing is contradicted by the gap between current deployment and what climate science requires. However, the picture is nuanced — even China's massive installation has not met its own carbon intensity targets due to grid infrastructure gaps.

## Question 3: What are the climate policy implications of the Section 301 probe for targeted countries that have built green industrial capacity — particularly China (solar, batteries, EVs), India (solar modules), and Southeast Asian countries hosting green supply chains?

### [Q5-013] India hit with 126% tariff on solar modules — effectively shut out of US market
**Source**: SRC-114 [new]
**Relevance**: Direct impact on India's green industrial capacity from US tariffs

> The US imposed a 126% preliminary countervailing duty on Indian solar cells and modules, effectively closing off the US market. Indian solar imports to the US grew from $84 million in 2022 to nearly $793 million in 2024 under Modi's production-linked incentive scheme. India's solar manufacturing capacity has grown 13-fold since 2020 with current capacity of ~160 GW vastly outpacing annual domestic demand at 45-50 GW. The US accounted for more than 90% of Indian solar exports in 2024.

### [Q5-014] Section 301 probe targets batteries, solar modules in 16 economies including major green producers
**Source**: SRC-065 [existing]
**Relevance**: Official USTR document confirming green technology sectors are targeted

> Several sectors are plagued by excess capacity and production. An illustrative list includes: aluminum, automobiles, batteries, cement, chemicals, electronics, energy goods, glass, machine tools, machinery, paper, plastics, processed food and beverages, robotics, satellites, semiconductors, ships, solar modules, steel, and transportation equipment.

### [Q5-015] USTR specifically cited China's EV overcapacity and BYD's overseas expansion
**Source**: SRC-018 [existing]
**Relevance**: USTR explicitly targeting China's green technology sectors

> USTR said although China's electric-vehicle capacity outstrips national demand, the country's top EV maker, BYD, was "aggressively expanding" its overseas manufacturing footprint, with factories in Uzbekistan, Thailand, Brazil, Hungary and Turkey, and was expected to expand capacity in Europe, where existing automotive plants are operating at only 55% of capacity.

### [Q5-016] Vietnam and Cambodia — hosts of solar supply chains — face 46-49% tariffs
**Source**: SRC-109 [new]
**Relevance**: SE Asian countries that built solar capacity are directly targeted

> The new tariffs will apply to two of the United States' largest sources of solar: Vietnam at 46 percent and Cambodia at 49 percent. In 2024, the United States imported over 54 gigawatts of solar panels, with the vast majority sourced from Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia, and Cambodia.

### [Q5-017] Trade wars motivate China to double down on green transition
**Source**: SRC-113 [new]
**Relevance**: Counter-effect — tariffs may accelerate rather than hinder China's green capacity

> "Since the eruption of the trade wars, we have seen China double down on its green transition," Thurbon said.

**Assessment**: This question is substantially answered. The probe directly targets green technology sectors in all the mentioned countries. India faces 126% solar tariffs, SE Asian countries face 46-49%, and China is explicitly cited for EV overcapacity. The climate implications are clear: countries that built green industrial capacity in line with climate goals are being punished for it. A remaining gap is detailed analysis of how individual countries' climate policies/NDCs are specifically affected.

## Question 4: How are climate-focused organizations, environmental groups, and energy policy experts assessing the tension between tariff policy and climate goals? Are there specific analyses of the carbon cost of trade fragmentation?

### [Q5-018] American Clean Power: "Policy whiplash will undermine domestic supply chain and energy security"
**Source**: SRC-115 [new]
**Relevance**: Major US clean energy trade association's assessment

> Vanessa Sciarra, vice president of trade and international competitiveness for American Clean Power, stated: "The policy whiplash from these tariffs will ultimately undermine the ability to realize a domestic supply chain and will constrain efforts to deliver energy security and reliability for Americans."

### [Q5-019] CSIS energy director: tariff complacency is biggest climate risk
**Source**: SRC-112 [new]
**Relevance**: Leading energy policy expert's analysis of trade-climate tension

> The biggest risk is that such tariffs become a source of complacency rather than temporary respite from unfair competition. The energy transition in the United States will be market led, so U.S. manufacturers need to develop products that are affordable and attractive. It is not a suitable strategy for global decarbonization for the United States to make itself an island; instead, investment in innovation here should aim to create compelling technologies for deployment abroad. Too much protectionism puts that at risk.

### [Q5-020] BloombergNEF: Tariffs may accelerate transition in poorer countries as China redirects
**Source**: SRC-115 [new]
**Relevance**: Analysis of trade fragmentation creating uneven climate outcomes

> A growing share of Chinese exports of batteries, solar panels, wind turbines and EVs are going to countries the World Bank classifies as lower or middle income. "So you start to see these sort of knock-on effects, and then you could conceivably see a world where the energy transition starts to accelerate slightly in some of those poorer countries where it was seemingly more of a rich country thing."

### [Q5-021] Solar Energy Manufacturers for America: tariffs alone not dependable enough to invest against
**Source**: SRC-115 [new]
**Relevance**: US solar manufacturing coalition acknowledges limitations of tariff-only approach

> "Tariffs can be a part of the solution set, but they're not necessarily dependable enough that you can invest against them," said Michael Carr, executive director of the Solar Energy Manufacturers for America Coalition.

### [Q5-022] China's climate envoy warns COP30 at risk, 3-5 year window critical
**Source**: SRC-113 [new]
**Relevance**: International climate diplomacy perspective on trade disruption

> China's Special Envoy for Climate Change, Liu Zhenmin, warned that the upcoming COP30 in Belem, Brazil, might be one of the most difficult of the past 30 years. If the world fails to use the next three to five years to accelerate the green transition, he warned that the global cost will rise significantly.

**Assessment**: This question is partially answered. We have perspectives from American Clean Power, CSIS energy experts, BloombergNEF, and the Solar Energy Manufacturers coalition. However, there is a gap in coverage of specific environmental NGO assessments (Sierra Club, NRDC, Greenpeace, etc.) and quantitative analyses of the carbon cost of trade fragmentation. No source provides a specific calculation of the emissions impact of tariff-induced deployment delays.

## Question 5: Does the tariff regime's targeting of green technology sectors reveal a contradiction between the stated U.S. goal of energy independence and the practical effect of making clean energy more expensive for American consumers?

### [Q5-023] Tariffs make both deployment AND reshoring more expensive — unlike prior tariff rounds, no manufacturing support
**Source**: SRC-109 [new]
**Relevance**: Core of the contradiction — tariffs without industrial policy support

> The newly proposed tariffs will make it more expensive to deploy clean energy technologies and reshore manufacturing to make them here, because the tariffs apply to other key inputs like cement, construction equipment, and electronic components. A final and critical difference for clean energy technologies is that the reciprocal tariffs are not being paired with support for domestic manufacturing. In fact, the opposite is true — funding from the IRA and BIL has been frozen, and there is a threat that the IRA tax incentives will be repealed imminently.

### [Q5-024] Each successive tariff on solar has failed to revive domestic manufacturing — imports grew despite tariffs since 2012
**Source**: SRC-109 [new]
**Relevance**: Historical evidence that tariffs have not achieved energy independence in solar

> Tariffs on clean energy goods are not new. In 2012, the Obama administration raised tariffs on Chinese solar panels and cells. Each successive administration increased those tariffs or expanded their scope. Despite those efforts, solar installers imported more, and domestic manufacturing lost market share.

### [Q5-025] Tariffs harm not just renewables but all energy infrastructure — transformers, inverters, grid components
**Source**: SRC-115 [new]
**Relevance**: Tariffs undermine energy reliability, contradicting "energy dominance" narrative

> A shortage of electrical components such as transformers, circuit breakers and switchgear has persisted for 54 consecutive months. This isn't a thing which is just good for renewables, bad for fossil fuels, or good for fossil fuels, bad for renewables. It's a period of massive uncertainty.

### [Q5-026] If EVs become unaffordable, the domestic auto industry is adversely affected
**Source**: SRC-112 [new]
**Relevance**: Tariffs on EV batteries undermine the domestic EV market they claim to protect

> Section 301 demonstrates the delicate balance between supporting U.S. national and energy security interests and the need to manage cost inflation. If electric vehicles ultimately become unaffordable, then it will be the domestic auto industry that's adversely affected.

### [Q5-027] US solar module production grew to 50 GW under IRA support but still uses imported components
**Source**: SRC-115 [new]
**Relevance**: Even with tariffs and subsidies, genuine energy independence remains elusive

> The U.S. has boosted its production of solar modules since the IRA went into effect, growing from 14.5 gigawatts of production in 2023 to 50 GW in early 2025. But those modules are still mostly made with imported components. Whether it will be cheaper to import entire solar modules rather than individual components will depend on the size of the tariffs and how they're implemented.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. The evidence reveals a clear contradiction: tariffs raise the cost of clean energy for American consumers and businesses while failing to deliver genuine energy independence. The record since 2012 shows tariffs have not revived domestic solar manufacturing. The current round is uniquely counterproductive because it is paired with cuts to manufacturing support (IRA/BIL frozen). The tariffs even impede fossil fuel energy infrastructure (transformers, switchgear), undermining the "energy dominance" agenda itself.
