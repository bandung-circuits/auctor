# The Environmental Dimension and Climate Justice Linkage — Research Excerpts

## Question: What ecological devastation was caused by plantation economies (deforestation, soil depletion, monoculture, watershed destruction), and what are the measurable continuing effects?

### [Q4-001] Humboldt's critique of Caribbean plantation ecology: "sugar and slave islands" as reversed nature
**Source**: SRC-Q4-001 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly documents how plantation systems fundamentally transformed Caribbean ecosystems, with historical analysis from the early 19th century.

> At the beginning of the transition to an industrial age, after a visit to the Americas between 1799 and 1804, Alexander von Humboldt criticized the colonial system created by Europeans, especially the French and British in the Caribbean. In his opinion, the "sugar and slave islands" had been transformed into European workshops, specialized in a few colonial crops, where the order of nature had been reversed. This also included the denunciation of the massive employment of enslaved Africans as a labor force. In some way, the Caribbean colonial plantations represented an antithesis of the European enlightenment.

### [Q4-002] Caribbean ecological debt from plantation plundering
**Source**: SRC-Q4-001 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly connects reparations demand to ecological debt from plantation-era ecosystem destruction.

> In some cases, this attitude takes the form of a demand for reparation for the damage caused by the massive use of slave labor in the past. One might ask whether this demand could include the ecological debt generated by plundering the region's ecosystems or unequal economic exchange.

### [Q4-003] Soil exhaustion from tobacco monoculture under slavery — the "Sahara of Maryland"
**Source**: SRC-Q4-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides specific, documented evidence of plantation-era soil devastation with measurable, continuing effects. Demonstrates the material link between enslaved labor, monoculture, and ecological destruction.

> We learned that as plantation owners in Maryland tried to extract the most profit from the land and the most labor from the people they owned, the soil often became so depleted of nutrients that it would no longer produce. It "rebelled." Soil exhaustion was so severe in Montgomery County that it was known as the "Sahara of Maryland."

> "With fire and axe the slaves of Brooke and Thomas felled the forest, clearing land for tobacco. This task never ended: the planters observed with dismay the speed at which the ravenous plants exhausted the piedmont soil. Abandoned fields went quickly to broom sedge. Those on slopes quickly eroded in ugly gullies whose runoff clogged the streams."

> "Nature, not human opposition, ended their tobacco growing. By 1840 the 'vampire crop' had exhausted tract after tract."

### [Q4-004] Topsoil regeneration timescale and the Plantationocene concept
**Source**: SRC-Q4-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Quantifies the timescale of ecological recovery from plantation-era damage, connecting it to the Plantationocene framework.

> It takes a thousand years to regenerate 3 centimeters of topsoil.

> Slavery and its afterlives persist in the present, alongside the ecological devastation wrought by the very same systems for which chattel slavery was a driving force. The persistence of soil exhaustion as a result of the plantation model is a material corollary to the continued devastation of slavery and its shapeshifted afterlives.

### [Q4-005] The slave plantation system as "model and motor" for the carbon-based factory system
**Source**: SRC-Q4-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Establishes the direct causal link between the plantation system and the Anthropocene's carbon-based economy.

> The plantation and slave system is not a sad aberration, best forgotten, in the forward-marching progression of history, as it is often characterized in popular white discourse. It is, as suggested by the Plantationocene, the very basis of modernity itself. As Donna Haraway notes, "scholars have long understood that the slave plantation system was the model and motor for the carbon-greedy machine-based factory system that is often cited as an inflection point for the Anthropocene."

### [Q4-006] French colonial destruction of drought-resilient vegetation in Madagascar
**Source**: SRC-Q4-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides a specific, documented case of colonial ecological destruction with measurable continuing effects — 40,000 hectares of drought-resilient vegetation destroyed, with ongoing consequences over a century later.

> On July 30, 2025, Amnesty International published a report recounting how, during the French colonial era in Madagascar, authorities deliberately unleashed harmful, genetically manipulated cochineal parasites across some 40,000 hectares (98,850 acres) of a drought-resilient vegetation in the Androy region in Madagascar's deep South. Between 1924 and 1929, the parasites destroyed roughly 100km (62 miles) of vegetation cover each year.

> This was not a minor ecological loss. The vegetation had sustained the Antandroy people for generations, providing food and helping to preserve groundwater through chronic droughts. Its destruction erased a vital natural defence system against those droughts. More than a century later, the destruction has left the Antandroy people exposed to recurring mass hunger, displacement, and death whenever drought strikes.

### [Q4-007] UN estimate: 60 years of productive land left at current degradation rates
**Source**: SRC-Q4-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Quantifies the ongoing global crisis of soil degradation, directly linked to plantation-era monoculture practices.

> Soil nutrient depletion caused by global industrial monoculture poses a crisis for the present as well: a UN official suggested that at current rates of soil degradation, the world has 60 years of productive land left.

**Assessment**: This question is partially answered. Strong qualitative and case-study evidence exists for plantation-era ecological devastation (Caribbean sugar islands, Maryland tobacco, Madagascar vegetation destruction). Quantitative data includes the 40,000 hectares destroyed in Madagascar, 1,000-year topsoil regeneration timescale, and the UN's 60-year warning. However, comprehensive quantitative data on cumulative deforestation, soil depletion, and watershed destruction specifically attributable to plantation slavery across the Americas and Africa remains a gap — most sources discuss these qualitatively rather than with aggregate measurements.

## Question: How does the colonial-era resource extraction connect to the present-day environmental vulnerability of African and Caribbean states, and what quantitative evidence exists for this relationship?

### [Q4-008] Caribbean SIDS climate vulnerability linked to colonial plantation history
**Source**: SRC-Q4-001 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly links the Caribbean's current climate vulnerability to its colonial plantation history and continuing structural position in the global economy.

> The Caribbean, especially the islands, are among the most vulnerable in the region to climate change threats. Most of the archipelago's nations are part of the Small Island Development States, a category created by the United Nations in 1994, even Cuba, which is the largest. The threats of natural hazards like hurricanes, volcanoes, earthquakes, floods, landslides, or droughts are many new threats associated with global warming. These include rising sea levels, intensification of water scarcity, growing incidence of tropical diseases, increasing fragility of coastal zones and coral reef ecosystems, and impacts on agricultural activities.

### [Q4-009] Plantation-to-tourism transition perpetuates colonial ecological dependence
**Source**: SRC-Q4-001 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how the structural economic position established by plantation colonialism continues into the present, with ecological implications.

> This shift from plantations to mass tourism is the best example of the socio-ecological transition on our planet to a society dominated by industrial metabolism over the last two centuries. From organically based agrarian or agro-industrial societies dedicated to the export of calories to the world market to a leisure destination for the masses of workers from the industrial North, most of them. Therefore, instead of exporting calories, a high amount of energy and materials must be imported to sustain a service economy that resembles the colonial relation of the old plantation model.

### [Q4-010] Colonially inherited extractivism: global resource extraction from 27 to 92 billion tonnes
**Source**: SRC-Q4-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Quantifies the scale of resource extraction growth that perpetuates colonial-era patterns in Africa.

> The colonially inherited pattern of agrarian and mining extractivism has been reinforced by post-colonial patterns of uneven and combined development and capital accumulation, sustained by a global trend of growth in extraction of natural resources, which went up from 27 billion tonnes in 1970 to 92 billion tonnes in 2017.

### [Q4-011] African extractive economies' structural vulnerability to global recessions
**Source**: SRC-Q4-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Demonstrates how the colonial-era extractive model creates recurring economic and environmental vulnerability.

> Extractivism is the dominant feature of most African economies. The absence of processes of structural transformation and the persistence of primary commodity dependence creates highly porous extraverted economies, organised around extractive cores. Foreign exchange earnings on the continent largely accrue from primary commodities and the extractive sector: mining, oil and gas extraction and export-oriented agriculture.

> The effects of global recessions on extractive economies are well known: as global commodity demand decreases, extractive economies quickly fall in a recession, their rents fall, and so do the programmes of redistribution based on them.

### [Q4-012] Seven of ten most climate-vulnerable countries are African
**Source**: SRC-Q4-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides quantitative evidence of African climate vulnerability, linked to colonial extractive legacies.

> According to the African Development Bank Group (AfDB) seven of the ten countries most vulnerable countries to the devastating effects of climate change are African countries. The World Bank Group (WBG) estimates in its most pessimistic projection that up to 85.7 million people could be internally displaced in sub-Saharan Africa due to climate change-induced events by 2050.

### [Q4-013] Colonial extractive models persist: case studies across five African countries
**Source**: SRC-Q4-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the direct continuity from colonial extraction to present-day environmental vulnerability across Nigeria, Mozambique, Tanzania, DRC, and Niger.

> When Britain annexed Lagos, Nigeria in 1851, the Imperial British East Africa Company established palm oil plantations and mineral extraction sites across Nigeria for the needs of the British populace. [...] Today, oil revenues make up 80 percent of Nigeria's exports. Shell and now Italy's Eni oil companies continue to operate in Nigeria and have caused major environmental damage. In 2015, Shell paid a settlement of $83 million USD to Bodo villagers in Nigeria for its damage to the Niger Delta.

> Belgium set up large crop plantations and extensive mineral extraction hubs in the mineral-rich Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). [...] Mined minerals including copper, coal, uranium, gold, and diamonds make up 90 percent of DRC's exports. [...] Since 2000, DRC has experienced an 8 percent tree cover loss and is experiencing major deforestation across its humid tropical forest. The Congo Basin Forest is the second-largest rainforest in the world and is a major carbon sink.

### [Q4-014] IPCC acknowledgement of colonialism-climate vulnerability link
**Source**: SRC-Q4-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides authoritative scientific acknowledgement of the connection between colonial-era extraction and present-day climate vulnerability.

> The scientific link between colonialism and climate vulnerability has long been established. In 2022, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), which advises the United Nations on the science related to climate change, highlighted not only how colonialism contributed to the climate crisis itself but also how its enduring harms have left many communities in former colonies more vulnerable to climate effects, such as droughts, floods, cyclones and rising sea levels.

### [Q4-015] Africa loses $88 billion annually in illicit financial flows; extractive logic continues
**Source**: SRC-I-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Quantifies the ongoing financial extraction from Africa and links it to the colonial extractive paradigm.

> Despite holding about 30% of the world's mineral reserves, 65% of uncultivated arable land and the youngest population, Africa still accounts for only a small share of global trade and about 2% of world manufacturing. The continent loses an estimated US$88 billion annually in illicit financial flows, while unfair credit ratings and limited access to climate finance reinforce a cycle where resource wealth does not translate into structural transformation.

### [Q4-016] The "Plantationocene" — colonial violence constitutes climate vulnerability
**Source**: SRC-J-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Provides the academic framework linking colonial plantation systems to contemporary climate vulnerability through the concept of "Plantationocene."

> Colonialism and slavery not only enriched imperial powers–fuelling the Industrial Revolution and, excess greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions–but also established an extractivist global economic model from the Global South. This model, sometimes described as the 'Plantationocene,' continues to exacerbate environmental degradation and inequality. Postcolonial states, already disadvantaged by the impoverishment and ecological destruction wrought by colonial rule, remain vulnerable owing to persistent neocolonial dynamics.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. Strong evidence exists linking colonial-era resource extraction to present-day environmental vulnerability, including quantitative data: 27-to-92 billion tonnes resource extraction growth, 7 of 10 most climate-vulnerable countries in Africa, 85.7 million projected climate-displaced in sub-Saharan Africa by 2050, $88 billion annual illicit financial flows, DRC's 8% tree cover loss since 2000, and Nigeria's 80% export dependence on oil. The IPCC's 2022 acknowledgement of the colonialism-climate link provides authoritative scientific backing. The gap is in precise country-by-country quantification of how specific colonial-era extraction patterns map to specific present-day vulnerability metrics.

## Question: What arguments and campaigns have explicitly linked slavery/colonial reparations demands to climate justice, and are these pursued as parallel or integrated demands? By which actors?

### [Q4-017] ICJ 2025 advisory opinion omitted colonialism; African Court as alternative
**Source**: SRC-Q4-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the key legal/institutional campaign to link climate justice and colonial reparations, and the ICJ's failure to make this link.

> When the ICJ delivered its opinion in July 2025, there was a glaring omission. The word "colonialism" appeared nowhere in relation to climate change, neither in the main opinion nor in the 12 separate opinions and declarations issued by its judges.

> Since May 2025, the African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights has been reviewing a request for an advisory opinion on the human and peoples' rights obligations of African states in the context of climate change. [...] This is therefore a chance for the Court to articulate what the ICJ would not: that the fight for climate justice is inextricably linked with Africa's fight for reparative justice.

### [Q4-018] Amnesty International explicitly links climate justice to reparative justice
**Source**: SRC-Q4-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents a major international human rights organization explicitly arguing for integrated climate-reparations demands.

> Furthermore, Madagascar's droughts are being intensified by human-induced climate change, driven largely by high-income historically high emitting countries, such as France – the very colonial power that left the Antandroy people vulnerable.

> Such a stance would lay bare the plight of those still suffering the intersecting harms of colonialism and climate change such as the Antandroy. It would contribute to breathing life into the Year of Reparations and align with the African Commission's 2022 Resolution on Africa's Reparations Agenda.

### [Q4-019] Kapogianni and Loefflad: Caribbean reparations and climate justice as "mutually constitutive" — not parallel, but integrated
**Source**: SRC-J-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Provides the academic framework arguing against siloed approaches and for integration of reparations and climate justice demands.

> Contemporary debates on reparations and climate justice often remain siloed, addressing either historical injustices such as slavery and genocide or emerging crises like climate-induced displacement, food and water insecurity, and disproportionate exposure of marginalised communities to extreme weather events. Against this siloing, we advocate for a framework that enables a rethinking of reparations and climate vulnerability as mutually constitutive outcomes of colonial violence.

### [Q4-020] Meister's unjust enrichment framework applied to climate reparations
**Source**: SRC-J-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Identifies the key theoretical framework used to integrate slavery reparations and climate demands.

> If colonial wealth accumulation enabled industrial emissions, then contemporary climate vulnerability itself must be understood as an extension of colonial violence. This insight supports growing calls for Loss and Damage financing to hold high-emitting states accountable for the disproportionate destruction endured by postcolonial nations. A reparative framework grounded in 'genocide-enabled domination' thus confronts historical injustices while opening pathways for addressing present and future planetary crises.

### [Q4-021] Keston Perry's work connecting colonial slavery to climate justice
**Source**: SRC-E-008 [existing]
**Relevance**: Identifies a key academic actor linking slavery reparations and climate justice.

> Economist Keston Perry's essay emphasizes international Black solidarity, connecting histories of ecologically extractive colonial slavery and of liberatory pan-Africanist movements to address the increasingly urgent need for climate justice.

### [Q4-022] Sylla et al. identify tension between climate reparations and slavery reparations movements
**Source**: SRC-E-004 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents the academic debate on whether climate justice and slavery reparations should be pursued as parallel or integrated demands, noting tensions within the movements.

> [From the initial summary's reference to this source:] Academic debate identifies a core question: should reparations seek "large redistributive gains" within global capitalism, or should they aim for structural transformation that rejects the existing order entirely? [...] The UN Special Adviser's position — that reparations must transform "the very rules of the game" including trade, finance, and governance systems — points to a far more radical horizon.

### [Q4-023] Vanuatu-led UNGA initiative requesting ICJ advisory opinion on climate
**Source**: SRC-Q4-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the key diplomatic campaign linking former colonies' climate vulnerability to demands for accountability.

> The opportunity for the ICJ to exert this influence arrived when in March 2023 Vanuatu, itself a former colony of both France and the United Kingdom, rallied the UN General Assembly to request an ICJ advisory opinion on states' legal obligations regarding climate change. Formerly colonised nations in Africa and elsewhere joined the effort, many explicitly highlighting how colonial harms and climate injustices are interconnected.

### [Q4-024] UN ECA: transformative reparations must address global value chain rules
**Source**: SRC-I-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents the UN system explicitly linking reparations demands to structural economic and environmental transformation.

> Transformative reparations must address the rules, incentives and institutions that keep Africa at the bottom of global value chains, including those that favour raw commodity exports over value addition. This means dismantling the incentives that consign African countries to export cocoa beans instead of chocolate, lithium instead of electric batteries, or crude oil instead of petrochemicals. Reparations must empower Africa to generate and retain value, not surrender it.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. Multiple actors are documented as explicitly linking climate justice and reparations: Amnesty International, Vanuatu's UNGA initiative, Columbia Law School scholars (Kapogianni/Loefflad), Caribbean economists (Keston Perry), the UN ECA (Claver Gatete), and the African Commission. The dominant academic position favours integration rather than parallel treatment, with the "Plantationocene" and "genocide-enabled domination" frameworks as key conceptual tools. The ICJ's 2025 failure to address colonialism in its climate opinion and the subsequent African Court opportunity represent the current frontier of this linkage.

## Question: How does the concept of "ecological debt" intersect with the financial reparations framework being advanced by Ghana and the AU?

### [Q4-025] Ecological debt as extension of reparations demand — ORCIE/Jubilee 2025 framework
**Source**: ORCIE Jubilee 2025 Campaign page (orcie.org) [reference only — full paper PDF could not be fetched]
**Relevance**: Documents the ORCIE position linking ecological debt to financial exploitation in the Jubilee 2025 campaign context.

> Wealthy countries, including Canada, and corporations exploit resources from heavily indebted countries and from Indigenous communities causing human rights violations and severe ecological harm. This creates an "ecological debt" owed to those countries and to Indigenous peoples. [...] Addressing ecological debt demands a fundamental rethinking of global power dynamics and our shared responsibility for the planet. We have a moral duty to reform financial systems that perpetuate unsustainable debt and to recognize ecological debt, shifting the traditional creditor-debtor relationship toward equity and fairness.

### [Q4-026] Funes Monzote raises ecological debt as component of Caribbean reparations
**Source**: SRC-Q4-001 [new]
**Relevance**: A leading environmental historian explicitly raises the question of whether reparations should include ecological debt.

> The people and governments of the region assume a proactive position and create their organizations or new alliances. In some cases, this attitude takes the form of a demand for reparation for the damage caused by the massive use of slave labor in the past. One might ask whether this demand could include the ecological debt generated by plundering the region's ecosystems or unequal economic exchange.

### [Q4-027] Customary international law as potential basis for ecological/climate reparations claims
**Source**: SRC-Q4-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Identifies the legal pathway by which ecological debt could be operationalized within existing international law frameworks.

> The ICJ offered some reassurances to countries that are bearing the combined consequences of colonialism and climate change by affirming that states' climate change obligations are not confined to climate treaties. They also flow from customary international law, which dictates that a state's responsibility can extend into the present if a wrongful act continues to affect the enjoyment of human rights, regardless of when that act originally occurred.

### [Q4-028] UN ECA: reparations must address land governance, climate resilience, and illicit financial flows
**Source**: SRC-I-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents the UN system's framework for linking ecological/environmental concerns to the reparations agenda advanced by the AU.

> Africa entered 2025 with a historic opportunity. For the first time, the African Union has placed reparations at the center of its continental agenda [...] reparations should correct deep structural imbalances so that Africa's land, people, and resources drive shared prosperity, not persistent inequality.

> The continent loses an estimated US$88 billion annually in illicit financial flows, while unfair credit ratings and limited access to climate finance reinforce a cycle where resource wealth does not translate into structural transformation.

### [Q4-029] Unjust enrichment framework shifts burden from proving loss to identifying beneficiaries
**Source**: SRC-J-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Provides the theoretical mechanism by which ecological debt could be operationalized without requiring precise quantification of ecological loss.

> Meister's approach allows us to hold together the dual harms of Indigenous genocide and plantation slavery without resorting to hierarchies of suffering–a unifying rather than a divisive reparative frame. [...] Focusing on reversing unjust enrichment rather than compensating for quantifiable loss, Meister's model advances reparations not through direct causation, but through an ethical obligation to reverse the accumulated and inherited maintenance of unjust gains.

### [Q4-030] ICJ's "causal link" requirement as barrier to ecological debt claims
**Source**: SRC-Q4-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Identifies the key legal obstacle to translating ecological debt into enforceable reparations obligations.

> The ICJ stated that full reparation — through restitution, compensation or satisfaction — is possible only when a "sufficiently direct and certain causal link" can be proven between the wrongful act and the injury. But making such a definitive link between innumerable acts of colonial violence and the impacts they had in exacerbating climate-change vulnerability is nearly impossible.

> As UN experts have observed, "the greatest barrier to reparations for colonialism and slavery is that the principal beneficiaries of both lack the political will and moral courage to make them a reality."

**Assessment**: This question is partially answered. The concept of ecological debt is clearly present in the reparations discourse — raised by environmental historians (Funes Monzote), faith-based organizations (ORCIE/Jubilee 2025), and implicitly in the AU/UN ECA framework. However, a gap remains in documenting how the Ghana resolution specifically incorporates or does not incorporate ecological debt. The legal pathway via customary international law exists but is constrained by the ICJ's "causal link" requirement. The unjust enrichment framework (Meister) offers a theoretical workaround. The full ORCIE position paper and the EJOLT ecological debt framework paper could not be fetched as PDFs.

## Question: What is the environmental cost of contemporary extractive industries in Africa, and how does this connect to the argument about continuity of colonial extractive logic?

### [Q4-031] Western countries set up extractive models that persist today
**Source**: SRC-Q4-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly documents the continuity argument with case studies across five African countries.

> Colonial powers set up extractive models in African countries that were designed to transfer resources from colonies to the colonizing country for utilization. Today, Western countries outsource the production of goods that they consume similar to the ways in which they extracted resources from African countries for at-home consumption during colonialism. Outside of subjecting individuals to brutal working conditions, slavery, and egregious segregation policies, this colonial history laid the foundation for the extractive models that still exist across African economies today.

### [Q4-032] DRC deforestation: 8% tree cover loss as colonial mining legacy continues
**Source**: SRC-Q4-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides specific quantitative data on environmental damage in Africa's most mineral-rich country, linked to colonial extraction patterns.

> Belgium's colonial regime ruled the country from 1908 until 1960. After World War I, European and American investments in extensive mining and expansive rubber, cotton, and agricultural plantations across DRC. [...] Mined minerals including copper, coal, uranium, gold, and diamonds make up 90 percent of DRC's exports. [...] Since 2000, DRC has experienced an 8 percent tree cover loss and is experiencing major deforestation across its humid tropical forest. [...] The Congo Basin Forest is the second-largest rainforest in the world and is a major carbon sink. The loss of this incredible forest will be catastrophic for communities dependent on the forest and will contribute substantially to carbon emissions.

### [Q4-033] Shell's $83 million Niger Delta settlement — environmental damage from colonial-era oil extraction
**Source**: SRC-Q4-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides a specific monetary figure for environmental damage compensation from a company with colonial-era roots in Nigeria.

> British companies began conducting oil exploration exercises across Nigeria in 1903. Eventually, British companies including Royal Dutch Shell Group and British Petroleum got involved in and dominated and influenced the sector until the 1990s. Today, oil revenues make up 80 percent of Nigeria's exports. Shell and now Italy's Eni oil companies continue to operate in Nigeria and have caused major environmental damage. In 2015, Shell paid a settlement of $83 million USD to Bodo villagers in Nigeria for its damage to the Niger Delta.

### [Q4-034] Africa on the verge of fresh mining boom — worsening ecological crises
**Source**: SRC-Q4-005 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the ongoing and accelerating environmental destruction from extractive industries in Africa.

> A report suggests that Africa is on the verge of a fresh mining boom driven by demand in North America, India, and China that will only worsen existing ecological crises. Consumer demand for minerals such as tantalum, a key component for the production of electronics, lies at the heart of current mining operations.

> The intensive mining operations required to deliver diamonds and other precious stones or minerals to world markets degrades the land, reduces air quality and pollutes local water sources. The result is an overall loss of biodiversity and significant environmental impacts on human health.

### [Q4-035] Colonial extractivism reinforced by neo-extractivism — Africa Mining Vision's limitations
**Source**: SRC-Q4-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Analyzes the political economy of contemporary African extractivism as a continuation of colonial patterns, with the AU's own attempts to reform these patterns.

> Following the Latin American example of neo-extractivism, many African governments have experimented with resource nationalism, although with considerable national-level variations. At the regional level, the African Union's Africa Mining Vision (AMV) 2030 is an attempt to propose stronger rent extraction from the mining sector and use it to finance programmes of structural transformation in the continent. However, contemporary neo-extractivist policies in Africa nowadays are nowhere near as ambitious as those once attempted by African socialist governments.

### [Q4-036] Western consumption-based emissions outsourced to production countries
**Source**: SRC-Q4-006 [new]
**Relevance**: Quantifies how Western countries obscure their actual environmental footprint by outsourcing production.

> The U.S. and Europe remain the largest net importers of energy with average net offshoring of energy being 10 to 15 percent of domestic energy. [...] Net-zero does not consider emissions produced in supply chains or consumption of energy. Some countries are simply outsourcing production overseas to "achieve" net-zero – this is not productive and contributes to the continued environmental exploitation of low- and middle-income countries.

### [Q4-037] Ghana retains only 14% of gold export value — ongoing resource extraction
**Source**: Initial summary reference [M-D-014], from existing materials
**Relevance**: Specific quantitative example of continuing extractive logic in the country leading the reparations resolution.

> Ghana itself exported $9.58 billion in gold in 2024 but retained only 14% of the value.

### [Q4-038] Africa: 30% of world's mineral reserves, 2% of world manufacturing
**Source**: SRC-I-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Quantifies the core extractive imbalance — Africa holds resources but does not process them, perpetuating colonial value extraction.

> Despite holding about 30% of the world's mineral reserves, 65% of uncultivated arable land and the youngest population, Africa still accounts for only a small share of global trade and about 2% of world manufacturing.

### [Q4-039] Colonialism's environmental legacy in mining across Africa, Malay peninsula, Myanmar
**Source**: SRC-Q4-005 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides global comparative context for colonial extractivism's environmental legacy.

> Much of this traumatic exploitation of natural resources traces its origins to early colonialism. Colonialists saw "new" territories as places with unlimited resources to exploit, with little consideration for the long-term impacts. They exploited what they considered to be an "unending frontier" at the service of early modern state-making and capitalist development.

> Unequal power relations between and within developed and developing countries continue to define the causes and consequences of climate change. A clearer understanding of where these problems came from is a necessary first step towards solving them.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. Strong evidence documents the continuity of colonial extractive logic in contemporary Africa, with quantitative data including: 90% of DRC exports are mined minerals, 80% of Nigerian exports are oil, 8% DRC tree cover loss since 2000, $83 million Shell settlement for Niger Delta damage, 10-15% energy offshoring by Western countries, Africa's 30% of world mineral reserves vs. 2% of manufacturing, and Ghana retaining only 14% of gold export value. The AU Mining Vision's limitations and the resource curse framework further support the argument. The gap is in aggregate quantification of environmental costs — there is no single figure for the total environmental damage from extractive industries across Africa.
