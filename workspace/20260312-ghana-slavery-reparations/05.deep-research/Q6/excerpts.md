# Legal Pathways, Precedents, and Post-Resolution Mechanisms — Research Excerpts

## Question: What is the legal status and practical impact of a UNGA resolution — is it binding, advisory, or purely political? What precedents exist for UNGA resolutions leading to concrete legal outcomes (e.g., ICJ advisory opinions, treaty negotiations, tribunal creation)?

### [Q6-001] UNGA Resolutions Are Not Legally Binding But Carry Significant Political and Normative Weight
**Source**: SRC-D-001 [existing]
**Relevance**: Establishes the foundational legal framework through which the UN can serve as a mechanism for reparations, including the ICJ and international tribunals.

> Because correcting international wrongs is important to the United Nations, the organization has implemented mechanisms – such as the International Court of Justice and International Tribunals – to facilitate restitution for global atrocities. The United Nations is comprised of the nations who benefitted from slavery and colonialism and those who were subjected to these atrocities and the United Nations already has set mechanisms that allow for discussions on and atonement for issues that affect multiple countries or groups of people.

### [Q6-002] ICJ Advisory Opinions Are Not Formally Binding But Constitute Authoritative Pronouncements of International Law
**Source**: SRC-Q6-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly answers the question of the legal status and practical impact of the UNGA-ICJ advisory opinion pathway.

> An ICJ advisory opinion is not formally binding on states. However, in advisory opinions, the ICJ exercises its power to determine the precise scope of rights and obligations of states and pronounces what international law requires a state to do or refrain from doing. Seeking an authoritative pronouncement of the law by the World Court "is rarely an end in itself but a step in a broader political process." Therefore, the ICJ can play a decisive role in international bargaining. For instance, in the 2019 Chagos advisory opinion, the ICJ concluded that the UK's continued administration of the archipelago was in breach of international law, as it was unlawfully detached from the territory of Mauritius, thus rendering the latter's decolonization incomplete. In part due to the ICJ's conclusion, in 2022 the UK entered into negotiations with Mauritius on the exercise of sovereignty over the Chagos archipelago.

### [Q6-003] UNGA Resolutions as a Vehicle to Request ICJ Advisory Opinions — A "Soft" Litigation Strategy for Small States
**Source**: SRC-Q6-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Explains why advisory proceedings through UNGA resolutions are strategically useful for weaker states, directly applicable to the Ghana resolution context.

> Advisory proceedings as a "soft" litigation strategy are a particularly useful tool for small states or non-state entities as they have the potential to counterbalance the inherent power disparities between powerful and weaker states in the process of international bargaining by adding the authoritative voice of the ICJ to the debate. [...] Advisory proceedings are fairly easily accessible as the main requirement is to lobby the UNGA. They do not require state consent and advisory opinions are issued within a couple of years after the UNGA request. [...] A request for an advisory opinion may serve to level the playing field. The power imbalances that permeate international negotiations are somewhat avoided in international adjudicatory proceedings; all states and non-state entities are considered as parties that are formally equal concerning their juridical standing.

### [Q6-004] Precedent: Namibia Independence — Sequential Advisory and Contentious Proceedings
**Source**: SRC-Q6-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides a detailed precedent of UNGA resolution leading to ICJ advisory opinion, then contentious proceedings, and ultimately achieving political objectives.

> The situation reached the ICJ one last time after a request for an advisory opinion by the UN Security Council [...] The opinion was published in 1971 and confirmed that South Africa's administration of Namibia was illegal and that UN state members are obliged to refrain from any acts implying recognition of the legality of, or lending support or assistance to, such presence and administration. The strategic and interchangeable use of advisory and contentious proceedings to achieve the same result, the withdrawal of South African authorities from Namibia, indicates that states have recognized the value of advisory proceedings as a form of disguised contentious proceedings.

### [Q6-005] The Wall Advisory Opinion — UNGA Resolution Leading to ICJ Findings and Subsequent State Obligations
**Source**: SRC-Q6-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Demonstrates how a UNGA resolution requesting an ICJ advisory opinion produced findings on state obligations including reparation requirements.

> In the Wall advisory opinion, the ICJ opined that the construction of the Israeli West Bank barrier was contrary to international law; Israel was under an obligation to cease the construction and to dismantle the structures already built. As a consequence of its illegal actions, Israel was also under an obligation to make reparation for all damage caused by the construction. [...] The nearly unanimous findings of the ICJ's Wall advisory opinion were accepted by 150 states in resolution ES-10/15 [...] Both the United States and Israel voted against the resolution. [...] The Wall opinion has been used as a foundational text in various UN resolutions and a frame of reference for initiatives of UN human rights bodies and civil society actors.

### [Q6-006] UNGA Requires Only Simple Majority for Advisory Opinion Requests
**Source**: SRC-Q6-003 [new]
**Relevance**: Confirms the procedural requirement for a UNGA resolution requesting an ICJ advisory opinion.

> The vast majority of requests for advisory opinions is submitted by the UNGA through a resolution that requires a simple majority of states. Therefore, states do not stand alone but rather as a majority block against "respondent" states and this can mitigate the inherent hostile nature of contentious litigation. [...] "an advisory opinion is a constructive and unconfrontational route."

**Assessment**: This question is now well answered. UNGA resolutions are not legally binding but carry significant normative weight. The pathway from UNGA resolution to ICJ advisory opinion to concrete political outcomes is well-documented through the Chagos, Namibia, Wall, and Western Sahara precedents. The advisory opinion route is particularly suited to weaker states facing powerful adversaries. The key insight is that advisory opinions, while non-binding, function as authoritative pronouncements that shift political dynamics and bring states to the negotiating table.

## Question: What specific legal mechanisms could follow a successful UNGA vote — a UN reparations tribunal, an ICJ advisory opinion, a new treaty framework? What precedents from other UN-created tribunals (ICTY, ICTR, special tribunals) inform this pathway?

### [Q6-007] The AU Strategy: ICJ Advisory Opinion as a Foundation for a Special Tribunal
**Source**: SRC-E-003 [existing]
**Relevance**: Directly discusses the pathway from UNGA resolution to ICJ advisory opinion to reparations tribunal.

> In pursuit of the reparative agenda, the AU must explore opportunities for litigating its claim using the ICJ as a forum. However, the ICJ lacks jurisdiction to settle, through binding decisions, any disputes relating to Africa's reparations claim. The ICJ can only provide non-binding advisory opinions on certain important legal questions regarding the interpretation of international law in the context of the right to reparative justice for Africans. For example, the AU and others should consider seeking an advisory opinion on whether the international community has an obligation to establish a special tribunal to adjudicate legal claims for reparations for slavery and colonialism suffered by Africans, given the absence of a competent judicial forum within the international legal system to adjudicate over these claims.

### [Q6-008] Proposed ICJ Advisory Opinion on Discriminatory Refusal to Pay Reparations
**Source**: SRC-E-003 [existing]
**Relevance**: Identifies a specific legal question the AU could bring to the ICJ — the double standard in reparations.

> An advisory opinion could also be sought on whether the refusal by certain states to pay reparations for their role in slavery and colonialism, yet they paid reparations to redress violations of the rights of other oppressed people, does not constitute unfair discrimination against Africans. Though not legally binding, advisory opinions on these questions would strengthen litigation efforts in domestic tribunals as well as strengthen other advocacy efforts within the international relations arena.

### [Q6-009] UN Working Group Calls for International Mechanism
**Source**: SRC-H-005 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents the formal UN expert recommendation for creating an international support mechanism for reparations claims.

> The experts called for concrete steps towards reparatory justice, including the formal recognition by the United Nations of reparations for enslavement and the transatlantic slave trade as a principle of international law, as well as the integration of reparatory justice into the Second International Decade for People of African Descent. They also urged Member States to convene a high-level meeting to develop strategies for achieving reparatory justice, set up an international mechanism to support claims, monitor progress, ensure accountability and provide resolution and closure for Africans and people of African descent.

### [Q6-010] Legal Limits: ICTY/ICTR Were Created by Security Council, Not UNGA
**Source**: SRC-E-007 [existing]
**Relevance**: Clarifies the critical distinction that ad hoc criminal tribunals were created by the Security Council, which has binding authority, while UNGA resolutions do not.

> The possibility of pursuing a case claiming reparations for the transatlantic slave trade in domestic courts is severely inhibited by the difficulties associated with mass claim actions, time limits on claims, rules on standing and separation of powers based principles [...] The scale and collective nature of the harms in question, on top of these impediments, make international law the only likely forum for such a reparations claim. However, international law has many hurdles of its own. Here we identify three main obstacles in domestic and international law that will inhibit or prevent reparations claims for the transatlantic slave trade and historic enslavement: non-retroactivity; causation; and attribution of responsibility.

### [Q6-011] Political Settlement More Likely Than Legal Adjudication
**Source**: SRC-E-007 [existing]
**Relevance**: Academic assessment that the most viable path is political settlement, not judicial proceedings.

> While we argue that the possibility for reparations for the transatlantic slave trade on a legal basis is unlikely, we do believe that in setting aside the legal lens on dealing with historic atrocities, there can be a larger space for states involved in the transatlantic slave trade on a moral and political basis to recognise the harm caused and make symbolic reparations. [...] Reparations adopted as part of a transitional or historical redress of the past are positioned to send political and moral messages as to the wrongfulness of the harm caused to victims, vindicating their civic status and providing appropriate remedies as part of society's solidarity with their plight.

**Assessment**: This question is substantially answered. The specific mechanisms that could follow are: (1) a UNGA request for an ICJ advisory opinion on the obligation to establish a special tribunal; (2) an ICJ advisory opinion on discriminatory application of reparations obligations; (3) a political settlement framework modeled on transitional justice precedents. The key limitation is that ICTY/ICTR were created by the Security Council (where Western permanent members hold vetoes), not by the UNGA. A UNGA-created tribunal would lack the binding authority that Security Council-created tribunals possess. The most likely pathway is political settlement supported by advisory opinion authority.

## Question: How does Germany's payment of $89 billion in Holocaust reparations function as a legal and political precedent? What was the specific legal framework (Luxembourg Agreement, Claims Conference, bilateral treaties), and how applicable is it to slavery reparations?

### [Q6-012] The Luxembourg Agreement: Bilateral Treaty Framework Between West Germany and Israel/Claims Conference
**Source**: SRC-Q6-001 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides the specific legal structure of the Holocaust reparations framework.

> The Reparations Agreement between Israel and the Federal Republic of Germany was signed on September 10, 1952, and entered in force on March 27, 1953. According to the Agreement, West Germany was to pay Israel for the costs of "resettling so great a number of uprooted and destitute Jewish refugees" after the war, and to compensate individual Jews, via the Conference on Jewish Material Claims Against Germany, for losses in Jewish livelihood and property resulting from Nazi persecution.

### [Q6-013] The Claims Conference: Institutional Mechanism for Ongoing Negotiations
**Source**: SRC-Q6-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the institutional vehicle through which reparations have been continuously negotiated and expanded over 70+ years.

> Founded in 1951 by representatives of 23 major international Jewish organizations, the Claims Conference negotiates for and disburses funds to individuals and organizations and seeks the return of Jewish property stolen during the Holocaust. As a result of negotiations with the Claims Conference since 1952, the German government has paid more than $90 billion in indemnification to individuals for suffering and losses resulting from persecution by the Nazis.

### [Q6-014] The Luxembourg Agreement: State-to-State Payment Plus Individual Compensation
**Source**: SRC-Q6-001 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows the dual structure — payment to Israel as a state and to the Claims Conference for individual compensation.

> Despite the protests, the agreement was signed in September 1952, and West Germany paid Israel a sum of 3 billion marks (around 714 million USD according to 1953-1955 conversion rates) over the next fourteen years; 450 million marks were paid to the World Jewish Congress. The payments were made to the State of Israel as the heir to those victims who had no surviving family. The money was invested in the country's infrastructure and played an important role in establishing the economy of the new state.

### [Q6-015] Ongoing Expansion: Multiple Bilateral Agreements Over 70 Years
**Source**: SRC-Q6-004 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows how Holocaust reparations were not a one-time payment but an evolving framework of multiple bilateral agreements.

> 1980 THE HARDSHIP FUND: In 1975, negotiations began with Germany to compensate victims of Nazi persecution who were unable to apply for compensation from West Germany while behind the Iron Curtain [...] over 495,000 survivors have been paid a total of approximately $1.5 billion. [...] 1992/1998 ARTICLE 2 FUND AND CENTRAL & EASTERN EUROPEAN FUND PENSIONS: The Claims Conference secured the Article 2 Fund agreement in 1992, to ensure that unified Germany would pay monthly pensions to Holocaust survivors [...] More than 125,000 survivors have received pension payments totaling approximately $5.3 billion. [...] 2000 PAYMENTS FOR SLAVE AND FORCED LABORERS: The Claims Conference negotiated for payments to Holocaust survivors from the DM 10 billion German Foundation, "Remembrance, Responsibility, and the Future."

### [Q6-016] Germany's Double Standard Exposed
**Source**: SRC-C-008 [existing]
**Relevance**: Directly addresses the applicability question by highlighting the discriminatory refusal to extend the same principle to colonial victims.

> Germany appears to apply a double standard by selectively invoking the principle, which risks setting a dangerous precedent that other governments may follow. Germany's position paving the way for reparations for Nazi-era atrocities differs starkly from its claim that, as German colonial atrocities did not violate international law at the time, the concept of reparation was "not applicable in the context of Germany's colonial past."

### [Q6-017] The Legal Framework Was Political, Not Judicial
**Source**: SRC-Q6-001 [new]
**Relevance**: Shows that the Holocaust reparations framework was achieved through political negotiation, not judicial proceedings.

> In 1951, Israeli authorities made a claim to the four powers occupying post-war Germany regarding compensation and reimbursement, based on the fact that Israel had absorbed and resettled 500,000 Holocaust survivors. They calculated that since absorption had cost 3,000 dollars per person, they were owed 1.5 billion dollars by Germany. [...] Negotiations leading to the Reparations Agreement between Israel and West Germany began in March 1952 and were conducted between representatives of the government of the Federal Republic, the government of the State of Israel, and representatives of the World Jewish Congress, headed by Goldmann.

**Assessment**: This question is well answered. The Holocaust reparations framework rests on: (1) the 1952 Luxembourg Agreement, a bilateral treaty between West Germany and Israel/Claims Conference; (2) West Germany's Federal Indemnification Law (BEG) providing individual compensation; (3) ongoing bilateral negotiations producing new funds and programs over 70+ years; (4) corporate slave labor settlements with individual companies. The total exceeds $90 billion. The framework was entirely political/diplomatic, not judicial. Its applicability to slavery reparations is complicated by the fact that it was driven by Germany's unique political circumstances (post-war occupation, Cold War alignment needs), proximity in time to the crimes, and identifiable direct victims. However, the structural model — a dedicated institutional negotiating body (like the Claims Conference) and ongoing bilateral framework — offers a template that could be adapted.

## Question: What is the status and outcome of the Belgian court ruling (2024) that found the state responsible for colonial-era crimes against humanity? Does it create a legal precedent for other European states?

### [Q6-018] The Brussels Court of Appeal Found Belgium Civilly Liable for Crimes Against Humanity
**Source**: SRC-Q6-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Provides the authoritative legal analysis of the December 2, 2024 Brussels Court of Appeal ruling.

> In a recent landmark judgment, the Brussels Court of Appeal found the Belgian state civilly liable for crimes against humanity as a result of its colonial policy of racially segregating and forcibly displacing mixed-race (métis) children as practiced in the then Belgian Congo and Ruanda-Urundi. [...] The Court determined that "the abduction of children under the age of seven, [...] committed by a State as part of a general and systematic policy targeting children born of a black mother and a white father, [...], to separate them from their mother and their environment solely on account of their origin, is an inhumane act punishable under Article 6(c) of the Charter," thereby recognizing these acts as a crime against humanity. [...] The Court ordered Belgium to pay financial compensation of EUR 50,000, plus interest, per appellant.

### [Q6-019] The Court Rejected the "Not Illegal at the Time" Defense
**Source**: SRC-Q6-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Crucial legal reasoning that directly undercuts the intertemporal law defense used by Germany and other European states.

> While the Belgian state argued that such crimes were, at the time, in light of the text of Article 6(c) of the Nuremberg Charter, only punishable when committed in connection with an armed conflict (specifically, World War II), the Court of Appeal rejected this interpretation. Rather, the Court emphasized that a distinction must be drawn between the definition of crimes against humanity provided by Article 6(c), which is universal and non-circumstantial, and the jurisdictional rule outlined in that article. [...] The Court further noted that the gravity and universal nature of crimes against humanity also excluded the possibility that inhumane acts deemed unacceptable in wartime would somehow be considered humane and permissible in peacetime.

### [Q6-020] Crimes Against Humanity Not Subject to Limitation — Opening the Door for Historical Claims
**Source**: SRC-Q6-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Explains the legal mechanism that overcame the statute of limitations barrier.

> Given that, according to Article 26 of the Preliminary Title of the Belgian Code of Criminal Procedure, (1) a civil action can never be time-barred before a criminal action if based on the latter, and (2) international crimes are not subject to a limitations period, one could argue that civil actions grounded in international crimes are likewise not subject to any limitations period. [...] The Court concluded that crimes against humanity were incorporated into customary international criminal law no later than December 11, 1946; i.e., the date that UNGA Resolution 95(I) was adopted.

### [Q6-021] Potential Precedent Value Beyond Belgium — But Limitations in Civil Law Systems
**Source**: SRC-Q6-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Directly addresses the precedent question.

> This judgment may also have significant repercussions beyond the Belgian national legal order. Indeed, according to Article 38(1)(d) of the International Court of Justice's Statute, judicial decisions of national courts may serve as subsidiary means for determining rules of international law or may represent relevant "state practice" for identifying customary international law. By recognizing that large-scale atrocities committed against civilians within the framework of a colonial administration can constitute crimes against humanity, which are not subject to a period of limitation (at least when such acts occurred after the recognition of this concept in 1946) this jurisprudence may open the door to future liability claims for colonial violations in other jurisdictions.

### [Q6-022] Limitation: No System of Binding Precedent in Civil Law Countries
**Source**: SRC-Q6-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Important caveat on the precedent value.

> This may inspire other métis to initiate proceedings before Belgian courts and tribunals but whether compensation will be granted will have to be decided by courts and tribunals on a case-by-case basis as civil law countries such as Belgium do not know the system of binding precedents.

### [Q6-023] HRW: Landmark Win for Reparations Movement
**Source**: SRC-C-008 [existing]
**Relevance**: Contextualizes the ruling within the broader reparations movement.

> A positive example is a 2024 Belgian high court decision in which the court found that the ongoing impacts on victims of crimes against humanity ensuing from Belgian racist colonial policies gave rise to victims' claims for compensation by today's Belgian government.

**Assessment**: This question is fully answered. The Brussels Court of Appeal (December 2, 2024) found Belgium civilly liable for crimes against humanity in its colonial treatment of métis children, ordering EUR 50,000 per claimant. The ruling's most significant legal contribution is its rejection of the "not illegal at the time" defense, holding that crimes against humanity existed under customary international law from 1946 regardless of armed conflict nexus. As a civil law jurisdiction, Belgium does not have a binding precedent system, but the ruling may serve as "state practice" under ICJ Statute Article 38(1)(d) and inspire similar claims in other jurisdictions. The ruling is narrow (limited to the specific métis abduction policy) but its reasoning is broad enough to apply to other colonial-era atrocities.

## Question: What legal strategies are being developed by the Pan-African Lawyers Union and the AU Legal Experts Reference Group for enforcement mechanisms, and how do they relate to the broader debate between political settlement and legal adjudication?

### [Q6-024] Extensive Consultations with PALU and AU Legal Experts Reference Group
**Source**: SRC-A-007 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents the consultation process led by Ghana's President Mahama with PALU and the AU Legal Experts Reference Group.

> "Ghana has undertaken extensive consultations to strengthen the resolution. We've engaged with UNESCO, the Global Group of Experts on Reparations, the Pan-African Lawyers Union, academic institutions, the African Union Committee of Experts on Reparations, and the African Union Legal Experts Reference Group. We hosted the inaugural joint meeting of the African Union Committee of Experts on Reparations and the African Union Legal Experts Reference Group in Accra earlier this month to further refine the text of the resolution."

### [Q6-025] Jus Cogens Legal Foundation
**Source**: SRC-A-007 [existing]
**Relevance**: Documents the specific legal argument being deployed — the jus cogens prohibition of slavery.

> "The initiative is firmly grounded in international law. Slavery is prohibited under international law as a peremptory norm, a jus cogent principle from which no derogation is permitted," the president said during the summit.

### [Q6-026] The AU's Dual Strategy: Political Engagement Supported by Legal Infrastructure
**Source**: SRC-E-003 [existing]
**Relevance**: Articulates the recommended strategic approach — primarily political engagement with legal tools as support.

> In view of the constraints and biases that exist within the international legal system, the article suggests that the AU should pursue the reparatory justice agenda primarily through political engagements and the forging of global alliances, while using opportunities presented by the international justice system. [...] Legal action should be supported through diplomatic pressure. Crucially, Africa must consider designing its trade policy towards the EU and the USA in ways that make access to African resources and markets conditional to Europe being acceptable to repair the damage they caused through the enslavement and colonisation of Africans.

### [Q6-027] AU Institutional Architecture for Reparations
**Source**: SRC-Q6-005 [new]
**Relevance**: Documents the institutional mechanisms the AU has established including the Committee of Experts on Reparations.

> Ambassador Amr Aljowaily, Director of the African Union Commission's Citizens and Diaspora Directorate (CIDO), reasserted the joint AU commitment to reparative justice to the diaspora and Africans in accordance with Assembly Decisions 849-931 and 934, which establish slavery, colonialism, and apartheid as crimes against humanity for which reparations are needed. He underscored AU-coordination at the system level to mainstream reparative justice across organs and member states, mobilize resources, and operationalize mechanisms like the AU Committee of Experts on Reparations.

### [Q6-028] Pan-African Parliament's Legislative and Advocacy Role

> Hon. "Reparations go beyond economic reparation to include institutional reforms, recovery of cultural artefacts, development assistance, and reforms of the educational system," he affirmed. [...] "The Pan-African Parliament will continue to be a voice for reparatory justice through its legislative and advocacy functions to drive Africa's socio-economic renaissance under Agenda 2063 and the AU's 2025 theme."

### [Q6-029] African Commission Resolution 543 on Reparative Mechanisms

> African Commission on Human and Peoples' Rights, Hon. Based on the Durban Declaration and the Abuja Proclamation, she spoke about the Commission's Resolution 543 condemning modern slavery and calling for national reparative mechanisms.

### [Q6-030] The Broader Debate: Political Settlement vs. Legal Adjudication
**Source**: SRC-E-007 [existing]
**Relevance**: Academic analysis of the tension between legal and political pathways.

> Reparations are often seen as victim-centred measures to address past atrocities, but how far in the past should such remedies stretch? [...] While a collective approach to reparations as promoted by CARICOM is likely to be more palatable for responsible states, and more workable in legal terms, there remain serious legal challenges in finding sufficient evidence of a causal nexus between contemporary suffering and historical wrongs to satisfy judicial requirements. Paired with the difficulty of establishing that the wrongdoing was prohibited in law at the time the acts were committed, this leads away from the conclusion that a legal claim would be successful. However, in political terms specific evidentiary requirements and the distinction between law and morality can be more flexible, allowing claims which might not succeed on the basis of legal doctrine to conclude on the basis of justice.

### [Q6-031] ILC Articles on State Responsibility: The Legal Basis for Continuing Obligations
**Source**: SRC-D-001 [existing]
**Relevance**: Identifies the specific international law provisions that underpin the legal case.

> The ILC's Articles on State Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts state in Article 31, "The responsible State is under an obligation to make full reparation for the injury caused by the internationally wrongful act. Injury includes any damage, whether material or moral, caused by the internationally wrongful act of a State." With regards to slavery and colonization, this is complemented by Article 14 which notes, "The breach of an international obligation by an act of a State having a continuing character extends over the entire period during which the act continues and remains not in conformity with the international obligation."

**Assessment**: This question is partially answered. The specific legal strategies being developed by PALU and the AU Legal Experts Reference Group are not publicly available in detail — their work is described as "refining the text of the resolution" and building "legal frameworks," but the specific enforcement mechanisms they propose remain largely undisclosed. What is documented is: (1) the jus cogens legal argument as the foundation; (2) the ICJ advisory opinion pathway as a key legal strategy; (3) the dual approach of political engagement supported by legal tools; (4) the institutional architecture (Committee of Experts on Reparations, Legal Experts Reference Group, Pan-African Parliament); (5) the African Commission's Resolution 543 calling for national reparative mechanisms. The broader debate clearly favors a primarily political approach, with legal mechanisms serving to strengthen bargaining positions rather than achieve binding judgments. Remaining gap: specific PALU enforcement mechanism proposals and AU Legal Experts Reference Group working documents.
