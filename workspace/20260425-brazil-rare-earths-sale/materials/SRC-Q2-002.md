Brazil's PL 2.780/2024: Critical Minerals Policy and Mining Veto Explained
BY MUFLIH HIDAYAT ON MAY 5, 2026

What PL 2.780/2024 Actually Proposes

At its core, PL 2.780/2024 establishes a National Policy for Critical and Strategic Minerals (PNMCE) designed to govern how Brazil identifies, regulates, and derives industrial value from its mineral wealth. The legislation is broad in scope and consequential in its institutional implications.

The bill was reported by Deputy Arnaldo Jardim, whose approach reflects a deliberate choice in favour of market-based instruments over state intervention. The final report explicitly excludes two proposals that had animated significant political debate: the creation of a state-owned mineral enterprise and restrictions on the export of mineral commodities. This was not an accidental omission but a considered positioning of the legislation as a regulatory framework rather than an industrial command structure.

The bill was progressing under a regime of urgency in the Chamber of Deputies, with a vote scheduled for May 5, 2026 following a postponement from the original April 22 date. That postponement was not procedural delay but deliberate negotiation space. The intervening period facilitated an agreement between the rapporteur and the Federal Government, specifically the Ministries of Mines and Energy and Industry, to strengthen the regulatory role of the State without crossing into operational control.

What emerged from those negotiations is a framework built on four interlocking structural pillars.

Pillar One: Regulatory Governance Through the CMCE

The Comitê de Minerais Críticos e Estratégicos (CMCE) is the institutional centrepiece of the bill. Linked to the National Council for Mineral Policy, this body is designed to function as a geopolitical safeguard rather than an operational regulator.

The CMCE's authority is concentrated in two key functions:
- Analysing and approving or rejecting changes in corporate structure involving companies that operate with critical or strategic minerals
- Monitoring international contracts to ensure that domestic supply is not compromised and that the country's geopolitical security is preserved

Pillar Two: Productive Verticalization

The second structural element addresses the long-standing critique that Brazil exports geological value while importing industrial value. The legislation proposes incentives to ensure that processing and industrialization stages occur on Brazilian territory rather than abroad.

Priority sectors include:
- Lithium battery fabrication and related electrochemical components
- Rare earth element processing and component manufacturing
- Technology-intensive transformation of mineral commodities before export

Pillar Three: The Mineral Activity Guarantee Fund

The third pillar is the most immediately tangible in financial terms. The bill proposes a Fundo Garantidor da Atividade Mineral with an initial target allocation of R$ 5 billion, structured to generate up to R$ 50 billion in new mineral project investment through credit guarantee mechanisms.

This 10:1 leverage ratio is not incidental. The fund is designed to address a specific market failure: the perception of excessive risk that prevents private capital from committing to long-cycle, capital-intensive mineral projects. By providing guarantees that facilitate credit access rather than injecting equity directly, the structure maintains market discipline while reducing the cost of capital for qualifying projects.

The fund operates on a guarantee model rather than a direct investment model, which means private sector risk assessment remains active while the cost of project financing is structurally reduced.

Pillar Four: Federal Mineral Transformation Program

The fourth pillar establishes a federal program to coordinate the transformation of mineral resources into higher-value industrial products. The operational details of this program are subject to further regulatory definition following legislative approval.

Clarifying the Veto Narrative: What the CMCE Can and Cannot Do

One of the most widely circulated characterisations of PL 2.780/2024 minerais críticos veto à venda de mineradoras is that it introduces a veto on the sale of mining companies. This reading requires careful unpacking because it is simultaneously partially accurate and significantly misleading.

The CMCE does hold approval authority over changes in corporate structure involving critical mineral operators. In that narrow sense, a transaction that the committee rejects effectively cannot proceed. However, the framework this represents is a geopolitical review mechanism, not a prohibition on foreign ownership or private asset transfers.

The practical implication is that a mining company operating with classified critical minerals can still be sold, restructured, or acquired. It simply must pass through a regulatory review process that evaluates whether the transaction creates unacceptable risks to domestic supply security or geopolitical positioning.

This is a model already familiar in other jurisdictions, including foreign investment review frameworks that scrutinise acquisitions of strategic assets without prohibiting them outright. The novelty in the Brazilian context is the application of this logic specifically to the mineral sector and its codification within a dedicated policy framework.

The State Versus Market Debate Behind the Legislation

Two competing models were active in the legislative debate before the rapporteur's framework prevailed.

The first was the Terrabras proposal, which would have created a state-owned enterprise specifically to operate in the critical minerals sector. This model drew on the historical precedent of Petrobras in oil and gas and Embraer in aerospace. The Ministry of Industry and Commerce (MDIC) publicly stated that there is no necessity for the Terrabras model, signalling government preference for the regulatory approach.

The second competing model, associated with the PT's legislative positioning, advocated for the CPRM (the Geological Survey of Brazil) to serve as a state operator in rare earth extraction. This would transform an existing scientific and geological institution into a commercial operator.

Neither proposal prevailed. The final framework positions the State as regulator and guarantor, not operator or owner.

Key Questions the Legislation Leaves Open

Which minerals are formally classified as critical and strategic? The bill establishes governance for classified minerals but the specific list requires regulatory definition.

How will the guarantee fund be capitalized and governed? The R$ 5 billion target is a legislative objective, not an appropriated allocation.

What criteria govern CMCE approval or rejection of corporate changes? This is the most consequential undefined element for investment risk assessment.

Can CMCE decisions be appealed through Brazilian administrative or judicial process? The legal review pathway for rejected transactions has significant implications for investment security.
