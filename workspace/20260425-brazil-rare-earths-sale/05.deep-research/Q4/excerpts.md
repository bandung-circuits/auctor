### [Q4-003] Pela Ema's Geological Uniqueness — Proterozoic Alkali Granite with Three Evolutionary Stages
**Source**: SRC-E-008 [existing]
**Relevance**: Detailed geological description of Pela Ema's formation process and its uniqueness

> "The Serra Verde REE deposit is associated with a Proterozoic alkali granite within the Goias province of Brazil. The REE introduction and enrichment may be related to three main stages of geological evolution:
> - Emplacement of the Pela Ema tin granites (~1.8 Ga). This resulted in the formation of typical tin-bearing greisen veins hosting monazite and xenotime.
> - Intrusion of an evolved alkaline (possibly carbonatite) complex at depth (~540 Ma). This caused potassium metasomatism (K-feldspar-biotite) with additional introduction or recrystallisation of monazite, xenotime and magnetite.
> - Lateritisation, possibly during the Miocene."
> 
> "A 30-35 metre thick lateritic weathering profile developed, resulting in the breakdown of feldspars to clay and the dissolution of REE-bearing minerals with reprecipitation in the saprolite ore zone."
> 
> "The Serra Verde deposit is rich in recoverable REE, with an inferred resource of 458 million metric tonnes at a grade of 0.098 per cent total REE (May 2012, 43-101 compliant). Heavy rare earth elements (HREE) account for 24 per cent of the deposit."
> 
> "Initial testing suggests that more than 50 per cent of the REE bulk is sorbed on clay and can be recovered by a simple elution process."

### [Q4-008] True IAC Deposits vs. Clay-Hosted Residual Monazite — Many "New" Deposits May Not Be True Ionic Clays
**Source**: SRC-Q4-007 [new]
**Relevance**: Critical distinction — many newly announced "ionic clay" deposits may actually be residual monazite-xenotime accumulations, not true IAC, affecting the actual count of genuine ionic clay deposits globally

> "It appears many of the new group of announced deposits could be clay-hosted, residual monazite-xenotime accumulations and not true IAC. Solubilizing monazite and xenotime is a known commercial process and the costs are well-defined but are significantly greater than for IAC extraction."

> "The REEs from the decomposed minerals migrate downwards as REE-ions in solution which can adsorb onto clay minerals such as kaolinite and become IAC deposits. Alternatively, the percolating solutions can combine with phosphate or carbonate to form secondary minerals (often in a colloidal phase) in a neutralization step. The more resistive minerals such as monazite and xenotime remain unaltered and can accumulate physically."

**Assessment**: This question is substantially answered. We now have a clear picture that genuine ionic clay (IAC) deposits are extremely rare outside China and Myanmar. Brazil (Serra Verde/Pela Ema, Caldeira, Carina, Colossus), Chile (Penco Module), Uganda (Makuutu), and Madagascar (early exploration) represent the only known non-Asian ionic clay deposits, with Australia still in research stage. Pela Ema's uniqueness lies in: (1) its 1.8 Ga tin granite parent rock with multi-stage REE enrichment (greisen, metasomatism, lateritisation); (2) 24% HREE content; (3) >50% of REE sorbed on clay recoverable by simple elution; (4) 458Mt inferred resource. However, a key caveat from SRC-Q4-007: many newly announced "ionic clay" projects globally may not be true IAC deposits but rather residual monazite-xenotime accumulations, which require more expensive processing.

## Question: What processing technologies and expertise are required to extract heavy rare earths from ionic clay deposits, and what is the current global distribution of such processing capability?

### [Q4-009] Processing Technologies for IAC Deposits — Three Leaching Methods
**Source**: SRC-Q4-001 [new]
**Relevance**: Describes the specific extraction methods used for ionic clay deposits

> "The extraction of rare earths from ionic clay deposits employs in-situ leaching (ISL) or in-situ recovery (ISR) techniques, representing a significant departure from conventional mining methods. This process follows a systematic approach:
> 1. Injection of mild lixiviants (leaching solutions) into the clay deposit
> 2. Dissolution of rare earth elements from clay particles
> 3. Recovery of the pregnant solution containing dissolved REEs
> 4. Processing of the solution to precipitate a mixed rare earth carbonate
> 5. Progressive land rehabilitation after extraction"

### [Q4-010] Chinese IAC Processing — Low-Cost Leaching with Solvent Extraction
**Source**: SRC-Q4-007 [new]
**Relevance**: Describes the specific processing technology China developed for IAC deposits

> "In regolith deposits, the Chinese found that weakly acidic ammonium sulphate or sodium chloride solution readily reclaims the rare earths from the ionic bonded clays allowing the resulting crude solution to be chemically treated to eliminate contaminants for further solvent extraction separation and refining. This processing can be in-situ leaching; heap leaching; or in-tank leaching with increasing cost and all with significant environmental impact."
> 
> "Generally, Chinese costs for REE reclamation from IAC deposits are low and despite the low recoveries peaking at around 30% to 40% in final products, these projects appear to be economic."

### [Q4-016] Recovery Rates from IAC — Pela Ema >50% by Simple Elution
**Source**: SRC-E-008 [existing]
**Relevance**: Quantifies how much can be recovered from Pela Ema's ionic clay using simple processing

> "Initial testing suggests that more than 50 per cent of the REE bulk is sorbed on clay and can be recovered by a simple elution process."

**Assessment**: This question is substantially answered. The processing technology for IAC deposits involves: (1) initial leaching (in-situ, heap, or in-tank leaching with ammonium sulphate or sodium chloride solutions to desorb REE ions from clay); (2) precipitation of mixed REE carbonate from the pregnant solution; (3) solvent extraction separation to isolate individual REE oxides (the most complex and expensive step); (4) metallization (oxide reduction to metal — electrochemical or calciothermic reduction at >1000 degrees C); (5) alloy production and magnet fabrication. China developed low-cost IAC extraction but only achieves 30-40% recoveries. Pela Ema uses agitated tank leaching and claims >50% recovery by simple elution. The critical bottleneck is solvent extraction separation, which China dominates at 91% of global capacity, with only a few facilities outside China (Malaysia, US, Estonia). No dedicated IAC processing facilities exist outside China — the processing gap applies to all REE types, not specifically IAC.

### [Q4-039] Value-Add Gap — Concentrate Worth $5/kg vs. Separated Oxide Worth $50+/kg
**Source**: SRC-Q4-002 [new]
**Relevance**: Quantifies how lack of processing capability destroys bargaining power — host country gets only concentrate value

> "One kilogram of neodymium oxide, present in these rare earths, is worth at least 10 times more than the US $5 dollars for a kilo of concentrate, Landgraf told IPS from Sao Paulo."
> 
> "The critical phase of processing, and the one that adds the most value, is the separation of rare earth elements. This phase is costly due to the numerous and successive treatments, not so much because of the technology."

**Assessment**: This question is substantially answered. The evidence clearly shows that geological scarcity of ionic clay HREE deposits does NOT directly translate to higher bargaining power for host countries. The relationship is mediated by processing capability. Key dynamics: (1) HREE pricing shows 5-6x disparity between China and international markets, driven by processing concentration (not deposit rarity); (2) Deposit rarity creates a "strategic scarcity premium" that attracts foreign investment (US paid $2.8B for Pela Ema based on deposit profile, not current revenue); (3) But the host country's bargaining power is undermined because: (a) without processing capability, the host can only sell concentrate at $5/kg vs. separated oxide at $50+/kg; (b) the rarity attracts powerful foreign actors who lock up output in exclusive offtake agreements (15-year US SPV commitment); (c) blocking exploitation paradoxically sustains Chinese monopoly by removing alternatives; (4) PIIE's formulation: "geography determines opportunities, but institutions determine who seizes them"; (5) Indonesia's nickel model shows that only countries with continental scale and diversified exports can leverage processing mandates to shift bargaining power; (6) Jack Lifton's assessment: IAC deposits outside China will not change the permanent magnet equation because processing access matters more than deposit access.
