# Historical Context — Relevant Excerpts

## [M-C-001] Brazil's Long History with Rare Earth Elements: From 1885 Monazite to Present
**Source**: SRC-C-006
**Relevance**: Provides the foundational historical timeline of Brazil's rare earth industry from colonial-era extraction to modern day, showing this is not a new sector but one with deep historical roots.

> Brazil's relationship with rare earth elements dates back to 1885, when the extraction of monazite sand began in the Prado region of Bahia. For decades, Brazil, alongside India, led the world market until the mid-1950s, when they were eventually overtaken by producers like South Africa that modernized their operations.

> Today, Brazil holds the third-largest rare earth reserves globally, with approximately 22 million tonnes primarily distributed across the states of Minas Gerais, Bahia, Goiás, and São Paulo.

> Despite these vast resources, Brazil's current production remains limited, with estimates ranging from 80 to 2,000 tonnes annually, a fraction of the global supply of approximately 300,000 tonnes.

## [M-C-002] Brazil's 1960s-70s Rare Earth Activity and Missed Opportunities
**Source**: SRC-C-006 (originally from IBRAM)
**Relevance**: Shows the cyclical pattern of Brazil recognizing but failing to capitalize on its rare earth wealth — a pattern relevant to understanding the current Serra Verde sale debate.

> In the 1960s-70s, monazite sands made headlines. Brazil even had a modest production of rare earth elements from marine monazite alluviums. Subsequently, the large carbonatite complexes of Araxá, Catalão, Poços de Caldas, Mato Preto, Salitre and several others, mainly in Minas Gerais and Goiás, were identified as possible sources of rare earths. All with large quantities of rare earths. In the Amazon, the Seis Lagos Complex remains completely abandoned despite enormous reserves of niobium and rare earths.

> We know well where our rare earths are, but in no case do we have a mine where rare earths are the main product. Despite large and rich deposits, our rare earths remain in the ground waiting for investments, technology and political will.

## [M-C-003] The Six-Decade Global Rare Earth Competition: US vs China
**Source**: SRC-C-003
**Relevance**: Provides essential historical context for why the Serra Verde acquisition by a US company is geopolitically significant — it is the latest move in a 60-year struggle for rare earth supply chain control.

> Historically, in the California desert, Mountain Pass has been a rare earth elements mine since its discovery in 1949 by the Molybdenum Corporation of America, peaking in production from the mid-1960s to the 1980s, and representing US control of the market over this period. However, challenges appeared in the form of environmental movements and regulatory pressures, leading companies to explore alternatives or relocate their industries to China.

> In the 1990s, China emerged as a dominant force in rare earth production. Accounting for 85-95% of the global supply. Deng Xiaoping's vision outlined in 1992 aimed for China to lead the world in the rare earth industry, famously saying that, "The Middle East has oil, China has rare earth."

> Magnequench, a rare earth-specialized company and subsidiary of General Motors, was acquired by a Chinese state-owned enterprise during the late 90s, with Deng Xiaoping's son-in-law serving as the new leader of the company.

## [M-C-004] The 2010 Senkaku Islands Crisis: Rare Earths as a Geopolitical Weapon
**Source**: SRC-C-003
**Relevance**: The 2010 Japan-China incident established the precedent for rare earths being used as geopolitical leverage — the key event that woke the world to supply chain vulnerability and ultimately motivated projects like Serra Verde.

> In 2010, China accounted for 95% of the world's rare earth oxides, and in that same year, a Chinese fishing trawler collided with two Japanese Coast Guard vessels near the disputed Senkaku Islands. As part of the backlash, China temporarily halted its rare earth exports to Japan, raising concerns that it might use its dominance in the rare earth market as a diplomatic tool.

> The incident caused prices to surge by up to 500% in 2011 and 2012, driven by speculation, and this impelled heightened awareness in the West about Chinese dominance, resulting in the start of over 200 new projects globally to diversify the supply chain. The USA's Mountain Pass mine was thus revived in 2012.

> The EU, Japan, and the US filed complaints with the WTO in 2012 against Chinese export restrictions on rare earths, culminating in a decision that deemed Chinese practices inconsistent with WTO commitments.

## [M-C-005] Serra Verde's 14-Year Development Timeline: From Exploration Claims to Commercial Production
**Source**: SRC-C-002
**Relevance**: The Serra Verde project's 14-year development history (2010-2024) illustrates the enormous time and capital investment required to bring a rare earth mine online, contextualizing the magnitude of the $2.8 billion acquisition.

> In March 2010, we submitted exploration claims to ANM (the Brazilian Mining Agency) covering 23 areas between the states of Goiás and Tocantins with the objective of better understanding the geological potential of the area.

> Timeline highlights:
> - 2010: Airborne geophysical survey
> - 2011: Start of exploration program
> - 2012: Preliminary Economic Assessment
> - 2014: Pre-Feasibility Study completed
> - 2015: Mining plan and Certification of Pela Ema rare earth deposit
> - 2019: Installation License granted; Completion of Definitive Feasibility Study
> - 2020: Received final construction permits and lifetime mining concession
> - 2023: VBR and EMG invest in Serra Verde; Commissioning begins; Received Environmental Operating License
> - 2024: Start of commercial production

> Serra Verde is one of the largest known ionic clay deposits in the world. The site is located in a formation developed from the Serra Dourada granite, the largest intrusion of the Goiás Tin Province.

## [M-C-006] USA Rare Earth's Rapid Build-Up: From Startup to $2.8 Billion Brazilian Acquisition
**Source**: SRC-C-004
**Relevance**: Shows how USA Rare Earth evolved from a 2019 startup to acquiring Serra Verde, with key backing from the Trump Administration — contextualizing the political dimensions of the deal.

> USA Rare Earth was founded in 2019 by Pini Althaus. The company initially focused on acquiring and developing domestic US resources to support a "mine-to-magnet" strategy.

> In September 2025, USA Rare Earth announced a binding agreement to acquire Less Common Metals, a UK-based rare earth metals and alloys manufacturer, for $100 million.

> On January 2026, the Trump Administration announced a US$1.6 billion debt-and-equity investment in the company acquiring a 10% stake in the company aimed at growing domestic mining and production of REEs.

> On April 20, 2026, USA Rare Earth announced the terms of a definitive agreement to acquire the Serra Verde Group for $2.8 billion. Serra Verde's Pela Ema mine in Goiás, Brazil, is the only scaled producer of all four magnetic rare earth elements outside of Asia: Neodymium (Nd), Praseodymium (Pr), Dysprosium (Dy), and Terbium (Tb).

## [M-C-007] Brazil's Constitutional Framework for Strategic Minerals
**Source**: SRC-C-007
**Relevance**: The legal basis invoked by Rede Sustentabilidade in their Supreme Court filing — Article 176 of the Constitution — has deep historical roots in Brazil's approach to mineral sovereignty, making this the legal-historical backdrop of the current challenge.

> Brazilian mining law is mainly regulated by Decree-Law No. 227 of 28 February 1967 ("Mining Code"). Mining activities must also comply with the Brazilian Federal Constitution.

> Article 176 of the Brazilian Federal Constitution and Article 1,230 of the Brazilian Civil Code set forth that mineral resources are property of the federal government. The Federal Constitution provides that reconnaissance, exploration or mining activities are of national interest and are considered activities of public utility.

> A foreign company cannot own a reconnaissance, exploration or mining permit in Brazil. However, a Brazilian company that holds such permit may be fully owned by a foreign company with the totality of its shares.

> The launch in April 2025 of the National Policy on Strategic Minerals aimed at attracting investment and strengthening the supply chain of critical minerals for the energy transition, with fiscal incentives and streamlined permitting.

## [M-C-008] Brazil's Ongoing Policy Debate: State Control vs. Private Investment in Critical Minerals
**Source**: SRC-C-005
**Relevance**: The current debate over Serra Verde's sale echoes a recurring tension in Brazilian history between state sovereignty over natural resources and reliance on foreign capital and expertise — a pattern visible since the Vargas era.

> Brazil's mining regulator chief said he expected a national policy for critical minerals to be unveiled within the next two to three months, adding that a proposal to create a state-owned company for rare earths was not a government initiative.

> Mauro Sousa, director-general of Brazil's National Mining Agency (ANM), told a press conference that 13 bills currently before Congress addressed rare earths and critical minerals.

> New bills proposing the creation of a state-backed entity dubbed "Terrabras" have been introduced by lawmakers, prompting mining industry association Ibram to push back against the idea.

> Ibram said Brazil's weakness in rare earths mining did not stem from a lack of state involvement, but rather from limited industrial-scale separation and refining capacity, and other factors such as insufficient financing and legal uncertainty that deters investment.

> President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva has repeatedly said Brazil should not remain merely an exporter of commodities in the rare earths sector, stressing the need to develop domestic processing capacity.

## [M-C-009] Rede Sustentabilidade's History of Legal Action and Environmental Advocacy
**Source**: SRC-C-001 and Wikipedia (Sustainability Network)
**Relevance**: The party filing the Supreme Court challenge has a long history of environmental and constitutional litigation, which contextualizes why they are the ones mounting this legal challenge.

> Left-wing Brazilian political party Rede Sustentabilidade asked the country's Supreme Court to suspend the sale of mining company Serra Verde Group to USA Rare Earth Inc on national security grounds.

> The party argued that Brazilian legislation doesn't provide sufficient safeguards for the national interest as required by the Constitution in the exploitation of strategic mineral resources.

> The Sustainability Network (Rede Sustentabilidade, REDE) is an environmentalist Brazilian political party founded in 2013 by Marina Silva. The party was the author of the lawsuit that asked defendants to leave the presidential line of succession of the Republic, which was accepted on November 3, 2016 by the Supreme Federal Court. In December 2016, the network called for the removal of Senate President Renan Calheiros from the presidency, based on constitutional arguments.

> The party currently holds 4 seats in the Chamber of Deputies (out of 513) and 0 in the Federal Senate (out of 81), forming part of the PSOL REDE Federation. The party joined the coalition supporting Lula's 2022 presidential candidacy.

## [M-C-010] US Bipartisan Strategy to Break Chinese Rare Earth Dominance: Context for the Serra Verde Deal
**Source**: SRC-C-003
**Relevance**: The Serra Verde acquisition did not happen in a vacuum — it is part of a broader, multi-administration US strategy to secure non-Chinese rare earth supply chains, with both Trump and Biden administrations investing in alternatives.

> The Trump administration was committed to not depending on Chinese markets for rare earth minerals. Executive Orders 13817 and 13953 pointed out that the United States imported as much as 80% of its REEs from China. The Department of Defense allocated $30 million to support Lynas, an Australian rare earth company, aiming to establish refining facilities in Texas.

> The Biden administration continued these efforts, investing in rare-earth separation processes and committing $16 billion to the infrastructure plan. This bipartisan commitment reflects a shared goal of bolstering the American rare earths industry, acknowledging the strategic importance of securing a stable domestic supply chain.

> China's dominance in the rare earths sector poses challenges for the United States, contributing as it does to just 12.2% of global production. To close the gap, the U.S. seeks strategic partnerships and investments, notably with Australia and Canada, but also global actors like Kazakhstan.
