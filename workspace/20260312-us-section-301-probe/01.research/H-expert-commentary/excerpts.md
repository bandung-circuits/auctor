# Expert Commentary — Relevant Excerpts

## [M-H-001] PIIE Senior Fellow: Section 301 has "least limits" but overreach risk remains
**Source**: SRC-058
**Relevance**: Senior PIIE trade expert Alan Wm. Wolff assesses Section 301 as the administration's most potent alternative authority, but warns that combining multiple statutory tools to recreate the struck-down tariff wall will trigger fresh judicial scrutiny.

> The one that has the least limits and process attached to it is section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, the president's retaliatory authority. It was enacted in the period when the threat that was perceived to American commerce was posed by Japan. Congress armed the president with unprecedented and virtually unlimited authority to retaliate against unreasonable and unjustifiable policies and measures of a foreign government that burden US commerce. "Unreasonable" is a very subjective standard. Any court would have trouble substituting its judgment for that of the president.
>
> The risk for the Trump administration is that it again engages in overreach. Will the use of alternative statutory authorities, in a transparent sham to largely restore the tariff wall, be rejected by the Supreme Court? If they in effect give him tablets that apply tariffs as broadly but citing other statutory authorities, that is likely to trigger the same reaction from the courts that he received last week, despite the existence of other authorities to impose tariffs.
>
> While the decision of the court on February 20 could be seen as being narrow, combining these other authorities to recreate the "reciprocal" tariffs will inescapably raise the larger question of whether the president and not the Congress has the tariff power. Article I referencing the powers of Congress contains the tariff power, not Article II. The attempt by the president to go too far with these delegated authorities will almost certainly be seen as illegitimate.

## [M-H-002] Atlantic Council: Section 301 is "plan B" — a tried-and-true tool providing new leverage
**Source**: SRC-059
**Relevance**: Former assistant USTR Mark Linscott characterizes Section 301 as a credible, durable tool but warns the administration must use it wisely or risk losing trading partners' cooperation.

> The Trump administration and the USTR are proceeding with plan B, namely the initiation of trade barrier investigations under Section 301 of the 1974 Trade Act. A more predictable, defensible, and legally credible approach could have been to initiate multiple Section 301 investigations on a country-by-country and sector-by-sector basis. It is a tried-and-true tool that has been around even before the WTO was established, and it has proven durable over the decades since. It has the advantage of providing impressive USTR authority to impose tariffs and other trade restrictions to address a full range of foreign trade barriers. The rub is that Section 301 investigations require a well-established process of consultations, opportunity for comment, hearings, timetables, and published findings that the president likely felt he could dispense with by using IEEPA authority.
>
> However, it will be critical for the Trump administration to use this tool wisely. If US trading partners find that their good-faith efforts to settle on terms for ARTs are rewarded with additional tariffs, then they may start to withdraw from these agreements, walk away from further negotiations, and reimpose higher tariffs and NTBs. Some may even choose to retaliate with even higher tariffs of their own. This is a true turning point for the Trump administration's trade policy.

## [M-H-003] Brookings experts: Section 301 restores structure but uncertainty persists for business
**Source**: SRC-043
**Relevance**: Brookings Director of Trade and Economic Statecraft Kari Heerman and panelists analyze the shift from IEEPA to Section 301, noting it restores process but does not change the administration's tariff-centric strategy.

> The Supreme Court's decision removed IEEPA as an open-ended authority for adjusting tariffs, pushing the administration back onto statutes with defined procedural constraints. It did not alter the administration's reliance on tariffs as a central policy instrument. The leverage derived from the credible threat of future tariff escalation remains intact.
>
> Shifting tariff policy from emergency authority to Section 301 does not alter the administration's strategy. It does, however, restore structure. Section 301 requires investigations, public notice and comment, consultation with trading partners, and formal findings—procedural steps largely absent under IEEPA.
>
> [Emily Blanchard] emphasized the realization that tariffs were likely to endure may be more consequential than the Court's ruling itself. Strategic ambiguity in tariff negotiations may be useful in bilateral dealmaking, but it is "kryptonite for firms" trying to plan in a globally integrated production system.
>
> For U.S. trading partners, the shift away from emergency authority changes the process, not the trajectory. Governments are making "no sudden movements."

## [M-H-004] Brookings experts: Tariffs as fiscal policy — $3 trillion revenue and the reshoring contradiction
**Source**: SRC-057
**Relevance**: Multiple Brookings scholars highlight the fundamental contradiction between using tariffs for revenue generation and for reshoring manufacturing, and note the real-economy impact on consumers and small businesses.

> William Gale and Elena Patel: Tariff policies would raise around $3 trillion over the next decade. The administration has presented tariffs as a tool to bring manufacturing back while also touting them as a major revenue source. Those goals conflict. Tariffs raise revenue precisely because imports continue. Aggregate trade deficits have not noticeably narrowed and manufacturing employment has remained broadly flat.
>
> Tonantzin Carmona: The U.S. average effective tariff rate climbed to nearly 17%, the highest since the early 1930s. Research from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York found that nearly 90% of those costs were borne by American firms and consumers. The Tax Foundation estimated tariffs added about $1,000 to household costs in 2025 and as much as $1,300 in 2026. A survey by Main Street Alliance found that 81.5% raised or considered raising prices, 41.7% delayed or considered delaying expansion, and nearly one-third anticipated layoffs.
>
> Sanjay Patnaik: The turn by the U.S. towards protectionism is deeply concerning for the long-term prospects of the U.S. economy. Higher prices due to tariffs hit low-income families the hardest.

## [M-H-006] Brookings: Section 301 and Section 232 — established but strained tools for systemic redesign
**Source**: SRC-057
**Relevance**: Kari Heerman of Brookings argues that statutory authorities designed for episodic use are being repurposed for systemic economic policy, creating incoherence and legal vulnerability.

> Kari Heerman: Section 232 and Section 301 provide established pathways for sustained tariffs. Unlike IEEPA, these authorities require investigations and formal findings. Yet, absent new congressional direction—and without consensus around a renewed multilateral framework—these statutory authorities have become the default tools for shaping the tariff regime. A more stable approach would require an articulation of how economic performance, strategic competition, domestic politics, and foreign policy fit together in modern U.S. trade strategy.

## [M-H-007] Brookings: Tariffs are a blunt tool — industrial policy alternatives needed
**Source**: SRC-043
**Relevance**: Brookings analysis argues tariffs are poorly suited for rebuilding manufacturing in a globally integrated economy, and more direct industrial policy tools would be more effective.

> Tariffs are a blunt tool for rebuilding manufacturing capacity in a globally integrated economy. U.S. manufacturers rely heavily on imported intermediates. Broad border tariffs tend to raise costs for domestic producers embedded in global value chains more reliably than they exert sustained pressure on foreign suppliers. Rather than enhancing competitiveness, they risk eroding it.
>
> If the primary goal is to build sectoral capacity, more direct industrial policy tools may be better aligned. Targeted subsidies, tax credits, procurement policies, workforce development, and regulatory design can be calibrated to specific production outcomes in ways tariffs cannot.
>
> Meanwhile, U.S. allies are adjusting—economically and strategically. Many are deepening economic ties with one another while recalibrating to a more protectionist and less predictable United States. Partners are diversifying partnerships, reducing exposure to U.S. volatility, and hedging against uncertainty. This quiet reshuffling of economic relationships may outlast any single administration.

## [M-H-009] Industry experts: Section 301 investigations are "checking the boxes" — outcomes largely predetermined
**Source**: SRC-054
**Relevance**: Former Biden-era assistant USTR Joshua Kagan and FDRA CEO Matt Priest provide industry-insider assessments that the investigations are designed to regenerate IEEPA-like leverage rather than genuine fact-finding.

> Joshua Kagan, special counsel at Kelley Drye and former Biden-era assistant USTR: "I suspect the aim is to have varying rates on a country-by-country basis, as in IEEPA, both to dissuade acts, policies, and practices that the U.S. government finds to violate the statute and to generate potential bilateral negotiating leverage." Nevertheless, applying Section 301 this way is unprecedented, he noted.
>
> Matt Priest, CEO and president of the Footwear Distributors and Retailers of America (FDRA): "With IEEPA being taken away, they've lost a lot of leverage, and they're trying to regenerate that." Priest noted that the timing of investigations is no coincidence: "Turning up the heat on China weeks ahead of a meeting in Beijing between Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping and targeting the EU, which recently put its reciprocal trade agreement with the U.S. 'on ice,' are almost certainly calculated attempts to exert control over negotiations."
>
> "It sets a stage, in particular, with the developing countries… If they're at the table for an agreement on reciprocal trade, I suspect the administration is trying to create leverage to continue to push those agreements through."
>
> "I think a lot of the 'investigative' part in their minds is probably just checking the boxes on process; a lot of their concerns have been well documented. I don't want to say the outcomes are totally baked, but at the same time, they just released the annual review of trade barriers that U.S. companies face last week, and that's kind of the playbook."

## [M-H-014] Brookings Kyle Chan: Ruling "ultimately strengthens" US approach to China by restoring institutional channels
**Source**: SRC-057
**Relevance**: Brookings China expert argues the Supreme Court ruling and resulting shift to Section 301 actually improves U.S. trade policy toward China by replacing blunt instruments with targeted, evidence-based mechanisms.

> Kyle Chan: By limiting IEEPA, the ruling ultimately strengthens the U.S. approach to China. It pushes trade policy back toward proper legal and institutional channels, focusing Washington on core trade and national security issues with China rather than wielding tariffs as ad-hoc leverage across a broad range of foreign policy disputes. Removing IEEPA as a tariff justification limits self-inflicted harm by the United States. Last April's "Liberation Day" tariffs prompted strong retaliation by China, turmoil in financial markets, and needless strain among allies. Broad tariffs on key trading partners undermined efforts to coordinate trade action on China.
