US Trade Experts: Section 301 Probe Signals Tariffs Will Stay - Seoul Economic Daily

International | Published 2026.03.12. 12:02:33 | By Kyunghwan Yoon, New York Correspondent

As the Donald Trump administration announced the launch of Section 301 trade investigations against South Korea, China, Japan and others, US trade experts said the move should be interpreted as a message that "tariffs will continue despite the Supreme Court ruling against them." They advised South Korea not to link the investigation to the Coupang situation, to focus US investments on next-generation technologies such as small modular reactors (SMRs), and to continuously emphasize the importance of the Korea-US Free Trade Agreement (FTA) to the United States.

Lauren Mandell, partner in the international trade practice at US law firm WilmerHale, said in an interview with Seoul Economic Daily and others at the Korea Society office in Manhattan, New York, on the 11th (local time), "This is a very uncertain time when the US administration is using tools it hasn't used before." She emphasized, "But the administration's message that 'tariffs are not going away' remains consistent." Mandell is a trade expert who served six years at the Office of the US Trade Representative (USTR) during both the Barack Obama administration and the first Trump administration. She served as lead negotiator for the investment chapter of the US-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) and played a key role in reviving Section 301 of the Trade Act. "The new tariff regime under Section 122 of the Trade Act is unprecedented," Mandell said, "but the Trump administration has powerful tools to impose tariffs on South Korea or other countries."

The Trump administration imposed a 10% global tariff for up to 150 days under Section 122 of the Trade Act against the entire world on the 24th of last month—just four days after the US Supreme Court ruled on the 20th that tariffs based on the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), including reciprocal tariffs and fentanyl tariffs, were unlawful. This bought time for the Section 301 investigation, with plans to invoke it alongside Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act five months later. Section 301 of the Trade Act grants the administration authority to impose tariffs against unfair, unreasonable, or discriminatory actions by foreign governments. USTR Representative Jamieson Greer announced the same day that Section 301 investigations had been initiated against 16 economic entities.

Troy Stangarone, research fellow at Carnegie Mellon's Institute for Strategic Technology, also asserted that the Trump administration's tariff stance would be difficult to reverse despite the Supreme Court ruling. Stangarone is an Indo-Pacific trade expert who long served as senior director for trade at the Korea Economic Institute (KEI). He is also regarded as having strong political acumen from his experience as a federal Senate and state government aide. "Even if the Democrats become the majority party (in the November midterm elections), unless they achieve an overwhelming majority capable of overriding the president's veto, it will be difficult to roll back," Stangarone said. "This is a characteristic of the American system."

Both experts agreed that linking the Section 301 investigation of South Korea to the Coupang situation would be a stretch. GreenOaks and Altimeter, investors in Coupang Inc, the US parent company of Coupang, had filed a petition with USTR on January 22 requesting a direct investigation under Section 301, claiming that South Korea was attacking the company at the government-wide level. They withdrew the petition on the 9th of this month after USTR indicated it would pursue a broader investigation. Regarding the US trade investigation, Prime Minister Kim Min-seok departed for the United States on the 12th. Prime Minister Kim had also visited the United States in January to discuss trade issues with Vice President JD Vance.

"The US administration is concerned about foreign government practices that discriminate against or disadvantage US companies, and Coupang is just one of them," Mandell said. "This is not limited to South Korea but is a response to global trends including the European Union (EU)." Stangarone noted, "From the US perspective, the only thing that matters is that Coupang is legally registered in the United States, has its headquarters in the United States, and conducts its research and development (R&D) in the United States. The fact that 90% of its business is conducted in South Korea is completely irrelevant." He added, "If the Korean government demonstrates that it is delivering fair judgments based on professional and thorough investigations regarding Coupang, the United States will have no complaints."

The experts advised that South Korea should demonstrate its commitment to implementing the trade agreements promised with the United States last year. "When South Korea makes investments in the United States, it should choose next-generation technology projects such as SMRs that will contribute to the long-term competitiveness of Korean companies," Stangarone said. "It should avoid situations where money is spent just to please the Trump administration but nothing is gained." Mandell said, "The Korea-US FTA is the foundation of the bilateral trade relationship, so even if it appears the United States is not respecting the agreement, South Korea should continuously communicate its commitment to compliance. Only then can South Korea make the argument to the US administration that 'the United States benefits from the agreement.'"
