### The Resolution: Origin, Content, and Institutional Architecture
1. What is the specific content of Ghana's proposed UNGA resolution, including its legal framing and the choice to invoke jus cogens?
2. What institutional preparations preceded the resolution — AU declarations, the Year of Justice for Africans, the Committee of Experts, the Legal Reference Group?
3. How has Mahama positioned the resolution on three pillars (historical accuracy, legal defensibility, continental and diaspora alignment)?
4. Why was March 25 (International Day of Remembrance) chosen for formal presentation, and what is the strategic significance of linking historical memory to political action?
5. What is the relationship between the 2025 AU summit resolution and the UNGA resolution — is the latter a direct translation or an evolution?

### Coalition and Vote Arithmetic
6. What is the current state of AU support (40 of 55 members) and CARICOM backing, and how far does this fall short of a UNGA simple majority (97 votes)?
7. What diplomatic outreach has Ghana conducted across blocs — G77+China, Non-Aligned Movement, Commonwealth, and even the EU?
8. How does the Africa-Caribbean alliance function — from the 2023 Accra Conference through the Addis Ababa Declaration to the current coordinated push?
9. What role does the Commonwealth, and specifically Secretary-General Shirley Botchwey, play in broadening support beyond the AU-CARICOM core?

### European Resistance: Positions and Justifications
10. What are the specific positions of Britain (Starmer's categorical refusal), France (Macron's "reconciliation" framing), Germany (colonial atrocities not violating international law at the time), the Netherlands (apology plus 200-million-euro fund), and Portugal (presidential suggestion overruled by government)?
11. How did the 2001 Durban Conference establish the precedent of European resistance, with Britain, Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands blocking a formal apology in favour of "regret"?
12. How does the rise of far-right populism in Europe (Chega in Portugal, Farage in Britain, Le Pen in France) shape the political conditions for reparations diplomacy?

### Quantitative Dimensions: Scale of the Crime and Economic Legacy
13. What are the quantitative parameters of the transatlantic slave trade (12.5-15 million transported, 1.8 million dead during Middle Passage, additional 10 million through other slave trades)?
14. What does Nathan Nunn's research establish about the slave trade's long-term economic impact (72% of Africa's income gap, 99% of gap with other developing countries)?
15. What does the Brattle Group estimate for total reparations ($100-131 trillion) and how is it broken down by country?
16. What are the current indicators of ongoing structural extraction (Africa losing $500 billion annually, Ghana retaining 14% of gold export value, DRC exporting cobalt at 1% refined)?

### Historical Precedents and Legal Foundations
17. What is the history of the international reparations movement from the 1993 Abuja Proclamation through Durban 2001 to the 2023 Accra Conference?
18. How does the legal argument based on jus cogens and the ILC Articles on State Responsibility function, and what are its practical limitations?
19. What is the historical irony of Britain compensating slave owners at abolition and Haiti being forced to pay France for its own liberation?
20. How does Germany's double standard — $89 billion in Holocaust reparations while denying colonial obligations — undermine European legal arguments?

### Contradictions and Tensions
21. What is the principal contradiction between peripheral nations demanding reparatory justice and core states defending the legitimacy of the global economic order built on slavery's proceeds?
22. How does the contradiction between European rhetoric of "partnership" with Africa and the categorical refusal of material accountability manifest — and how does it push African states toward alternative partners?
23. What is the tension between reparations as systemic transformation and reparations as redistribution within existing capitalist structures?
24. How robust is the intra-African consensus given low head-of-state turnout at the September 2025 summit and some states preferring bilateral diplomacy?

### Narrative Contestation
25. How do conservative British media (Telegraph, Daily Mail) deploy the abolitionist narrative and the "Arab slave trade" deflection to resist accountability?
26. How does the "but for" argument (descendants are "better off") function as a circular logic that uses the consequences of the crime to argue against remedying it?
27. How does the framing of reparations as "politically impossible" in Western democracies naturalise the resistance of populations that benefited from slavery?

### Research Group Q1: Structural Adjustment as Neo-Colonial Continuity and the Mechanisms of Ongoing Extraction
**Priority**: High
**Rationale**: Fred M'Membe explicitly identifies structural adjustment as the mechanism bridging colonialism to the present, and John Ross traces a three-phase capital extraction model (slave trade, colonial infrastructure, IMF structural adjustment). The initial research documents the aggregate figure ($500 billion annually) but does not investigate the specific mechanisms by which post-independence economic sovereignty was denied. This group also responds to the REF-MRX-001 Layer 2 analytical gap on neocolonialism and primitive accumulation, and to the REF-MRX-003 contradiction between finance capital and investment/development needs.

1. How did IMF and World Bank structural adjustment programmes in the 1980s-1990s specifically affect public investment levels in key African economies (Zambia, Ghana, Nigeria, Tanzania), and what was the measurable impact on fixed capital formation ratios?
2. What specific mechanisms — debt structures, trade agreements, commodity pricing, conditions on development financing, ownership of extractive industries — have denied economic sovereignty to African states after formal political independence?
3. What is the empirical relationship between net fixed capital formation as a share of GDP and economic growth across major economies, and does John Ross's claim of a 0.91 correlation coefficient hold under scrutiny?
4. How do contemporary illicit financial flows from Africa ($500 billion annually, per the initial research; $88 billion annually per ISS Africa) operate — through what corporate structures, transfer pricing mechanisms, and regulatory gaps — and which specific actors and jurisdictions are involved?
5. What is the ownership structure of key extractive industries in Africa today? In Ghana's gold sector (14% value retention), the DRC's cobalt sector (1% refined domestically), and comparable cases — who owns, who profits, and through what legal arrangements?
6. How do inflated borrowing costs and credit rating practices by Western agencies structurally disadvantage African economies, and what is the quantitative cost in development terms?

### Research Group Q2: Corporate Accountability, Abolition Compensation, and the Capital Trail from Slavery to Present-Day Institutions
**Priority**: High
**Rationale**: The initial summary identifies a key gap on corporate reparations and the role of private actors. Kwesi Pratt Jr. names specific cities and institutions (Liverpool, Bristol, London, New York) as built on slavery. John Ross makes the same identification. The academic research documents Lloyd's of London's direct profit from the slave trade [M-E-024]. Pratt also highlights abolition compensation flowing to slave owners, not the enslaved. This group fills the gap on tracing the specific capital trail from slavery to present-day institutions, responding to the REF-MRX-001 Layer 2 focus on primitive accumulation.

1. What does the current state of economic historiography (Eric Williams thesis, Joseph Inikori, and more recent scholarship) establish about the proportion of British capital accumulation in Liverpool, Bristol, and the City of London that derived directly from the slave trade?
2. How was Britain's abolition compensation loan (roughly 2 billion euros in today's money) specifically deployed after emancipation — what infrastructure, industries, financial institutions, or imperial ventures were funded with it?
3. What compensation mechanisms operated at abolition in France, Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands, and where did those funds flow?
4. Which specific contemporary corporations, financial institutions, and insurance companies (Lloyd's of London, Barclays, others) have documented historical connections to the slave trade, and what has been their response to calls for corporate reparations?
5. What is the current status of corporate reparations lawsuits and campaigns — in the US (Cato et al. v. United States, other litigation), the UK, and internationally — and what legal obstacles have they encountered?

### Research Group Q3: Working-Class, Grassroots, and Diaspora Perspectives on Reparations
**Priority**: High
**Rationale**: The initial summary explicitly identifies "no direct coverage of how African publics — as opposed to governments and intellectuals — view the reparations issue" as a key gap. Irvin Jim provides a rare trade union perspective; the initial research is otherwise dominated by governmental, diplomatic, and academic voices. The REF-MRX-003 contradiction between popular classes of the Global South and the US-led imperial elite demands investigation of grassroots agency. The REF-MRX-001 Layer 3 analysis of class composition requires understanding how different social classes within Africa and the Caribbean relate to the reparations demand.

1. How do organised labour movements across Africa (NUMSA, other trade unions, workers' federations) and the Caribbean engage with the reparations demand — do they frame it differently from governmental positions, and do they link it to domestic economic struggles like nationalisation and labour rights?
2. What do opinion surveys, public discussions, and grassroots organising reveal about how ordinary African citizens — not governments or intellectuals — view the reparations issue and what forms of justice they prioritise?
3. How has the African diaspora in Europe and the Americas engaged with the Ghana resolution — through what organisations, campaigns, and political demands, and is their framing aligned with or distinct from the AU's state-to-state approach?
4. What is the relationship between domestic economic policy demands (nationalisation, land reform, industrial policy) and international reparations claims in countries like South Africa, where Irvin Jim argues colonial economic structures persist? Are these parallel struggles or parts of a single programme?
5. How does the concept of "colonialism of a special type" — where colonial economic structures persist after formal political transition — apply beyond South Africa to other African states with concentrated economic ownership?

### Research Group Q4: The Environmental Dimension and Climate Justice Linkage
**Priority**: Medium
**Rationale**: Kwesi Pratt Jr. introduces "environmental destruction" as an integral dimension of reparations, entirely absent from the initial research. The academic excerpts [M-E-023] note that colonialism damaged Africa's natural environment and made Africans more prone to climate change effects, and the AU summit linked reparations to climate injustice [M-I-013]. This group responds to the REF-MRX-001 Layer 2 analysis of ecological degradation and the metabolic rift, and to the REF-MRX-003 contradiction between Western capitalism and planet/humanity.

1. What was the ecological devastation caused by plantation economies in the Caribbean and Americas — deforestation, soil depletion, monoculture, watershed destruction — and what are its measurable continuing effects?
2. How does the pattern of colonial-era resource extraction connect to present-day environmental vulnerability in Africa and the Caribbean, particularly climate change exposure? What is the quantitative relationship between historical extraction and current ecological fragility?
3. What are the arguments and campaigns linking slavery/colonial reparations to climate justice and climate reparations — are these being pursued as parallel or integrated demands, and by which actors?
4. How does the concept of ecological debt — the environmental damage inflicted on the Global South by colonial extraction and industrialisation in the Global North — intersect with the financial reparations framework?
5. What is the environmental cost of contemporary extractive industries in Africa (mining, oil, deforestation for export agriculture) and how does this connect to the argument that the extractive logic of colonialism continues?

### Research Group Q5: Geopolitical Dynamics — China, Asian States, and the UNGA Vote
**Priority**: High
**Rationale**: The initial summary identifies "the position of Asian states (India, Indonesia, etc.) on the resolution is not documented despite their potential importance in reaching the UNGA majority" as a key gap. The initial summary also raises but does not answer the question of China's strategic interest in supporting the resolution. The REF-MRX-003 primary contradiction (moribund imperialism vs. emerging socialism led by China) and the secondary contradiction (G7 ruling classes vs. capitalist elites in Global South) both bear directly on how the vote will be determined by geopolitical alignment.

1. What is China's position on the Ghana reparations resolution, and how does it relate to China's broader strategy of building influence in Africa through the Belt and Road Initiative, FOCAC, and multilateral platforms?
2. How are major Asian states — India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Pakistan, Bangladesh — likely to vote on the resolution, given their own complex histories with colonialism and slavery, and what diplomatic outreach has Ghana conducted toward them?
3. What positions have Latin American states (Brazil, Mexico, Colombia, Bolivia) taken, given that Brazil was the largest single destination for enslaved Africans and has its own internal reparations debate?
4. How does the US position on the resolution connect to domestic US politics, particularly the African-American reparations debate (HR 40), and how might the Trump administration's stance differ from previous US positions?
5. What are the specific voting dynamics within the G77+China bloc on this resolution — are there internal divisions, and which states might abstain or vote against despite bloc solidarity expectations?
6. How does Russia position itself on the resolution, given its strategic interests in Africa and its own anti-Western diplomatic posture?

### Research Group Q6: Legal Pathways, Precedents, and Post-Resolution Mechanisms
**Priority**: Medium
**Rationale**: The initial summary identifies "no coverage of the specific legal mechanisms that would follow a successful UNGA vote — the pathway from resolution to tribunal to enforceable obligations" as a key gap. The experts do not directly address this, but the academic research [M-E-019] argues political settlement is more likely than legal adjudication, while the initial summary notes the push for a special UN reparations tribunal. This group responds to the REF-MRX-001 Layer 5 analysis of state power and international institutions.

1. What is the legal status and practical impact of a UNGA resolution — is it binding, advisory, or purely political? What precedents exist for UNGA resolutions leading to concrete legal outcomes (e.g., ICJ advisory opinions, treaty negotiations, tribunal creation)?
2. What specific legal mechanisms could follow a successful UNGA vote — a UN reparations tribunal, an ICJ advisory opinion, a new treaty framework? What precedents from other UN-created tribunals (ICTY, ICTR, special tribunals) inform this pathway?
3. How does Germany's payment of $89 billion in Holocaust reparations function as a legal and political precedent? What was the specific legal framework (Luxembourg Agreement, Claims Conference, bilateral treaties), and how applicable is it to slavery reparations?
4. What is the status and outcome of the Belgian court ruling (2024) that found the state responsible for colonial-era crimes against humanity [M-D-018]? Does it create a legal precedent for other European states?
5. What legal strategies are being developed by the Pan-African Lawyers Union and the AU Legal Experts Reference Group for enforcement mechanisms, and how do they relate to the broader debate between political settlement and legal adjudication?
