CRITICAL MINERALS & RESOURCE NATIONALISM 2.0: Why the Policy is More Critical than the Mineral
Working Paper 92
Authors: Amir Lebdioui (University of Oxford) & Thea Riofrancos (Providence College)
Published: August 2025, Technology, Industrialisation and Development (TIDE) Centre, University of Oxford
Final version included as a chapter in the Handbook of Resource Nationalism, published by Edward Elgar Publishing.

ABSTRACT

"Critical minerals" are driving a new wave of resource nationalism around the globe. But to what extent is the current approach to critical minerals aligned with the achievement of long-term developmental goals? How novel are the policies currently implemented and to what extent do they replicate previous extractivist patterns, including environmental devastation? For whom are those minerals critical, and for how long?

This paper provides a comprehensive examination of the role of critical minerals in driving resource nationalism, drawing lessons from different illustrative cases to highlight the opportunities and challenges facing a renewed resource-based development agenda in the 21st century. It highlights some of the common policy tools witnessed around the globe before highlighting some of the risks, common misconceptions, and factors of success.

We argue that, although nations should strive to gain sovereign control over their natural resources, considerable risks exist in attempts to leverage mineral abundance for socio-economic development. Strong technological foresight capabilities, inclusive social governance, robust environmental regulations, and a careful coordination of critical minerals strategy within a broad, sustainable, and equitable industrial policy is critical for long-term success.

1. Introduction: Critical for whom?

Resource nationalism -- commonly understood as a state-led strategy to assert greater control over subsoil assets for developmental ends -- has long played a central role in the political economy of the Global South. Throughout the twentieth century, resource-rich countries frequently pursued policies such as nationalization, export controls, and state-owned enterprises (SOEs) to capture more value from commodity exports and to spur industrial development. Latin America was in the vanguard of these policy experiments, with forays into state-led extraction of oil and gas beginning in the 1920s (Berrios, Marak, and Morgenstern 2011; Rosales 2022). In some cases, such policies achieved important goals, ranging from protection from global price volatility, to incubation of technological expertise, to poverty reduction. However, resource nationalist efforts have not always been successful, and have at times exacerbated the risks of heightened commodity dependencies, exclusionary rents capture and distribution, and ecological harm.

Today, in the twenty-first century, a new wave of resource nationalism is emerging around "critical minerals," such as lithium, copper, nickel, and rare earths -- minerals deemed essential for the global shift to low-carbon technologies, consumer electronics, or military preparedness. Those minerals are offering a renewed opportunity for countries endowed with those resources to leverage them for prosperity. However, some scholars have also warned against replicating extractivist models that may not deliver on structural transformation (Hamouchene, 2023; Bringel and Fernandes, 2024; Riofrancos, 2025; Lebdioui, 2024). This juncture also raises important questions about the viability and sustainability of resource nationalism in an era increasingly defined by climate change, geopolitical competition, and technological disruptions.

Critical minerals differ from traditional hydrocarbons in several ways. Although both classes of resources carry the promise of contributing to immediate export revenues, unlike fossil fuels, critical mineral sectors are linked to the broader developmental goals of seizing the green window of opportunity in low carbon technologies (Lema et al. 2020). For instance, lithium, nickel, cobalt, and graphite are currently crucial to battery performance and longevity (International Energy Agency 2021). This shift therefore positions mineral producing countries at the centre of global supply chains for green technologies. Meanwhile, in a scenario that meets the Paris Agreement goals, the demand for the so-called critical minerals (particularly those that are essential inputs of low-carbon technologies) would rise significantly over the next two decades. In Global South countries, this has been cause for optimism among the political and economic elites that see a developmental pathway running through expanding mining to serve growing critical minerals markets. Even the governments of nations that are primarily fossil fuel producers are looking to diversify into extractive sectors like lithium and rare earths.

However, the stakes for critical mineral producing nations go beyond economic gains; they also encompass social and environmental dimensions. Policymakers and scholars alike are questioning whether resource nationalism can be reinvigorated under the banner of "greening" the global economy -- or whether the prevailing paradigm simply recycles past extractivist models, characterised by a resource drain and environmental damage in producing nations. Within this agenda, a key emerging issue in the critical minerals policy space is how to align extractives-led development, environmental protection, democratic participation, and Indigenous rights in producing regions.

What also differentiates critical minerals from other mined commodities is that they are not only considered essential for the national and/or global economy, but that also either have no viable substitutes or their supply may be at risk of disruption (due to geographic concentration, proximity to chokepoints, geological scarcity/accessibility, geopolitical issues, or other factors). Their identification is highly context-dependent across space (a mineral that is critical to a country's economy might not be considered critical in another region) and across time, since the importance of a mineral and the nature of its supply chain can change due to technological innovations/disruptions. "Criticality" is thus never an objective feature of certain minerals, but instead a dynamic and emergent quality that is the ongoing product of the interplay of endogenous and exogenous factors.

This is why we should shy away from unbridled optimism surrounding critical minerals. First, it is worth observing that the concept of "critical" or strategic minerals originated in the lead-up to World War II, as the US government sought to ensure access to materials for the war mobilization (Black 2018: 68-83). The concept, in definition and ordinary use, retains its bellicose origins and, by linking a given mineral to "national security," can have a chilling effect on needed public and scholarly debate. Second, mineral "criticality" is both context-dependent and temporally bound. Third, social and environmental governance issues are becoming increasingly salient. Communities in resource-rich regions often bear the brunt of mining's negative consequences -- such as land dispossession, water pollution, and threats to Indigenous rights -- raising questions about who really benefits from the extraction of these strategic minerals (Riofrancos et al. 2023). Lastly, the international political economy of critical minerals remains contested: reliance on foreign capital and markets can dilute the very control that resource nationalism aims to enhance, while global supply chains create pressures and constraints that individual states cannot easily circumvent.
