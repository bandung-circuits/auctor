# Academic Research — Relevant Excerpts

## [M-E-001] International Legal Foundations for Slavery Reparations
**Source**: SRC-E-001
**Relevance**: Establishes the international legal precedent for reparations claims arising from transatlantic slavery, including the Factory at Chorzow principle and ILC Articles on State Responsibility.

> Reparations have long been seen as the best way to atone for international wrongs. Factory at Chorzow made this international precedent. In that case the Permanent Court of International Justice ("PCIJ") stated, "reparation must, as far as possible wipe out all the consequences of the illegal act[.]" Additionally, the ILC's Articles on State Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts state in Article 31, "The responsible State is under an obligation to make full reparation for the injury caused by the internationally wrongful act. Injury includes any damage, whether material or moral, caused by the internationally wrongful act of a State." With regards to slavery and colonization, this is complemented by Article 14 which notes, "The breach of an international obligation by an act of a State having a continuing character extends over the entire period during which the act continues and remains not in conformity with the international obligation."

## [M-E-002] European Resistance to Formal Apology at Durban Conference
**Source**: SRC-E-001
**Relevance**: Documents the deliberate European strategy to avoid legal liability by refusing formal apology, choosing instead language of "regret" -- directly relevant to understanding the resistance Ghana's UN resolution will face.

> There was an outright refusal by certain countries from the European Union to apologize for slavery. This push was led by Britain and joined by Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands who believed that formally apologizing for slavery and colonization (as requested by African, Caribbean, Latin American and Asian countries) would create legal implications that would force their countries to pay reparations. In order to appease these countries, the European Union released a draft statement noting its "regret" about the Trans-Atlantic slave trade.

## [M-E-003] Reparations Beyond Financial Compensation: Debt Cancellation and Trade Restructuring
**Source**: SRC-E-001
**Relevance**: Academic argument that reparations should take forms beyond monetary payments, including colonial debt cancellation and restructuring of global trade regimes -- a framework relevant to understanding comprehensive reparatory justice demands.

> Financial reparations would be most beneficial to the recipient countries in the form of debt cancellation and repayment. Many former colonies were forced to pay colonial debts when they gained their independence. These payments served to further destabilize and undermine growth and development and have left these countries playing catch-up to this day. [...] Undoubtedly, the global trade regime has its roots in slavery and colonialism. Most of the countries that are considered the global north participated heavily in and benefitted from the Trans-Atlantic slave trade and colonialism, while those that are considered the global south are those that suffered from these atrocities.

## [M-E-004] Second Africa-CARICOM Summit and the Addis Ababa Declaration
**Source**: SRC-E-002
**Relevance**: Provides academic firsthand account of the 2025 Africa-CARICOM Summit that produced the Addis Ababa Declaration -- a key institutional milestone immediately preceding Ghana's UN resolution initiative.

> "Heads of State and Government reiterated their unwavering call for reparatory justice for Africans and People of African Descent. They underscored that the enduring legacies of the transatlantic trade in enslaved Africans and chattel enslavement constitute grave crimes against humanity, which necessitate restitution, compensation and development. To this end, the Summit agreed to adopt 'The Addis Ababa Declaration on Transcontinental Partnership in Pursuit of Reparatory Justice for Africans and People of African Descent Through Reparations'."

## [M-E-005] Scale of Transatlantic Slave Trade and Reverse Reparations to Slave Owners
**Source**: SRC-E-002
**Relevance**: Provides key quantitative context on the slave trade and the perverse historical precedent where the enslaved peoples' nations were forced to compensate their enslavers.

> During this time an estimated 12.5 million Africans were kidnapped and trafficked across the Atlantic -- a journey that some 10.7 million survived. The vast majority were taken to Brazil and the Caribbean, while less than five percent were sent directly to North America. Emancipation included no compensation for the enslaved, although payments were made to some owners. Indeed, Haiti was forced to pay reparations to France (~ $617 million in 2025) and the British government borrowed funds from private financiers (~ 2 billion euros in 2025) to compensate slave owners for the loss of literal human capital.

## [M-E-006] AU 2023 Resolution and the Reparatory Justice Framework
**Source**: SRC-E-003
**Relevance**: Details the African Union's 2023 resolution establishing an African Committee of Experts on Reparations and proposes using UN Basic Principles as a legal framework -- the institutional groundwork for Ghana's UN resolution.

> In February 2023 the African Union (AU) [...] adopted a resolution calling for, among other steps, the establishment of 'an African Committee of Experts on Reparations for the purpose of developing a common African position on reparations and incorporate therein, an African reparatory programme of action.' [...] In November 2023 the AU held the Accra Reparations Conference, which culminated in the adoption of a declaration which affirmed as follows: 'The fulfilment of reparations is a moral as well as a legal imperative rooted in principles of justice, human rights and human dignity, and that reparations represent a concrete step towards remedying historical wrongs and fostering healing amongst people of different nations and continents.'

## [M-E-007] Underdevelopment Theory as Foundation for Reparations Claims
**Source**: SRC-E-003
**Relevance**: Summarizes the academic "underdevelopment school of thought" (Rodney, Williams) that provides the theoretical basis for arguing slavery and colonialism created structural global inequality requiring reparatory justice.

> Rodney argues that the underdevelopment of Africa, compared to Europe, is a direct result of the exploitation of Africa by Europe. He argues that slavery and colonialism resulted in the underdevelopment of Africa while enriching the Global North, through the abduction of manpower and grand looting of natural resources from Africa by the slave traders and colonisers. The 'underdevelopment school of thought' expounded on the earlier work of Williams, whose seminal research in 1944 demonstrated that the enslavement of Africans was central to the rise and sustenance of capitalist economies and societies of the Global North.

## [M-E-008] Slavery as Violation of Customary International Law
**Source**: SRC-E-003
**Relevance**: Addresses the key legal debate about whether slavery was unlawful at the time of its commission, arguing that customary international law already prohibited such treatment of human beings.

> Although some have counter-argued that slavery and colonialism were not unlawful at the time of their commission, it has sufficiently been demonstrated, including by Wittmann, that the abduction of human beings and the sale of them to perform forced and unpaid labour (slavery) were contrary to customary international law, which already was in existence at the time. [...] Although some of the slave-trading states observed domestic legal rules that recognised slaves as a form of property, these rules applied only within those states and could not apply internationally. [...] Therefore, although slavery may have been legalised within the slave-trading states, it remained illegal in so far as those states were engaging in it in international territories where they were abducting human beings and selling them as slaves.

## [M-E-009] Proposed Framework of Reparatory Measures Using UN Basic Principles
**Source**: SRC-E-003
**Relevance**: Presents an academic proposal for a comprehensive reparatory framework based on UN Basic Principles, including compensation, institutional reform, and reform of the United Nations itself.

> I propose that the United Nations Basic Principles and Guidelines on the Right to a Remedy and Reparation for Victims of Gross Violations of International Human Rights Law and Serious Violations of International Humanitarian Law (UN Basic Principles) offer a more comprehensive framework for developing and determining the scope of reparatory measures that the AU should demand from perpetrator states and non-state actors. [...] According to the UN Basic Principles, the term 'reparation' encapsulates measures aimed at redressing violations of human rights by undertaking a range of material and symbolic measures in favour of the victims or their families as well as affected communities. [...] the scope of reparatory measures must include measures for restitution; compensation; rehabilitation; repatriation; satisfaction and cessation of continuing violations; truth seeking; search for the disappeared persons or their remains; reburial of remains; and public apologies.

## [M-E-010] Global Reparations as Reversal of Historical Power Dynamics
**Source**: SRC-E-004
**Relevance**: Provides a political economy analysis of how contemporary reparations claims represent an unprecedented reversal -- victims demanding justice from incumbent powers rather than traditional war reparations extracted by victors.

> The reparations claims that emerged in the wake of anti-colonial struggles in the Global South and civil rights movements in the United States, and their association with struggles for social justice by oppressed and exploited peoples, have been different from these earlier war-related ones. They have fundamentally reversed the direction of claims for restorative justice, from victors demanding compensation from the vanquished to victims demanding reparatory justice from victors, even whilst those victors (or the descendants of slave owners and colonialists) are still at the centre of global power.

## [M-E-011] Resistance to Reparations: Historical Examples of Imperial Backlash
**Source**: SRC-E-004
**Relevance**: Documents cases where reparations movements were met with violent resistance from Western powers, providing historical context for understanding European resistance to Ghana's UN resolution.

> Prominent examples include the US, French and Canadian coordination of a coup d'etat in Haiti in 2004 that ousted democratically elected President Jean-Bertrand Aristide after his government campaigned for US$ 21 billion in reparations from France, among other progressive policy positions. Another example is Italy's repudiation in 2011 of its treaty agreed with Libya in 2008 to pay reparations, following its support for the destructive NATO intervention in Libya in that year which resulted in the fall and assassination of Gaddafi.

## [M-E-012] Tensions Between Redistributive and Transformative Reparations
**Source**: SRC-E-004
**Relevance**: Identifies a core academic debate about whether reparations should seek gains within existing capitalist structures or aim for structural transformation -- a question directly relevant to the scope of Ghana's resolution.

> It highlights the contentious, unresolved questions about how reparations movements should interact with the highly unequal structures of global capitalism. To what degree should they seek large redistributive gains within these structures, or else aim for more revolutionary standpoints which reject these structures? If the former, would this compromise any hope for reparations to be truly transformative and able to address the challenges presented by global white supremacy?

## [M-E-013] History of the International Reparations Movement
**Source**: SRC-E-005
**Relevance**: Traces the trajectory of the international reparations movement from the OAU Group of Eminent Persons through Durban 2001 to the CARICOM Reparations Commission, providing academic historical context.

> At approximately the same time as the Basic Principles were first being drafted in the 1990s, the then Organization of African Unity (now African Union) established a Group of Eminent Persons whose mandate was to seek reparations to Africa. [...] In September 2001, the United Nations held a World Conference against Racism, Racial Discrimination, Xenophobia, and Related Intolerance in Durban, South Africa, at which there was much discussion about reparations for the slave trade. While the official, government delegations did not release a call for reparations, the parallel NGO conference did so. Unfortunately, the discussions at Durban were overshadowed by the 9/11 attacks on the USA.

## [M-E-014] Proposed Reparations: Truth Commission, Apology, and Symbolic Measures
**Source**: SRC-E-005
**Relevance**: Presents a scholarly proposal for forms of reparations that Western states might offer, including truth commissions and memorialization -- less contentious forms that may find broader support.

> I propose that the following ought to be reparations offered by all Western powers, including the USA, that engaged in the slave trade. Reparations should include participation in and financing of a truth commission on the slave trade; acknowledgement of and apology for the harms caused by the trade; and symbolic financial and other assistance in establishing monuments to the slave trade, museums exhibits, and educational programs, both in Africa and the USA.

## [M-E-015] Africa as Net Creditor: Contemporary Structural Injustice
**Source**: SRC-E-006
**Relevance**: Provides quantitative evidence that Africa continues to lose wealth through structural exploitation, arguing that reparations must address present systems not just historical crimes.

> In this system, Africa is a net creditor to the world, losing more than $500 billion every year through illicit financial flows, unfair trade practices, exploitative investment frameworks, and debt servicing, while it is home to some of the world's poorest populations. [...] Ghana exported $9.58 billion in gold in 2024, yet it only retained 14% of the value due to the nature of multinational agreements. The DRC produces over 70% of the world's cobalt, yet only 1% is refined in the country before being exported.

## [M-E-016] Reparations as Systemic Transformation Beyond Financial Transfers
**Source**: SRC-E-006
**Relevance**: Articulates the UN Special Adviser's position that reparations must be understood as systemic transformation of global economic architecture, not merely financial compensation.

> The 21st century demands a bold redefinition of what reparations truly mean. Reparations must not be seen merely as financial compensation for past events. They must be understood as a call to transform the very rules of the game -- the international trade, finance, and governance systems that have perpetuated injustice for centuries. Otherwise, a profound contradiction arises: reparations would be paid with Africa's own stolen wealth.

## [M-E-017] Legal Challenges of Intergenerational Reparations Claims
**Source**: SRC-E-007
**Relevance**: Identifies the core legal obstacle to slavery reparations -- the difficulty of establishing a causal nexus between historical wrongs and contemporary suffering across generations.

> The intergenerational nature of claims relating to historical injustices erodes the basis of reparations claims. Identifying a causal nexus between the continuing suffering of contemporary descendants and the original wrongdoing becomes increasingly difficult as time passes. In the case of racialised transatlantic enslavement, intervening factors also contribute to the harms experienced by descendants -- apartheid, colonialism, black codes, segregation, international debts and other discriminatory practices all contribute to current suffering in a way that at least obscures, and at most completely overrides, the historically based claims.

## [M-E-018] CARICOM Ten-Point Plan as Collective Reparations Model
**Source**: SRC-E-007
**Relevance**: Analyzes the CARICOM Ten-Point Plan as a collective rather than individual reparations model, a framework Ghana's UN resolution builds upon.

> In 2013 the Caribbean Heads of State created the CARICOM Reparations Commission to advance the case for reparations for the transatlantic slave trade. In 2014 the Commission launch a Ten Point Plan for reparations that seeks 'to provide resources to victimized groups with the aim of creating the material basis and security necessary for them to become full participants in social, political, and economic life.' The demands focus on: (1) full formal apology; (2) repatriation; (3) indigenous peoples development program; (4) cultural institutions; (5) public health crisis; (6) illiteracy eradication; (7) African knowledge program; (8) psychological rehabilitation; (9) technology transfer; and (10) debt cancellation.

## [M-E-019] Political vs Legal Pathways for Reparations
**Source**: SRC-E-007
**Relevance**: Academic argument that political settlement rather than legal adjudication is more likely to succeed for slavery reparations -- relevant to understanding why a UN resolution is the chosen pathway.

> While we argue that the possibility for reparations for the transatlantic slave trade on a legal basis is unlikely, we do believe that in setting aside the legal lens on dealing with historic atrocities, there can be a larger space for states involved in the transatlantic slave trade on a moral and political basis to recognise the harm caused and make symbolic reparations. [...] A political settlement drawing from the experience of transitional justice is therefore likely to be more appropriate in providing redress.

## [M-E-021] Reparations as Simultaneously Past Debt and Future Development
**Source**: SRC-E-008

> The CARICOM Reparations Commission (CRC) insists on the twofold predicament of the claim: it is simultaneously an issue of past debt and an issue of future development. ## [M-E-022] Growing European Willingness to Acknowledge Historical Injustices
**Source**: SRC-E-008
**Relevance**: Documents the gradual shift in European attitudes, from Belgian royal regret to Dutch apology considerations, suggesting Ghana's resolution enters a slowly shifting political landscape.

> European governments' willingness to embrace responsibility for historical injustices (Barkan 2001) is steadily growing and has recently extended to the crown of a former colonizing European nation-state. In late June of 2020, the Belgian King Philippe issued a statement expressing his regret for the country's harrowingly violent colonization of the Congo under the leadership of King Leopold II. [...] Although an international framework for reparation claims remains absent, these national and local initiatives indicate a larger global turn to reparatory justice in the early decades of the twenty-first century.

## [M-E-023] Continuing Harm: Evidence of Intergenerational Impact of Slavery
**Source**: SRC-E-003
**Relevance**: Rebuts the counter-argument that current generations cannot demonstrate harm from slavery, providing specific evidence of continuing damage.

> There is ample evidence to demonstrate that current generations of Africans have been disadvantaged by slavery and colonialism and that they continue to suffer the harm caused by these heinous criminal acts. For example, several post-independence African governments inherited huge financial debts accrued by colonial governments and they continue to struggle with a debt crisis that is partly as a result of inheriting these debts; colonialism resulted in the damage of the natural environment in Africa and this has made Africans more prone to the contemporary devasting effects of climate change crisis; slave trade removed African families from their homelands and resettled them in regions of the world (including the Caribbean islands) that are more prone to the devastating effects of the ongoing climate change crisis.

## [M-E-024] Slavery's Contribution to British Economic Development: Insurance Sector
**Source**: SRC-E-003
**Relevance**: Presents specific academic research demonstrating how slave trade profits built British financial infrastructure that persists today -- evidence supporting the reparations case.

> A recent study published by the John Hopkins University also vindicates the underdevelopment school of thought, as originally argued by Williams and perfected by Rodney. The John Hopkins study demonstrates that slave trade contributed to the booming of the insurance sector in Britain. It shows that several insurance companies, including Lloyd's of London, made huge profits from extending insurance cover to voyages transporting slaves and insuring slaves as cargo. The booming of the insurance services brought about the accumulation of capital in the British financial services sector, which was used to finance other development initiatives, including the building of infrastructure.

## [M-E-025] Reparations Movements Met with Strong Institutional Resistance
**Source**: SRC-E-004
**Relevance**: Contextualizes the political dynamics Ghana's resolution will face, noting that reparations movements have gained enough traction that former colonial powers feel compelled to respond rather than ignore demands.

> Despite such imperial and internal resistance, reparations have remained on the agenda of multilateral institutions like CARICOM and the African Union. Calls for reparations are also increasingly made by the United Nations. These calls have achieved enough prominence that world leaders feel the need to respond to demands they simply could have ignored only a decade or two ago. For instance, John Kerry, the then US Climate Envoy, insisted just before attending COP28 in 2023 that the US would not 'under any circumstances' pay reparations to developing countries hit by climate change-fuelled disasters.
