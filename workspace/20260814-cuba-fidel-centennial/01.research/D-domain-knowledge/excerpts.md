## [M-D-001] Helms–Burton Act: Core Legal Mechanism of the US Embargo
**Source**: SRC-D-001
**Relevance**: The Helms-Burton Act is the foundational legal instrument of the US embargo against Cuba; its provisions define the extraterritorial reach that the international community (including the EU) has condemned for three decades.

> The Cuban Liberty and Democratic Solidarity (Libertad) Act of 1996 (Helms–Burton Act), Pub. L. 104–114 (text) (PDF), 110 Stat. 785, 22 U.S.C. §§ 6021–6091) is a United States federal law which strengthens and continues the United States embargo against Cuba. It extended the territorial application of the initial embargo to apply to foreign companies trading with Cuba, and penalized foreign companies allegedly "trafficking" in property formerly owned by U.S. citizens but confiscated by Cuba after the Cuban Revolution. It also covers property formerly owned by Cubans who have since become U.S. citizens.

## [M-D-002] Helms–Burton Act: Codification of Embargo and Congressional Veto Over Presidential Authority
**Source**: SRC-D-001
**Relevance**: The Act codified the embargo into law, removing the President's unilateral ability to lift it — a structural feature that explains why the embargo persists across administrations regardless of policy preferences.

> Title I strengthened sanctions against the current Cuban Government. Among many other provisions, it codified the U.S. embargo on trade and financial transactions which had been in effect pursuant to a Presidential proclamation since the Kennedy Administration.

> Provides power to the Legislative Branch to override an Executive Branch cancellation of the embargo, although such a legislative veto had been ruled unconstitutional by the Supreme Court 13 years earlier. Unlike in that case though, which concerned a review only by the House of Representatives, the Helms–Burton Act (sec. 204(e)) subjects the President's decision to a review by joint resolution of both chambers of Congress.

## [M-D-003] Helms–Burton Act: Title III Private Cause of Action and Presidential Suspension Authority
**Source**: SRC-D-001
**Relevance**: Title III allows US nationals to sue foreign companies "trafficking" in confiscated property — the most extraterritorial and internationally contested provision, suspended by successive presidents until Trump let it lapse in 2019.

> Title III creates a private cause of action and authorizes U.S. nationals with claims to confiscated property in Cuba to file suit in U.S. courts against persons that may be "trafficking" in that property. […] The Act grants the President the authority to suspend the lawsuit provisions for periods of up to 6 months if it is necessary to the national interest of the United States and will expedite a transition to democracy in Cuba. Successive presidents have exercised this authority, most recently in June 2018, pursuant to a non-binding declaration of intention in April 1997 that came out of a trade dispute with the European Union. In 2019 President Trump allowed the suspension to expire, and Carnival Cruise was promptly sued under the act.

**Source**: SRC-D-001

## [M-D-005] Helms–Burton Act: International Reactions and Counter-Legislation by EU, Canada, Mexico, UK
**Source**: SRC-D-001
**Relevance**: The extraterritorial provisions triggered formal counter-legislation from major US allies, making the embargo not just a bilateral issue but a conflict within international law.

> The Helms–Burton Act was condemned by the Council of Europe, the European Union, Britain, Canada, Mexico, Brazil, Argentina and other U.S. allies that enjoy normal trade relations with Cuba. The governments argued that the law ran counter to the spirit of international law and sovereignty.

> The European Union introduced a Council Regulation (No 2271/96) (law binding all member states) declaring the extraterritorial provisions of the Helms–Burton Act to be unenforceable within the EU, and permitting recovery of any damages imposed under it. The EU law also applied sanctions against US companies and their executives for making Title III complaints.

> Mexico passed the Law of Protection of Commerce and Investments from Foreign Policies that Contravene International Law in October 1996, aimed at neutralizing the Helms–Burton Act.

> Similarly, Canada passed the "An Act to amend the Foreign Extraterritorial Measures Act" (C-54), a law to counteract the effect of Helms–Burton.

## [M-D-007] Cuba's 2019 Constitution: Recognition of Private Property and Foreign Investment
**Source**: SRC-D-002
**Relevance**: The constitution legally recognizes private property and foreign investment as fundamental to development — a structural shift in Cuba's socialist economic model that frames the current economic crisis.

> The document does the following:
> - Recognizes private property and promotes foreign investment as fundamental to the development of the economy.
> - Limits the term of the president—who is selected by the National Assembly, as in parliamentary systems—to two consecutive five-year terms, and requires that the president be under sixty when s/he is elected.
> - Restores the pre-1976 position of Prime Minister, an official selected by the president who leads government ministries on a day-to-day basis.
> - Forbids discrimination based on sexual orientation.
> - Guarantees women's sexual and reproductive rights and protects women from gender violence.
> - Establishes the presumption of innocence in criminal proceedings and the right to habeas corpus.
> - Strengthens the authority of local governments.
> - Allows holding dual citizenship.

## [M-D-008] Cuba's Constitutional Process: Citizen Consultation and Referendum Mechanics
**Source**: SRC-D-002
**Relevance**: The constitutional drafting process — involving 8 million participants in 112,000 debates — illustrates the participatory mechanism through which Cuba legitimizes political change, relevant to understanding how the centennial is being framed.

> The first draft of the constitution was approved by the National Assembly in July 2018. For a subsequent three-month period, Cubans were invited to suggest changes to the proposed draft. According to official numbers, more than 8 million people participated in nearly 112,000 debates in workplaces, schools, and community centers, and suggested a large number of proposed modifications to the constitution draft.

> According to the Cuban electoral commission, voter turnout reached 84 percent (slightly higher than in Cuba's last election cycle in April 2018), with 87 percent of the votes in favor. The size of the vote suggests that, whatever misgivings or frustrations Cubans had with the new Constitutional proposal, they saw it as a step in the right direction.

## [M-D-019] Fidel's Ideological Synthesis: Martí, Marxism, and Economic Sovereignty
**Source**: SRC-D-006
**Relevance**: teleSUR's centenary profile documents the ideological foundation of the Cuban revolutionary state — the integration of José Martí's anti-imperialist nationalism with Marxist-Leninist economic planning — the doctrinal framework being commemorated at the centennial.

> The political philosophy of Fidel Castro emerged from a synthesis of nineteenth-century Cuban anti-colonial thought and twentieth-century Marxist-Leninist historical materialism. At the core of this ideological framework was José Martí, whose emphasis on national sovereignty, anti-imperialism, and racial equality formed the intellectual foundation of the Cuban Revolution.

> Fidel Castro integrated Martí's nationalist principles with Marxist socio-economic analysis, arguing that true national independence required dismantling colonial land structures, nationalizing strategic industries, and replacing market-driven wealth allocation with state-directed economic planning.

> Upon taking power in 1959, the revolutionary government prioritized immediate socio-economic reform through legislative action. In 1959, land reform limited holdings to 402 hectares and nationalized foreign-owned estates. Subsequent laws nationalized major utility companies, foreign banking institutions, industrial plants, and transportation networks.

## [M-D-020] The US Embargo's Origin and the Special Period Economic Mechanism
**Source**: SRC-D-006
**Relevance**: This passage documents the legal origin of the embargo (1960 nationalization → US blockade → 1962 full trade blockade) and Cuba's adaptive "Special Period" response — the economic mechanism now echoed in the current crisis.

> Following the nationalization of U.S. property in 1960, the United States administration imposed an economic blockade that grew into a full trade blockade in 1962. The loss of trade with the U.S. forced Cuba to realign its economy with the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (CMEA) led by the Soviet Union.

> When the Soviet bloc collapsed between 1989 and 1991, Cuba lost 80% of its international trade and experienced a sharp drop in Gross Domestic Product, initiating an economic crisis known as the "Special Period in Time of Peace." To navigate this period without dismantling the basic social welfare system, Fidel Castro implemented targeted changes, permitting foreign investment in tourism, legalizing limited non-state enterprise, and diversifying international trade partners.

## [M-D-021] Cuba's Internationalist Mechanisms: ELAM, Henry Reeve Contingent, NAM Leadership
**Source**: SRC-D-006

> Through the creation of the Latin American School of Medicine-ELAM (1999), Fidel allowed thousands of young vulnerable people of other countries to study without a tariff in Cuba.

> In diplomatic arenas, Fidel Castro positioned Cuba as a key bridge within the Non-Aligned Movement (NAM), serving as its General Secretary twice (1979–1983 and 2006–2009). He advocated for restructuring the financial system, writing and speaking extensively on the unpayable nature of Third World foreign debt and the long-term ecological risks of industrial overconsumption.

> According to records compiled by Fabián Escalante, the former head of the Cuban Department of State Security, intelligence agencies identified 638 distinct assassination plots targeting the Cuban leader between 1959 and his retirement from public office.
