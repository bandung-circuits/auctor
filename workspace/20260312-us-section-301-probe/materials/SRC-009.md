Abstract

Since 2016, the resurgence of protectionist trade measures culminating in the Trump administration's escalations and their intensification in 2025 has unsettled the foundations of global trade governance. This paper examines how unilateral tariffs and non-tariff barriers reshape legal norms and economic welfare through an interdisciplinary approach that combines historical inquiry, doctrinal analysis, and macroeconomic evaluation. The findings reveal persistent patterns of retaliatory escalation, systemic inefficiency, and normative ambiguity. Tariffs generate visible fiscal revenue but impose diffuse costs on consumers and supply chains, while regulatory barriers often erode welfare without delivering compensatory gains. Legally, the analysis interrogates the expansive use of national security exceptions in trade law, contrasting contemporary practices with earlier interpretations that emphasised good faith and proportionality. These developments have strained the credibility of the multilateral trading system. The paper argues for reforms centred on climate-aligned trade instruments, disciplined subsidy governance, and clarified security exception criteria. Its core contribution lies in integrating economic evidence with legal analysis to demonstrate how the choice of instruments shapes distributive outcomes and determines the viability of multilateralism in an increasingly fragmented order.
