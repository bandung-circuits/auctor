# The Mine That Was Never Brazilian: Serra Verde, Sovereignty, and the DFC Pattern of Resource Capture

## "A Disgrace" -- The Sale That Exposed 14 Years of Surrender

President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva called it "uma vergonha" -- a disgrace. The sale of Serra Verde, Brazil's only operating rare earth mine, to the US-government-backed USA Rare Earth for $2.8 billion had become, in the president's framing, an affront to national sovereignty. The governor of Goias had "made a deal with American companies, granting concessions of things he cannot grant, because they belong to the Union," Lula declared. "If we are not careful, these people will sell Brazil."[^1]

What Lula did not say is that Serra Verde was never Brazilian. The company was constituted in 2010 with entirely foreign capital: Denham Capital of Boston, the Energy & Minerals Group of Houston, and Vision Blue Resources of the United Kingdom, founded by Mick Davis, the former Xstrata chief who built his empire through acquisitions before merging with Glencore and later joining the UK Conservative Party to rebuild its campaign capability against the left.[^2] No Brazilian founder. No national fund. The transaction was a transfer of control between foreign groups, not the sale of a Brazilian enterprise to foreigners.[^3]

For fourteen years, Serra Verde exported practically all its raw ore to Shenghe Resources, a partially state-owned Chinese company based in Chengdu. During the governments of Lula, Dilma Rousseff, Temer, and Bolsonaro, no Brazilian authority raised any objection to the delivery of low-value-added raw material to a Chinese processor.[^4] In December 2025, after receiving $565 million in DFC financing, Serra Verde renegotiated and slashed its decade-long Chinese offtake agreements by nearly eight years, with shipments to Shenghe ending by close of 2026.[^5]

Brazil had three chances to act and failed at every one. The federal government knew of the DFC loan at each key decision point. Itamaraty confirmed awareness that the deal was a federal-level negotiation while publicly treating it as a state matter.[^6] This inaction crossed six consecutive administrations: Lula 2, Dilma 1 and 2, Temer, Bolsonaro, Lula 3. And in 2025, under Lula's own government, BNDES approved loans for the foreign-controlled enterprise.[^7]

The PT protested only when the output stopped going to China.

## The Deal's Architecture -- State Capital and Strategic Minerals

The terms of the acquisition are precise. USA Rare Earth acquires 100 per cent of Serra Verde Group for $2.8 billion: $300 million in cash and 126.8 million newly issued shares.[^8] Behind this corporate transaction sits a dense architecture of state capital. The DFC committed $565 million to Serra Verde, with a fifteen-year, 100 per cent exclusive offtake agreement to a special purpose vehicle capitalized by various US government agencies and private capital sources, including guaranteed minimum floor prices for each of the four magnetic rare earths -- neodymium, praseodymium, dysprosium, and terbium.[^9] The Trump administration invested $1.6 billion in USAR through the Commerce Department, acquiring an equity stake exceeding 15 per cent: $1.3 billion in loans plus $277 million in federal funding.[^10]

Pela Ema's strategic significance is real. The mine hosts an ionic clay deposit geologically similar to China's Jiangxi Province, with a 458 million metric ton inferred resource and 24 per cent heavy rare earth content.[^11] Over 50 per cent of the REE bulk is sorbed on clay and recoverable by simple elution.[^12] At Phase 1 nameplate capacity, Serra Verde is projected to produce 6,400 metric tons of total rare earth oxides per year by the end of 2027, representing over 50 per cent of non-China heavy rare earth supply.[^13] It is the only active ionic clay rare earth mine in Brazil, producing all four magnetic rare earth elements outside Asia.[^14]

Yet the scale gap exposes the structural problem. Brazil holds 21 million tonnes of rare earth reserves -- 23 per cent of the global total -- but produces less than 1 per cent of global output.[^15] The following sections explain why.

## From Financier to Owner -- The DFC Pattern Across the Global South

Serra Verde is one node in a global network. The DFC's critical minerals portfolio stretches across the periphery: $565 million for Brazil, a Gecamines-Mercuria copper joint venture in the DRC shipping 100,000 tonnes to the United States, $700 million for tungsten development in Kazakhstan, $75 million in the United States-Ukraine Reconstruction Investment Fund, $600 million in the Orion Critical Mineral Consortium partnered with ADQ and the UAE, project development support for Pensana rare earth refining in Angola, and Mkango Resources' Songwe Hills mine in Malawi.[^16]

The Tricontinental Institute identified the pattern precisely: "entra como financiador, garante o fluxo da producao e compra quando o ativo esta maduro" -- enter as financier, guarantee production flow, buy when the asset is mature.[^17] The DFC's own mission statement confirms the strategic intent: "We invest in mining, processing, and transportation to break adversary control over strategic supply chains and protect the U.S. and our allies from economic coercion."[^18]

Since January 2025, the Trump administration has invested more than $1 billion to acquire stakes in critical mineral companies globally, with explicit conditions in financing agreements specifying that minerals must be sold to "friendly" jurisdictions.[^19] The DFC Chief of Staff participated in the Critical Minerals Ministerial alongside officials from the Department of Commerce, the Department of Energy, the Export-Import Bank, and the Department of War's Office of Strategic Capital -- revealing the military dimension of what is presented as development finance.[^20]

Even the Cato Institute, a libertarian think tank, concedes the character of this arrangement. Direct state equity stakes "blur the lines between industrial policy, political power, and private profit -- mirroring an approach similar to China's" and represent "state corporatism."[^21] The institute notes that Vulcan Elements was valued at $200 million before government blessing and nearly $2 billion afterwards, demonstrating that Washington's favour has become a business asset in itself.[^22]

The collision between the DFC's mission and Brazil's declared position is the subject of the next section. Brazil's Industry Ministry insists the country will "be friends with all countries from every bloc."[^23] The DFC exists to break adversary control. These two positions cannot coexist under the terms that have been signed.

## The 15-Year Lock-In -- Contractual Architecture That Negates Non-Alignment

Brazil's declared policy of non-alignment on critical minerals rests on two public statements. Industry Ministry senior director Leonardo Durans declared that Brazil's historical policy is to "be friends with all countries from every bloc," adding that the country will not take a side with the United States or China.[^24] A Brazilian government representative told BNamericas that Brazil is "not interested in entering into any kind of exclusivity agreement" with any country to develop its critical minerals, seeking instead "the best market solutions... with the largest possible number of participants."[^25]

Against these declarations, the contractual architecture of the Serra Verde transaction reveals a cascade of contradictions. The fifteen-year, 100 per cent exclusive offtake commits all Phase 1 production to a special purpose vehicle capitalized by US government agencies.[^26] The exclusivity clause prohibits sale to any company outside the SPV -- including Brazilian enterprises.[^27] Guaranteed minimum floor prices for each magnetic rare earth secure the US-aligned SPV against market fluctuations.[^28] After meeting Trump at the White House, Lula declared that Brazil has "no veto" on US participation and "no intention to offer preferential access," yet the fifteen-year exclusive offtake creates precisely that: preferential, exclusive access for US-aligned entities.[^29]

The analytical pivot is this: deposit rarity, which should confer leverage, actually reduced Brazilian bargaining power. The fifteen-year exclusive offtake prevents China from outbidding the sale.[^30] Brazil lacks domestic processing capability that would create alternative options. Non-alignment is not a diplomatic posture. It requires concrete institutional and legal infrastructure -- and Brazil never built it.

## The Regulatory Vacuum -- Constitutional Principle, Legislative Absence

Article 176 of the Brazilian Constitution establishes that mineral resources belong to the Union and that exploitation must serve the national interest.[^31] The principle is clear. The implementation is absent. Brazil lacks any equivalent of the US CFIUS, Australia's FIRB, or Canada's Investment Canada Act. Constitutional provisions are invoked ad hoc through the Supreme Court rather than enforced through dedicated statutory mechanisms.[^32]

The Serra Verde transaction proceeded without any national-interest review. The current concession framework, dating to 1967, allows foreign majority stakes without specific federal approval.[^33] The Supreme Court's 9-0 ruling on ADPF 342, three days after the deal announcement, upheld 1971 farmland restrictions, with Justice Alexandre de Moraes explicitly citing "critical minerals" -- naming lithium, rare earths, and nickel by category. None of the justices mentioned Serra Verde by name. They did not need to.[^34]

Rede Sustentabilidade's ADPF follows a documented strategic pattern of filling legislative gaps through constitutional litigation: ADPFs 749, 743, 651, and 572 all sought to force judicial recognition of gaps where the legislature had failed.[^35] Professor Diego Werneck Arguelhes explains the institutional reality: the STF has no deadline, can keep a case for five to fifteen years, then "resurrect" it.[^36] Judicial intervention is slow and uncertain, making legislative action the real prerequisite.

The new critical minerals bill (PL 2.780/2024) only partially fills the vacuum. The Chamber of Deputies approved it on May 6, 2026, but the original "prior approval" (anuencia previa) requirement was replaced with a weaker screening/homologation mechanism after mining industry pressure.[^37] The Critical Minerals Association chair pushed to convert the CMCE's veto into a mere notification regime.[^38] Key implementation details -- screening criteria, appeal processes, the minerals list -- remain undefined, to be set by future regulation.[^39] Legal expert William Freire assessed: "I do not think anything effective will happen in 2026."[^40]

The CELIS Institute frames the dilemma for Global South nations: adopting security-based screening reduces strategic flexibility and risks signalling geopolitical alignment. But the current vacuum does not preserve non-alignment. It simply allows the most powerful foreign actors to capture assets without oversight.[^41] The absence of screening does not preserve "strategic flexibility" if the result is a fifteen-year exclusive lock-in agreement.

## Geology Is Not Destiny -- Why Rare Deposits Do Not Confer Leverage

Pela Ema's geological facts are striking. The ionic clay deposit hosts a 1.8 billion-year-old tin granite with multi-stage rare earth enrichment, 24 per cent heavy rare earth content, over 50 per cent recoverable by simple elution, and 458 million tonnes of inferred resource.[^42] Before Serra Verde became operational, industrial-scale extraction from ionic clay deposits was almost exclusive to China and Myanmar.[^43]

The pricing data overturns the intuitive assumption. Concentrate is worth approximately $5 per kilogram. Separated oxide commands $50 or more per kilogram -- ten times the value.[^44] Heavy rare earth prices show a five-to-six-fold disparity between China and international markets, driven by processing concentration, not deposit rarity.[^45] The "strategic scarcity premium" that made Pela Ema worth $2.8 billion to USAR reflects the deposit profile, not current revenue -- and it accrues to the acquirer, not the host country.[^46]

Brazil's processing capability reveals the structural dependency. The MagBras initiative brings together 38 partners with a R$73 million budget, targeting 2030 for industrial-scale production. On a pilot scale, the technological cycle for NdFeB magnets has been mastered, but industrial mastery stands at 60 to 70 per cent, and all key equipment is imported from China.[^47] The CETEM research centre has separated praseodymium and neodymium using synthetic solutions -- not real ionic clay concentrate -- because Brazil does not yet produce that concentrate domestically. A minimum of 20 kilograms of real concentrate is required for testing, and few Brazilian companies can provide it.[^48] The magnet grade gap is stark: 42 MGOe at laboratory scale versus 55 MGOe required for commercial applications such as wind turbines.[^49]

Jack Lifton's assessment is definitive: ionic adsorption clay deposits outside China will not change the permanent magnet equation, because processing access matters more than deposit access. China is the only nation self-sufficient in the total supply chain necessary for the complete range of rare earth permanent magnets.[^50]

The paradox deepens. Blocking the Serra Verde sale does not preserve Brazilian sovereignty in isolation. It maintains Chinese processing dominance by removing the most credible near-term alternative.[^51] IBRAM states the structural reality plainly: "We know well where our rare earths are, but in no case do we have a mine where rare earths are the main product."[^52] The reserves-output paradox -- 23 per cent of global reserves, less than 1 per cent of global output -- is not an accident. It is a structural consequence of the processing bottleneck.

Indonesia's nickel model offers a counter-example. Indonesia imposed a blanket export ban, forced Chinese firms to relocate processing facilities domestically, and transformed itself from a raw material supplier into the world's largest nickel products exporter, with 55 processing facilities and second-place global stainless steel production.[^53] Brazil has not deployed comparable policy instruments. Indonesia targeted a sector where processing is less complex than rare earth separation, and imposed its ban from a position of continental scale and diversified exports.[^54]

## The Erased -- Quilombola Communities and the Costs No One Counts

Families in the Serra Verde area report that since mining began, two streams have become muddy, with a greasy substance turning clear water a reddish colour.[^55] The mine sits on terrain previously operated by SAMA, Brazil's last asbestos miner, which worked the site for decades before switching to rare earth extraction. The cumulative environmental legacy compounds the impact: asbestos contamination layered with rare earth processing on already degraded land.[^56]

Across Brazil, 2,900 active rare earth exploration requests have been filed. Eighty-two per cent of them were submitted after 2022, following the surge in global demand and geopolitical competition.[^57] The National Mining Agency (ANM) has been systematically granting research authorizations without community consultation, violating ILO Convention 169, which Brazil has ratified.[^58] The Kalunga _quilombola_ community in Goias -- one of Brazil's largest _quilombola_ (Afro-Brazilian descendant) territories -- has mining claims overlapping its land. ANM granted research authorization without notifying the _quilombola_ association, writing that if the community did not expressly withdraw within ten days, the process would proceed regardless.[^59] Article 68 of the Federal Constitution guarantees _quilombola_ territorial rights. The Kalunga's own internal regulations require collective consent for mining activities.[^60]

The DFC Project Summary claims that the project "will not adversely impact Indigenous Peoples or Cultural Heritage."[^61] This claim rests on the fact that the mine operates on existing mining land rather than pristine territory. It omits the proximity of _quilombola_ communities and the cumulative impact of decades of extraction in the Minacu area.

The environmental math exposes what both geopolitical and legal framings have omitted. Producing one ton of rare earths generates 13 kilograms of hazardous dust, 9,600 to 12,000 cubic metres of hazardous gas, 75 cubic metres of wastewater, and approximately one ton of radioactive residue.[^62] Serra Verde's claim of "sustainable" mining is relative: ionic clay extraction uses shallow holes without explosives and grid electricity with renewable sources. But the leaching process still requires chemical reagents, and the solvent extraction stage involves hazardous chemicals. Lower impact than hard-rock mining is not the same as no impact.[^63]

Vandeli Paulo dos Santos, national coordinator of CONAQ (Coordenação Nacional de Articulação das Comunidades Negras Rurais Quilombolas), frames the structural reality:

> "The argument that mining comes to develop the country or produce clean energy serves an isolated group. Brazil to this day is a country exploited to enrich other countries, especially from Europe, the US, and China. The black, brown, poor, Indigenous, _quilombola_ population and other traditional communities do not benefit from this wealth at all."[^64]

Luiz Paulo Siqueira, of the Movimento pela Soberania Popular da Mineracao (MAM), warns that the mining lobby is appropriating the sovereignty debate to facilitate further extraction, pushing legislative packages that guarantee fiscal exemptions for critical mineral companies and loosen environmental licensing.[^65]

The dispute is not about which foreign power controls the mine. It is about who bears the costs and who builds the alternative. Brazil's rare earth sovereignty requires not just blocking this sale but building domestic processing, enforcing community consultation, and breaking the colonial pattern of exporting raw materials while importing finished goods. A transformation that demands sustained popular organisation, not judicial injunctions alone.
