# Event Overview -- Relevant Excerpts

## [M-A-001] The Deal Announcement: USA Rare Earth Acquires Serra Verde for $2.8 Billion
**Source**: SRC-A-002
**Relevance**: Core factual record of the acquisition announcement from Reuters wire service -- the most authoritative account of the deal terms.

> April 20 (Reuters) - USA Rare Earth (USAR.O) said on Monday it will buy Brazilian rare earths miner Serra Verde in a cash-and-stock deal worth $2.8 billion, the latest in a string of acquisitions boosting the company's mining, processing and magnet-making portfolio.
>
> USA Rare Earth will pay $300 million in cash and 126.9 million in its own newly issued shares for Serra Verde, with the deal expected to close in the third quarter of 2026, a statement said.
>
> The company agreed to a $1.6 billion debt-and-equity funding package with the U.S. government in January, while privately held Serra Verde agreed a financing deal worth $565 million with Washington in February.
>
> Serra Verde's mine launched commercial production in early 2024, and has not yet hit full output, which is expected to be about 6,400 metric tons of total rare earth oxides a year by 2027.
>
> With the takeover, Serra Verde CEO Thras Moraitis will become president of USA Rare Earth and join its board, while chair Mick Davis, the former head of Xstrata, will also join the board.
>
> Serra Verde's current owners -- which include private equity groups Denham Capital, Energy and Minerals Group and Vision Blue -- will hold 34% of the combined company.

The party said that no transaction should be carried out that could directly or indirectly transfer significant economic control over the country's strategic mineral assets.

## [M-A-004] Constitutional Basis and Brazil's Regulatory Gap
**Source**: SRC-A-004
**Relevance**: Explains the constitutional legal framework and Brazil's lack of a dedicated foreign investment screening mechanism, critical context for understanding the legal challenge.

> Rede Sustentabilidade's filing with Brazil's Supreme Court centres on a straightforward but profound argument: that existing Brazilian legislation is constitutionally inadequate when it comes to protecting the national interest in the exploitation of strategic mineral resources. The party's position holds that no transaction should proceed if it results in the direct or indirect transfer of significant economic control over strategic mineral assets to foreign entities.
>
> This framing is deliberate. By grounding the challenge in constitutional language rather than standard foreign investment or antitrust objections, the party is attempting to invoke a higher legal threshold. Brazil's Constitution contains provisions embedding the concept of national interest into the governance of mineral exploitation, and the argument is that these provisions create a structural obligation the legislature has not yet fulfilled through specific statute.
>
> What makes this legally significant is the absence in Brazil of a dedicated foreign investment screening body equivalent to those operating elsewhere:
>
> Country | Regulatory Mechanism | Critical Mineral Application
> Australia | Foreign Investment Review Board (FIRB) | Mandatory review for foreign acquisitions of critical mineral assets
> Canada | Investment Canada Act | National security review triggered for strategic resource transactions
> United States | Committee on Foreign Investment (CFIUS) | Authority to block foreign acquisition of US critical mineral producers
> Brazil | No equivalent body | Constitutional provisions invoked on an ad hoc basis via Supreme Court

## [M-A-005] Deal Financial Structure and US Government Involvement
**Source**: SRC-A-005
**Relevance**: Primary source -- official company press release providing definitive deal terms, US government financing details, and strategic rationale.

> USA Rare Earth, Inc. today announced a definitive agreement to acquire 100% of Serra Verde Group, owner of the Pela Ema rare earth mine and processing plant in Goias, Brazil. The transaction consideration consists of $300 million in cash and 126.849 million shares of newly issued USAR common stock, which at USAR's closing share price of $19.95 as of Friday, April 17, 2026, implies an equity value of c.$2.8 billion for Serra Verde. The acquisition is expected to close in the third quarter of 2026, subject to customary closing conditions and regulatory approvals.
>
> Serra Verde has secured a $565 million financing package from the U.S. International Development Finance Corporation ("DFC") to fully fund optimization and expansion initiatives through to positive cash flow. In addition, Serra Verde has secured a 15-year, 100% offtake agreement to supply a special purpose vehicle capitalized by various U.S. Government agencies, as well as private capital sources with all four of the magnetic rare earths that are required to make a permanent NdFeB magnet. This offtake includes guaranteed minimum floor prices for each of Nd, Pr, Dy and Tb.
>
> At Phase 1 nameplate capacity, expected to be achieved by the end of 2027, Serra Verde is projected to produce c.6,400 metric tons of TREO per year.
>
> Serra Verde production is expected to represent over 50% of total non-China HREE supply by 2027.
>
> Serra Verde is backed by leading institutional investors including Denham Capital, Vision Blue Resources and The Energy & Minerals Group.

## [M-A-006] Pela Ema Mine: Production and Strategic Significance
**Source**: SRC-A-004
**Relevance**: Detailed technical and geological information about the mine, its production profile, and why it is strategically exceptional.

> Located in Goias State in central Brazil, Pela Ema hosts an ionic clay rare earth deposit, a deposit type that carries disproportionate strategic significance relative to more commonly discussed hard-rock or carbonate-hosted rare earth formations.
>
> Serra Verde commenced commercial production at Pela Ema in 2024. Current annual output stands at approximately 100 metric tons of rare earth oxides. The operation is being actively upgraded, with production targeted to reach approximately 6,400 metric tons per year by the end of 2027, representing a 64-fold scaling of output over roughly three years.
>
> The deposit profiles at Pela Ema are geologically similar in character to the ionic clay formations found in China's Jiangxi Province, which have historically supplied the overwhelming majority of the world's heavy rare earth oxides.
>
> Heavy rare earth elements such as dysprosium, terbium, and yttrium are disproportionately concentrated in ionic clay deposits compared to other deposit types.

## [M-A-007] Brazil's Antitrust Investigation (CADE)
**Source**: SRC-A-006
**Relevance**: Second legal/regulatory challenge to the deal -- CADE antitrust investigation opened on May 12, 2026.

> Brazil's antitrust watchdog has opened an investigation into USA Rare Earth's proposed acquisition of its only rare earth producer, posing a regulatory hurdle to America's critical minerals plans in Latin America's largest market.
>
> On Monday, the Administrative Council for Economic Defense said it has initiated proceedings to review the transaction, including the offtake agreement. The agency, also known as Cade, is responsible for evaluating business combinations and finding potential competition issues.
>
> Cade will look to assess whether the USAR-Serra Verde combination constitutes a "concentration act" under Brazilian competition law. If so, it will determine whether the parties were required to notify the transaction or whether it should request a submission for antitrust analysis.
>
> The proceeding comes amid efforts by the US government to seal a critical minerals pact with Brazil, host to the second-largest volume of rare earth reserves in the world behind only China.
>
> Despite having been in talks as early as January, the parties have yet to finalize an agreement. So far, the US only has a memorandum of understanding at the regional level with the state of Goias, focused on investments in Serra Verde.

## [M-A-008] Brazilian Government Rejects State Miner "TerraBras"
**Source**: SRC-A-007
**Relevance**: Establishes the Brazilian government's official stance on critical minerals policy -- rejecting state intervention while the court challenge proceeds.

> Brazil's government ruled out creating a state-owned critical minerals company while a left-wing political party filed a Supreme Court challenge seeking to block the $2.8bn sale of rare earths producer Serra Verde Group to US-listed USA Rare Earth.
>
> Industry Minister Marcio Elias Rosa told local broadcaster CanalGov there was 'no need whatsoever to create a state-owned company to carry out the exploration or processing of critical minerals,' pushing back on proposals for a state-backed entity that has become known informally as 'TerraBras.'
>
> Finance Minister Dario Durigan echoed that position, arguing that strong global demand already makes Brazil attractive to investors without broad fiscal incentives.
>
> The government's stance comes as a proposed national regulatory framework for critical minerals remains stalled in Congress -- with 13 related bills under review, according to the National Mining Agency -- and as the cabinet presided by Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva missed its own deadline to deliver a comprehensive mining strategy.
>
> Hours after the ministerial statements, the Rede Sustentabilidade party filed a petition with Brazil's Supreme Court asking it to suspend the Serra Verde transaction on national security grounds.

## [M-A-010] Supreme Court Farmland Ruling: Critical Minerals Connection
**Source**: SRC-A-009
**Relevance**: Key contextual event -- the Supreme Court's 9-0 ruling three days after the deal announcement explicitly links farmland restrictions to critical minerals sovereignty.

> Brazil's Supreme Court voted 9-0 on April 23 to uphold a 1971 law that restricts foreigners and foreign-controlled companies from buying Brazilian farmland.
>
> Justice Alexandre de Moraes, who had initially voted against the restrictions, flipped his position and cited the need to protect "national sovereignty and critical minerals."
>
> The ruling lands three days after US-listed USA Rare Earth agreed to pay US$2.8 billion for the Pela Ema mine in Goias -- the largest rare-earths transaction in Latin American history.
>
> "Current geopolitics demonstrates the importance of preserving Brazil's internal and external security on the basis of the territorial question," Moraes wrote. He added that the restrictions were necessary "to safeguard national sovereignty and critical minerals" -- and he named lithium, rare earths, and nickel by category rather than abstractly.
>
> The 1971 law does not prohibit foreign ownership of Brazilian farmland. It caps it. Foreign individuals and foreign-controlled companies cannot own more than 25% of the rural area of any single Brazilian municipality, and no single foreign nationality can own more than 40% of that 25%.
>
> None of the justices mentioned Serra Verde by name. They did not need to. Moraes referenced critical minerals in the same sentence he restated the restrictions, and Brazil's only operating rare-earths mine had just been sold to a US-government-backed acquirer three days earlier.

## [M-A-011] Cross-Reference: TerraBras Rejection and Policy Vacuum
**Source**: SRC-A-004
**Relevance**: Explains the contradiction between the government rejecting state intervention and the court challenge seeking constitutional protection.

> The Supreme Court filing did not emerge in a policy vacuum. Just one day before Rede Sustentabilidade lodged its petition, Brazil's government formally rejected the concept of a state-run critical minerals entity proposed under the working title of "TerraBras."
>
> This creates a notable political contradiction. On one hand, Brazil's government has rejected the concept of state intervention in critical minerals management through a dedicated public entity. On the other hand, a party challenging the absence of adequate legislative protections is now seeking judicial intervention to achieve similar protective outcomes through constitutional means.
>
> Unlike Chile, which has pursued a hybrid model for lithium through state participation requirements, or Indonesia, which mandated domestic processing before export in the nickel sector, Brazil lacks a coherent intermediate framework between full state control and unrestricted foreign acquisition.
