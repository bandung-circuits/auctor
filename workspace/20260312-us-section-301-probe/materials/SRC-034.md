President Trump's Tariffs and the Separation of Powers at the Supreme Court - Stanford Law School

"The biggest separation-of-powers controversy since the steel seizure case in 1952."

In April, President Trump declared a national emergency and assumed the power to levy tariffs, introducing uncertainty into global trading by reneging on previously negotiated agreements. One of the attorneys representing the challengers to the president's decree in Trump v. VOS is Stanford Law Professor Michael McConnell, a constitutional law expert and former Tenth Circuit judge.

Transcript of Stanford Legal podcast discussion with Professors Michael McConnell, Pam Karlan, and Diego Zambrano:

Michael McConnell: Tariffs are taxes. Taxes on Americans. The tax bill, it goes to my clients. My clients pay the bill. Tariffs are not paid by foreign governments as President Trump sometimes says. Taxes on American citizens is something that is closely guarded by Congress. We actually fought the American Revolution largely on the slogan of no taxation without representation.

On IEEPA: On April 2nd, he issued an executive order, which declared an emergency under the National Emergencies Act. IEEPA is a successor statute to the Trading with the Enemy Act. None of IEEPA's authorities historically had anything to do with tariffs. They all had to do with things like embargoes, blockades, asset freezes.

On Constitutional Authority: The taxing power is vested in Congress. This is Article 1, Section 8, Clause 1. In the early years of the Republic, tariffs amounted to the vast majority of federal revenues, often more than 90%.

On Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974: Congress allowed the president to act in case of large and serious trade deficits, but limited to 15% tariff increase and only for 150 days. Most of Trump's tariffs exceed both limits.

On the Nixon Precedent: In 1971, Nixon imposed a 10% surcharge, temporary lasting less than five months. Congress ratified his decision but with a very narrow and specific statute. They then repealed the Trading with the Enemy Act except for declared war, and enacted IEEPA. By this time, they had already dealt with the tariff question so they don't even mention tariffs in IEEPA.

On the Major Questions Doctrine: Congress has to express itself clearly. If it isn't clear, what President Trump did can't possibly be sustained. The Supreme Court wielded this doctrine to strike down Biden administration actions (eviction moratorium, student loans, vaccination mandate, climate regulations).

On Greater-Includes-Lesser Argument: Taxation yields revenue, and that's a real temptation. Embargoes don't bring in money. Tariffs are taxes on Americans. The idea that regulation is "greater" and it includes the "lesser" is a category error.
